# Core Concept — The Long Siege

## One-sentence story
A young duchess inheriting a fracturing fifteenth-century dominion must marry the man she believes once publicly scorned her, turning a forced political alliance into the only thing that can save her lands — and herself.

## Premise
March 1484. Duke Hugues de Vauréal is killed and his army destroyed at the Battle of the Roches-Noires. His only surviving child, Jeanne, twenty-two, inherits one of the richest composite dominions between France and the Holy Roman Empire: cloth cities, river tolls, iron mines, banking houses, and restive vassals. The French regency declares her southern fiefs forfeit and marches. The merchant cities revolt for charters. Her vassals auction their loyalty between Paris and the Emperor. The only viable army belongs to Thiébaut, Duke of Lorraine — her father's former ward, the boy she grew up beside, the man who renounced her publicly seven years earlier and never explained. A marriage treaty is signed by councils on both sides while neither principal has a choice.

## Themes
- Sovereignty as intimacy: ruling and loving are both acts of deciding what to keep inside the walls and what to surrender.
- The cost of protected ignorance: people are destroyed by truths that were withheld "for their own good."
- Power shared is not power lost; power hoarded becomes a siege.
- Grief as administration: a state does not permit you to stop and mourn.
- The gap between public performance and private truth (court, marriage, faith, rebellion).
- Women's rule in a world that treats a female sovereign as an unclosed gate.

## Core conflict
External: France + rebellious cities + disloyal vassals vs. the Vauréal–Lorraine alliance.
Internal: Jeanne's need to remain inviolable sovereign of herself vs. the necessity of trusting a man whose knowledge of her weaknesses is total.
Sustained mystery: what actually happened at the rupture seven years ago.

## Emotional destination
Hard-won, earned partnership rather than rescue. Jeanne does not give up power to be loved; she extends it. The ending is hopeful but expensive — survival has invoices.

## Genre promise
Literary historical fiction for mature readers. Political realism in the Dorothy Dunnett / Sharon Kay Penman vein. Slow-burn marriage-of-convenience romance. No modern anachronistic framing. Explicit but purposeful sex and violence.

## Locked decisions
- Fictional polity (Vauréal) against a real historical frame (France of Charles VIII / Anne de France, Emperor Frederick III, Duchy of Lorraine).
- Close third person, past tense, Jeanne-anchored with selected Thiébaut chapters.
- The rupture secret is held by Chancellor Foulques and revealed in Chapter 18; no earlier POV may disclose it.
- The marriage happens in Chapter 7. The war is not single-campaign; span March 1484 to spring 1486.
- Jeanne remains the ruling duchess; the marriage is contractual and she never cedes sovereignty.
- Historical texture is pre-Reformation, pre-print-explosion: pikes, artillery in its youth, guild militias, plate armor, relics, Latin liturgy.

## Avoid list
- Modern idiom, modern psychology vocabulary, modern gender discourse in narrative voice.
- Romance-genre shorthand (alpha-male posturing, "you're mine" dialogue, love triangles engineered for jealousy).
- Info-dump historical lectures; exposition only through character need.
- Perfect battle outcomes, trivialized logistics, instant communication.
- Villains who know they are villains.
- Cliffhanger cheapness at every chapter end; prefer resolved-beat, changed-state endings.
