# Master Outline — The Long Siege (25 chapters, target ~5,000 words each)

## ACT I — SUCCESSION AND THE FORCED ALLIANCE (Ch. 1–8)

### Ch.1 — The Ledger of the Dead (Jeanne; Aubecourt; March 1484)
Routed survivors bring news of Roches-Noires. Hugues's ransomed corpse arrives at night; Jeanne receives it, locks her grief away, sits with the chancellor and treasurer before dawn. State of the treasury, the army, the borders. First dispatches: Sainterre guilds assembling, French royal letters declaring forfeit. Establish her self-control, her love of ledgers, the empty Haut-Tertre. End: she orders the mourning bells and the war muster in the same breath.

### Ch.2 — The Vultures' Congregation (Jeanne; Aubecourt)
Court of mourning. Envoys: Von Zollern (Empire), a French herald (forfeit summons), Dolfin (credit), guild deputation (charter demands as price of loyalty), Hautperthuis (solicitous, angling for regency powers). Jeanne asserts rule in open council, denies a regent, and learns the council has already opened Lorraine marriage talks. First mention of Thiébaut in seven years; her controlled reaction.

### Ch.3 — The Old Wound (Jeanne; Aubecourt)
Flashbacks woven through present: childhood with the ward Thiébaut — lessons, escapes, the eyebrow scar, her father's coldness. The rupture as Jeanne remembers it: the storm, the night in the solar (chaste, fervent), then the public renunciation before the court, his words, his back as he rode out. Present: Brive and Foulques argue strategy; no army, no money without a marriage. Two suits: imperial prince (empty) vs Lorraine (armies). She chooses Lorraine because it is the only choice that keeps her sovereign.

### Ch.4 — Nancy's Bargain (Thiébaut POV; Nancy)
Lorraine under strain: empty granaries, unpaid garrisons, French-backed claimants. Thiébaut's council presses the Vauréal marriage. Show his competence, his poverty, his guilt; memory of the rupture from his side — the dread and the choice, but NOT the threats/secret. Jean and the Abbé de Viray. He signs the treaty his council drafted, then decides to ride to Aubecourt himself rather than send a proxy.

### Ch.5 — The Road Between Armies (split; the road)
Thiébaut's small column through disordered country; skirmish with French-backed marauders near Harchies; first blood, shows his craft. Arrival at Aubecourt. The first meeting in seven years: public ceremony, every word formal, private shock. They are assigned adjacent chambers. Night: each alone behind a shared wall.

### Ch.6 — The Treaty of Everyone But Them (Jeanne; Aubecourt)
Negotiations over the marriage contract: dower, revenues, troop quotas, her retained sovereignty, separate councils. Long verbal duet — fencing, testing; he yields surprising guarantees of her authority. She cannot tell if it is strategy or knowledge of her. Signed by councils; engagement proclaimed. News arrives of Sainterre's open revolt and the fall of a southern bridge-fort to the French.

### Ch.7 — The Wedding at Haut-Tertre (Jeanne; Aubecourt)
Wedding mass, spectacle as statecraft, vassals watching for weakness. Feast, bedding ceremony, the public stripping of privacy. Wedding night alone: two people who know each other's childhood and nothing of each other now. Awkward, tender, explicit but character-driven; neither pretends love. Afterwards they lie awake negotiating the dark.

### Ch.8 — Two Heads, One Pillow (Jeanne; Aubecourt)
First weeks of marriage: bed politics, council politics, servants' politics. Clash over military command and over the charter question; first real compromise — a joint framework offering limited charters in exchange for war taxes. News: Mont d'Or under threat. Act I closes with the two of them riding north at the head of a combined army, still strangers, already a government.

## ACT II — WAR, REVOLT, AND THE SLOW THAW (Ch. 9–17)

### Ch.9 — The Charter and the Cannon (Jeanne; Sainterre)
Entry into restive Sainterre. Guild politics: Coppens the negotiator, de Key the demagogue, the printer's shop, Dolfin's fondaco. Standoff, then de Key's riot; blood on the cloth hall steps; Thiébaut's soldiers restrain while Jeanne negotiates. A charter is granted — sealed under duress, a precedent she consciously accepts. First taste of partnership: he holds the line while she writes the terms.

### Ch.10 — South to the Frontier (Jeanne; march and camp)
The combined army marches for Mont d'Or. Camp life, logistics, mercenaries, Thiébaut teaching her artillery and the mathematics of gunnery. Night conversation by a siege fire: fragments of the seven missing years, no rupture talk. French probe at dawn; repelled; she sees him command and he sees she does not flinch.

### Ch.11 — The Weight of Bodies (Jeanne; border town assault)
The storming of French-held Pont-d'Ortelle region town; graphic, confused, costly. Jeanne in the rear with surgeons and refugees; a boy from her own household guard dies in her arms. The town taken, sacking narrowly prevented by Thiébaut's discipline. Aftermath: grief, mud, mortality; the second sex scene — desperate, binding, wordless at first. She begins to know him physically and emotionally.

### Ch.12 — The Banker's Ledger (Jeanne; Aubecourt, winter 1484–85)
War of money: Dolfin's loans, taxation battles, vassal resentment. Hautperthuis's first quiet treachery (missing muster, suspicious leases). Claire de Sombernon arrives as a refugee from overrun Lorraine lands; Jeanne's jealousy at court, a feast where the three of them perform. Confrontation between spouses at night; he tells the truth of Claire without being asked; first voluntary trust. She does not ask about the rupture.

### Ch.13 — The Preacher's Fire (Jeanne; Grisay)
Iconoclastic frenzy under Brother Anselm; forges idle, mob justice, a cleric killed. Jeanne enters the city with a small guard; sermon confrontation; Lisette nearly killed in the crowd. Mix of force and law: Anselm arrested for the cleric's death by city statute, not for belief; de Key flees to French lines. Theology and governance debate; Jeanne's faith is practical, not mystical.

### Ch.14 — Letters from Paris (Jeanne; Aubecourt)
Captured French dispatches reveal a fifth column and Graçay's spring plan; the Regent's silky terms arrive by herald. Rostaing's massacre of a village that sheltered French raiders comes to light — women and children. Jeanne must judge a popular hero of the army: she breaks him in public ceremony, commutes death to loss of rank and frontier penury. Law over morale; Brive troubled, Thiébaut quietly supports her. Foreshadow Foulques's guilt: he flinches at her words about protected girls.

### Ch.15 — The Frost March (Thiébaut POV; Harchies Forest, Jan–Feb 1485)
Winter war: Thiébaut leads a desperate march to relieve a Lorraine fort; ambush in the snow; Jean badly wounded taking a pike meant for his brother. Jeanne appears with a paid convoy and Grisay gunners through forest roads the French think impassable — her plan, her risk. Near-death night in a charcoal burner's hut; he nearly tells her the rupture truth; the chapter ends before he can.

### Ch.16 — The Hunting Lodge (Jeanne; spring 1485)
Rare quiet: a repaired hunting lodge, hawking, one whole day with no dispatches. They speak of childhood, of fathers, of what rule costs; laughter for the first time. Sensual, hopeful chapter; she almost asks the rupture question and chooses not to — she wants the present to hold. Dolfin renews credit against the Sainterre customs; Erquelines's imperial flirtation becomes open. Return: word that Hautperthuis has seized Mont d'Or.

### Ch.17 — Treason's Market (Jeanne; the south)
Hautperthuis declares for France, hands French troops the fortress gate. Marcenay's free company sacks a border town; atrocity rendered through survivors, not spectacle. Jeanne's maid-intelligence network (Lisette) maps the fifth column. Council split: Thiébaut and Brive want reprisal by fire; Jeanne insists on attainder by law and the protection of tenant rights. Foulques, aging and ill, begins to crack. Act II ends with Jeanne kneeling alone in the palace chapel, then rising to summon the chancellor.

## ACT III — THE SIEGE, THE TRUTH, AND THE PRICE OF PEACE (Ch. 18–25)

### Ch.18 — What the Chancellor Carried (Jeanne; Aubecourt)
Foulques, failing of his heart, confesses the full rupture story and surrenders the signed renunciation with Hugues's marginal threats. Jeanne's reconstruction of her own memory; the long confrontation with Thiébaut, who confirms every word and adds his seven years of silence. Not absolution: she sends him away, spends the night with the document and her dead father's shadow. Dawn: she does not forgive fully, but she chooses him — and chooses to tell him so in her own time. Foulques resigns the seal; she refuses to accept it, assigning him penance through service.

### Ch.19 — The Siege of Sainterre (Jeanne inside / Thiébaut outside; summer 1485)
Graçay's grand offensive: Sainterre invested. Jeanne, in the city for a charter council, chooses to stay rather than flee; Thiébaut keeps the field army outside. Parallel pressures: bombardment, rationing, de Key returned as French collaborator preaching surrender, flux in the wells. Coppens and the guilds rise to the defense. Thiébaut's raids and letters (arrow-shot over walls). First siege assault repelled at hideous cost.

### Ch.20 — The Sallies (Jeanne; inside Sainterre)
The siege's heart: Jeanne runs the civilian defense — rations, hospitals, fire-watches, the women's water-and-sand crews. Marcenay breaches the outer ward and threatens the captured women's quarter; Jeanne arms the townswomen and leads the counter at the breach (command, not sword-heroics). She realizes she is pregnant. A letter to Thiébaut carries the news and the siege's last casualty lists. The gunpowder runs low.

### Ch.21 — The Breaking of the Siege (split)
Thiébaut's relief plan: Brive's feint, imperial-paid Landsknechts, a night march, Coppens opening the water gate. Coordinated battle inside and outside the lines; de Key hanged by his own weavers; Marcenay killed by the disgraced Rostaing, redeeming himself at the cost of his life. Graçay conducts a professional retreat. Reunion in the ruined cloth hall: no grand speech, two people holding on. Jeanne tells him of the child.

### Ch.22 — The Price of Crowns (Jeanne; Sainterre–Aubecourt)
Aftermath: ruins, debt, famine prices. Dolfin's renegotiation; the charter re-granted and enlarged in exchange for permanent war taxes — the nobility rage, the cities bind themselves to her. Erquelines's homage bought with a toll monopoly. Thiébaut and Jeanne now visibly joint sovereigns; her pregnancy shows. Hautperthuis still holds Mont d'Or; winter siege planned.

### Ch.23 — The Fortress at Mont d'Or (split; winter 1485–86)
Trenches, mines, snow, artillery duels; an inside attempt on Thiébaut's life by a bribed groom (the fifth column's last throw), foiled by Perrin. Jeanne directs the battery in person when the master gunner falls. Assault through the mine breach; Thiébaut's old shoulder wound reopens; Hautperthuis taken alive in the donjon. She looks at the kinsman who dandled her as a child and orders him held for law, not vengeance.

### Ch.24 — The King's Peace (Jeanne; Heurtois)
Peace conference in the prince-bishopric. Graçay across the table; the Regent's letters still silky. France restores the occupied towns in exchange for a compromise homage on the southern fiefs and a marriage-promise dowry of cash; Empire suit fended with politeness. Then Hautperthuis's public trial for treason: Jeanne pronounces the beheading sentence on her own kin and attends it, veiled. Hardest scene of the book; law as grief.

### Ch.25 — The Peace (Jeanne; Aubecourt; spring 1486)
Rebuilding: the sealed Sainterre charter, the first elected aldermen, the new battery at Mont d'Or, ledgers moving from war to roofs and roads. The child is born — a daughter, named for Jeanne's mother. Thiébaut present throughout. A final private reckoning with her father's tomb and with the old rupture document, which she burns. Closing return to the Ch.1 motif: the night ledger, but now a second hand reaches for the other quill. End on the peacetime siege — chosen, shared, no longer alone.
