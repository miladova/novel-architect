# Chapter Sixteen — The Hunting Lodge

The spring of 1485 came in like a relief column. The ice went out of the Ortelle with a sound like distant guns, the roads dried from mud into ruts and then into dust, the rooks returned to the Haut-Tertre elms, and the war, which had spent the winter indoors among the ledgers, put its armor back on.

The news in the first warm weeks was mostly good, which made the court nervous. The Harchies relief had done the work Jeanne had designed it for: the story of the Vauréal guns coming through the snow to save a Lorraine fort was told in every market from Sainterre to Nancy, and recruits were coming into the musters faster than the treasury could equip them. Graçay's army had gone into quarters around the forfeit fiefs without a single new conquest to show for the season's expense, and the Regent's letters to her own war secretary, bought at considerable price through Dolfin's friends in Paris, had taken on the thin tone of a woman questioning her invoices. At the Heurtois conference the duchess's envoys were being endlessly reasonable, and the French negotiators were being endlessly polite, and every week of it cost Paris money and cost Vauréal only patience.

"We have one season of true advantage," Thiébaut told the war council, with the maps spread and the windows open onto the April rain. "Graçay needs a victory. His treasury needs a victory more. When the roads harden he'll move, and he'll move at the thing that will make the loudest story, which means Mont d'Or. We spend the spring making Mont d'Or expensive and keeping the field army whole."

"Valvert has nine hundred men now," Brive said. "The new battery's mounted. He says he can hold against six weeks' open trenches."

"Then six weeks is what we'll make sure Graçay doesn't get." Thiébaut's finger traced the river roads. "The army camps within four days' march, not four hours. Close enough to relieve, far enough not to be pinned. The war is a clock; we remain the slower clock."

Jeanne let the military talk run and attended to her own side of it, the side that would decide whether any of it could be paid for: the subsidy receipts coming in better than the winter's forecasts, the Sainterre customs rising with the reopened trade, the new weekly news sheet carrying the winter's victories into every guildhall, and the quiet, unglamorous work of keeping the vassals uncertain enough to be loyal.

It was in the middle of this spring accounting that Oriabel produced the hunting lodge.

"The lodge at Vertbois," she said, over the ledgers, as though it were a tax receipt. "It's been repaired, against the day the court wanted it. The roof is new, the glass is new, the keeper has been stocking the river with trout. My cousin, you have not spent one day outside the sound of bells since your father died. Neither, for that matter, has your husband. The court can survive a single day without either of you. The household will, in fact, be delighted, because nervous rulers make nervous servants."

"The dispatches—"

"Will be held at the gate until morning. I have spoken to Foulques. One day. Hawks, horses, a supper without a clerk at the table. If the French besiege Paris between dawn and dusk, I will personally bring you the letter."

Jeanne looked at Thiébaut, who had come in at the end of this. "Can the field army spare you for a day?"

"The field army has survived worse than a day without my conversation." His face was perfectly grave. "Also I have not seen you properly upright on a horse since the snow, and I am told the hawking is excellent."

So it was arranged, in the teeth of the protocol officers, that the duke and duchess would ride to Vertbois with four companions, two grooms, and the hawks, and no clerks at all.

* * *

The morning they left was the first truly warm morning of the year, the kind that turns the woods green overnight and makes the horses dance with their own foolishness. They rode west through the forest roads under a canopy of new leaves, the party small and quick, Jean still favoring his healed shoulder but refusing to be left behind, Claire and Oriabel ahead with the falconer, and Jeanne found, with something like surprise, that the weight between her shoulders was actually lifting, the way a pack does when the road finally runs downhill.

Vertbois sat in a clearing above a bend of a small river, an old stone lodge her grandfather had built, with a steep roof and a terrace and the new glass glittering in its narrow windows. The keeper's family had it open and aired, with woodsmoke and the smell of bread, and the hawks were given their block and the horses their oats, and for the first time in a year Jeanne stood in a room that contained no maps.

They hawked the river meadow after the midday bread. The falconer flew the peregrine at duck, and the bird climbed into the blue like a thrown soul and stooped out of the sun, and the whole party shouted at the strike in the unguarded way of people released from court, and Jeanne laughed aloud, a real laugh, and heard it with the mild shock of a person meeting a younger version of herself.

"You're good at this," Thiébaut said, cantering alongside as she reined in.

"I was raised for this. All the useless accomplishments. Latin, accounts, and the management of birds of prey. My father said a ruler who understands a hawk understands a vassal: keep it hooded, fly it often, reward it exactly."

"A cheerful doctrine."

"It was a cheerful childhood, in the manner of fortified places."

They left the others at the river and took the woodland path slowly, two horses side by side in the dappled light, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the silence was the new kind, the kind that had to be built and had been. It was he who broke it first.

"Vertbois. I remember it. Your grandfather's great hunt, the year I was fourteen. You got lost in this wood and were found asleep under a fallen oak, entirely unconcerned, and your father had the whole forest beaten for two hours and then beat the gamekeeper instead."

"He beat the gamekeeper because he couldn't beat me without scandal. I remember the oak. I remember you were the one who found me, actually. You followed the dog."

"The dog followed the bread trail you'd been leaving. You'd stolen a whole loaf from the breakfast table. Even lost, you were provisioning."

She laughed again, and he looked at her with an expression she was learning to recognize, the expression of a man watching something he had thought dead show signs of life.

They found the fallen oak, or one like it, and dismounted in a glade where the bluebells were coming up, and sat with their backs against the bark, and the horses cropped the new grass, and the wood went on with its own business around them.

"I want to ask you something," she said, "and you may tell me it's none of my affair."

"Ask."

"Did you ever want it? Lorraine, I mean. As a boy. Before the treaties and the wardship. Did you want to be the duke, or did it happen to you the way it happened to me?"

He considered it, turning a piece of bark in his fingers. "It happened to me. I wanted to be the master of the horse, I think. Someone whose whole office was horses. I was seven. Then my father died and the regents stole everything that wasn't nailed down, and by the time I came home from your father's court the wanting had been replaced. There's a point where a duchy stops being an inheritance and becomes a patient. You don't ask whether you want it. You ask whether it will live through the night."

"That's exactly it." She plucked a bluebell and turned it in her fingers. "I wanted to be a clerk. A woman clerk, which the Church wouldn't have, so really I wanted to be left alone with books. Now I keep a country instead. Sometimes I think the people who want power are the last who should have it, and the people who don't are the ones who can't escape it."

"Your father wanted it. Every room, every hour. And it ate him."

"It ate my mother first. She died buying him the spare heir, and he loved her, I think, in his fashion, and went to war six weeks later because grief was the one account he didn't know how to keep." The bluebell went round and round. "I've been afraid, since the wedding, that I would turn into him. That the ledgers would eat everything and there'd be nothing left but the high chair."

"You won't."

"How can you be certain?"

"Because you keep the book of the dead." He said it simply, without rhetoric. "He kept a book of victories. You keep a book of names. That's the whole difference, and it's a difference of soul, not policy. A ruler who counts the cost in people will never be the ruler who counts only the cost in ground."

The wood was very quiet. Somewhere a woodpecker worked at a trunk, methodical and calm. She did not look at him, because if she looked at him she would do something unduchesslike in a spring wood, and instead she said, to the bluebell, "When we open the green case, Thiébaut, whatever is in it — I need you to know I've already decided one thing. The marriage is not on trial. The alliance is not on trial. Whatever your seven years were, they don't get to undo the year we've had. I don't know what I'll feel. I know what I'll do."

He was quiet long enough that she turned at last, and his face was the face of a man who has been handed a reprieve he had stopped praying for. "I would have built my whole life," he said, slowly, "around deserving that sentence. You've just given it to me before I've earned it."

"Then earn it after. We have a war."

* * *

They stayed the night, because the day had been given to them and it seemed foolish to spend it on the road, and the keeper's family moved into the forester's cottage and left the lodge to the court. The others took the lower rooms; Jeanne and Thiébaut had the upper chamber under the steep new roof, with the river sound below the windows and no dispatch box anywhere in the room, an absence so strange that she stood looking at the empty table for a moment.

The supper was trout from the river and early asparagus and a cold fowl, eaten without ceremony at the kitchen table, and Jean told his worst forest stories and Claire sang an old Lorraine ballad in a clear small voice, and for a few hours the war existed only as a thing outside the lamplight. Afterwards, on the terrace, the others drifting in one by one, they were left alone with the stars coming out over the black mass of the woods, and the first nightingale.

"No one can see us," she said. "No one is watching for the story."

"I'm always watching for the story. It's a professional deformity." He was leaning on the stone beside her, close enough that their sleeves touched. "But I'll try to be merely a man, if you'll try to be merely a woman. For one evening."

"Merely." She said the word with distaste, and smiled. "It's the hardest thing we've attempted."

He turned to her then, and took her hand the way he had taken it in the smoky hut, and she let him, and after a while he said, "Jeanne. I would like to kiss you without an audience of four hundred people. May I?"

It was the asking, again; the asking that undid her more reliably than any force. She put her other hand to his face, the weathered cheek and the small scar at the jaw, and said, "You may. You may also stop asking permission for things I have never once refused you," and he laughed, low, and kissed her under the new leaves with the nightingale going and the river doing the talking below.

The chamber upstairs had none of the great bed's state, only a wide wooden bed with clean linen and a window open to the spring night, and what happened there had nothing of the wedding night's careful instruction and nothing of the battle night's desperation. It was slow, and easy in the way of people who had learned each other's weather, the shoulder protected without being mentioned, the cold hands warmed, the laughter once at a knot in the lacing and once at nothing at all. Afterwards she lay with her head on his good arm and listened to the river, and he said into the dark, "If the duchy fell tomorrow, I could live in this lodge."

"No, you couldn't. You'd be rebuilding the forester's cottage within a week and issuing him regulations."

"That's true. I'm afraid of being happy, it turns out. I keep expecting an invoice."

"Expect it in the morning. The invoices always come in the morning." She pressed closer. "Tonight we're bankrupt and content."

They slept, astonishingly, until full daylight, and woke to the keeper's wife knocking with hot water and the news that no dispatch had arrived, which was the best news of the year.

* * *

The morning of the return had the particular sweetness of all last mornings. Mist lay over the river and the woods were full of birdsong, and they ate their bread and cheese on the terrace with the hawks hooded on their blocks and the horses saddled below, and nobody mentioned the word _dispatch_.

Jean, pale and thin from the wound but armed with opinions, had spent the previous evening losing at cards to Claire and spent breakfast re-litigating every hand. "The queen of spades was the issue. A woman with that expression does not appear in a hand by accident."

"She appears," Claire said, buttering bread, "to men who waste their trumps. Your brother plays correctly and says nothing, which is why he wins and you don't."

"Because he has no conversation. I have conversation."

"You have monologues. Conversation requires a second person."

Oriabel caught Jeanne's eye across the table, and the two women smiled the private smile of people watching a court arrange itself into something resembling a family, and Jeanne thought: this is what the treaties never buy. This is what a year has bought, if anything has. The easy table, the wounded brother, the widow who was once the mistress and is now the friend, the cousin who runs the baggage like a duchy. She had inherited a state and a siege; she seemed, without quite noticing, to have also inherited a household.

Thiébaut walked the terrace with her after the meal, along the stone above the river, where no one could overhear. "A year ago this week, the gate bell woke you."

"You remember the date."

"I remember everything about that week. The rain, the number of your escort, the grey mare, the curtain that fell a heartbeat too late." He leaned on the parapet. "Jeanne. If the green case holds what I think it holds, and if after it you find you cannot bear the sight of me, I want the treaty to hold anyway. The duchy comes first. I'll sign whatever distance you require. I needed to say it before the spring, so you know it's policy and not panic."

She looked at him for a long moment, at the grey at his temple and the lines around the eyes that had not been there at twenty. "What if I find I can bear it? What then?"

"Then I will have been wrong about being unworthy, and I will need new plans. I have none ready."

"Make some." She turned toward the horses, where the grooms were waiting. "On the road. You have a year of my accounts to catch up on, and I expect a thorough job."

* * *

The invoices, as promised, came home with them.

Dolfin requested an audience within twenty-four hours, and this time he came without a clerk, carrying the news in his head rather than a chest. The spring campaigns of every court in Europe were being financed in the same handful of counting houses, and the Venetian's information was therefore better than the spies'.

"Graçay's spring pay is short by a third," he said, settling into the treasury office. "His Swiss contingent has been sent home unpaid, which is a story the cantons will remember. The Regent's new loan in Paris is being subscribed slowly, because the merchants have noticed her war has no victories. My house, by contrast, is prepared to renew your full credit against the Sainterre customs, now that the customs have performed for two quarters. I mention this not as generosity but as evidence: Venice believes you are going to win. It is the only kind of belief I deal in."

"The rate?"

"Eleven. Down from twelve. The collateral is better and the borrower is punctual; punctuality is the most expensive virtue, which is to say the best rewarded." He permitted himself a thin smile. "I would also, madame, advise the timing of your next negotiation with Paris. A borrower whose credit is falling while yours rises should be met at the moment she has just paid an army to do nothing. The Heurtois talks will smell different by midsummer."

"You make policy sound like the Rialto."

"All policy is the Rialto with better clothes."

The same post brought a letter from Coppens, and she opened it after the banker had gone, because the old guildsman wrote as he spoke, at length and without flattery. The charter was holding. The elected aldermen had survived their first winter and were now arguing among themselves with the ferocity of free men, which he considered the highest possible compliment. The militia drilled every Sunday after church under officers holding the duchess's commission, and the two-key powder store had become a kind of civic temple, shown to visiting merchants. The weekly printed sheet, Verhoeven's news, was being copied as far as Bruges and Cologne, and Sainterre cloth was carrying the duchess's badge to markets that had never heard of the Roches-Noires. He ended with the bill for the city's spring war contribution, paid in full and early, and a single line: _The weavers who used to shout de Key's name now shout at the aldermen about drains. I count this a victory._

"Read that," she said to Thiébaut, passing it across the table.

He read, and the not-smile crossed his face. "You built a country in a year. One city at a time, one charter at a time. Paris still thinks countries are built with marriages."

"Marriages helped."

"Marriages were the down payment. The rest is drains."

She wrote Coppens a reply in her own hand that evening, no clerks, confirming the toll review the city had asked for and adding a postscript about the drains, which she said sounded to her like the proper music of a well-governed city. It was the kind of letter no sovereign wrote who had not begun, somewhere in the winter, to think of the towns as partners rather than property, and she sealed it without quite recognizing the change in herself.

The second invoice arrived from the east, in the form of Othon d'Erquelines himself, who had not previously deigned to visit the court in person. He came with a large train and a letter from the Emperor's chancellery, and he was received with the full ceremony due to a man whose family had held the marches for two hundred years, and in the round chamber he proceeded to demonstrate why two hundred years of ancestry did not buy two hundred minutes of sense.

The Emperor, he said, viewed with growing alarm the Vauréal alignment with a Lorraine house that had, in the imperial view, become too clever by half. An alternative remained: an imperial alliance in earnest, with an archducal marriage (the cousin again, now with a name and a small estate), imperial garrisons at the eastern forts, and the immediate withdrawal of the French forfeit claims under the Emperor's guarantee. In exchange, Erquelines himself would be named the duchess's lieutenant in the eastern marches.

"Let me be certain I understand." Jeanne kept her voice pleasant. "The Emperor, who has sent no troops and no money and whose promises I could paper a privy with, now offers me garrisons I don't want and a suitor I've refused, and asks in exchange for the eastern marches under your lieutenancy. Have I missed a clause?"

"The guarantee, madame—"

"The guarantee of a man who cannot keep his own Swiss paid. Count Othon. I will answer in writing, as is proper. But I will give you the shape of it now. The marches are held by officers who hold my commission, and the eastern forts are commanded by my garrisons, and neither will change. You may keep your Emperor's friendship, which I value, and your own homage, which I require. The door is open on the way out."

When he had gone, purple and stammering, Thiébaut, who had sat silent through the audience, said, "He'll go to Paris now."

"No. He'll go home and wait to see who wins. That's his genius; he never commits until the invoices are settled. But he'll make us pay for the marches when we need them. Note it." Foulques noted it. "What else is in the post? I've had a day in the country and I intend to catch up on every disaster in Christendom."

The third dispatch came at dusk, and it was the disaster that mattered.

It was from Mont d'Or, but not from Valvert. The writing belonged to the garrison's second-in-command, a young officer named Sancerre, and it was short because it had been written in hiding and smuggled out inside a cooper's barrel: Hautperthuis had visited the fortress two days before, on a courtesy inspection with a train of sixty men, and at the changing of the watch his people had seized the south gate. Valvert was dead, killed in the first minute at the gatehouse, and the French vanguard was already marching through the pass under Gui's banner. The fortress had fallen without a siege, to a smile, a handshake, and sixty cutthroats. The rock that was to have cost Graçay six weeks had been given to him in a night.

Jeanne read it aloud to the council in the silence that followed, set the paper down, and looked at the map she knew by heart, at the black mark of Mont d'Or on the kingdom road.

"The clock just changed," Thiébaut said quietly.

"Yes." She folded her hands to keep them steady. "Then we change with it. Marshal Brive, the host. Full muster, three days. Foulques, the attainder papers, every lease and treason in the chancellery, drawn by the week's end. Dolfin gets a message tonight; tell him the iron tithe may be needed sooner than we thought. And send to Sainterre. The war we planned in the spring is over. The war for Sainterre begins."
