# Chapter Two — The Vultures' Congregation

The rule of a dead duke's daughter, Jeanne discovered in her second week, consisted largely of sitting down. She sat in the round chamber for councils, in the hall for audiences, in the chapel for offices she did not have time to attend to properly, and at the long table in her father's chamber for the ledgers she kept instead of sleeping. Her knees ached. Her back ached. The ring of the duchy, too large for her finger on a chain at her belt, knocked against the table edge when she wrote and left a little bruise above her hip.

On the first day of April, when the snow had gone and the Ortelle was running high and brown with meltwater, she sat in the round chamber to receive the vultures in their proper order, which Foulques had arranged on a single sheet of paper according to the rank of their masters and the degree of danger they represented, these being two different sums.

"The French first," the chancellor said, arranging the paper precisely under her hand. "Insolence should be received early, while your patience is fresh. The imperial after, to show him he is not first. The Venetian third, since he will care more about the quality of the wine than the order of his audience. The guild deputation last and separately, in the lower hall, never at this table. Do not let the merchants grow accustomed to this room."

"And Hautperthuis?"

"Gui is not an envoy, my lady. He asks for a private audience, and he shall have one, in the solar, at the end of the day, when witnesses can be arranged without seeming to be. I have taken the liberty of inviting Dame Oriabel to be choosing linens in the adjoining chamber."

Jeanne looked at him. Foulques's face was the face he kept for documents: smooth, noncommittal, faintly tired. She had known him all her life. He had confirmed her, corrected her Latin, and on one never-spoken-of morning seven years ago had appeared in the solar at dawn with four guards and a face like a shut door. She trusted his mind and had long since stopped asking after his heart, which she assumed he kept in a strongbox somewhere with the older treaties.

"Very well," she said. "Send in the Frenchman."

* * *

The herald's name was de Pleine-Selve, and he came with a clerk and two men-at-arms and an expression calibrated to suggest that everyone present was already, in some technical sense, a rebel. He wore the royal arms on a tabard so new the silk still creaked, and he carried the Regent's letter in a velvet case as though it were a relic.

He read the letter aloud, which was an insult in itself, since everyone in the chamber could read. It was a handsome document. It regretted the death of "our well-beloved cousin the late duke," noted the ancient tenure by which the three southern fiefs were held of the French crown, rehearsed at length the failures of homage and the "treasonable correspondence maintained with the Emperor's ministers," and concluded by declaring the said fiefs forfeit and absorbed, their inhabitants released from their allegiance, their new mistress summoned to Paris to make what submission she might before the king's grace was withdrawn.

When the reading was done, the room held the particular silence of men waiting to see whether a twenty-two-year-old woman would weep, rage, or faint.

Jeanne held out her hand. "The letter, if you please. I keep originals."

De Pleine-Selve hesitated, then gave it up. She read it herself, slowly, turning the pages with the finger she did not wet, while the silence aged. Foulques had taught her the uses of silence at thirteen: it is a tax on the other party's nerve, and most people overpay.

"The tenure argument," she said at last, "is the work of a clever clerk with a weak case. The homage for the southern fiefs was last performed by my grandfather and remitted by crown charter in 'fifty-nine for two generations, a document registered at the Parlement of Paris. My father's homage fell due last Michaelmas. He was in the field against rebels armed from French arsenals, a fact your own masters will not enjoy seeing argued in open court. There is therefore no failure that is not first their own breach."

She laid the pages flat.

"As for correspondence with the Emperor, every lord in Christendom corresponds with every other lord in Christendom; the crime exists, if it exists, only in the choosing of recipients, and the choosing of recipients is policy, not treason. You may tell Madame la Régente that her letter is received, that its law is bad, and that its Latin is good, and that I will answer it in writing through my chancellor within the month. You may also tell her that if the Sire de Graçay crosses the fords before that answer reaches Paris, she will find the cost of the fiefs considerably higher than their value. Good day, herald."

De Pleine-Selve opened his mouth, found nothing in it that improved on her arrangement of the facts, bowed with the slight stiffness of a man revising his estimate of the merchandise, and went out. Brive exhaled through his nose like a warhorse.

"Your father," he said, "would have thrown him into the garderobe tower."

"My father had an army," said Jeanne. "I have a month. Next."

* * *

The imperial envoy came in smelling of rosewater and walked as though the floor were a diplomatic floor and might give way. Herr von Zollern was a Swabian knight of forty with a scholar's brow and an empty purse, which last Jeanne knew because Dolfin's factors financed half the Empire's embassies and kept no secrets that earned interest. He brought greetings from the Most Invincible Emperor Frederick, guardian of the peace of Christendom, defender of the churches, and a quantity of other titles that took the better part of a minute to unroll.

The offer, when it came, was silk over air. The Emperor, grieving to see a noble lady exposed to the predations of the French, was prepared to extend his protection: an imperial garrison for the northern towns, an imperial commissioner at the duchess's council, the payment, eventually, of troops, and in the fullness of time a suitable marriage with an illustrious prince of the Empire, whose name would be supplied in a later dispatch.

"Garrisoned," Jeanne repeated. "By which I understand the towns would receive imperial soldiers at their own charge, under imperial captains, while the troops remain unpaid until the fullness of time."

"Madame, the imperial liberality—"

"Is proverbial. The towns would also, I think, be required to take the imperial eagle on their gates, and the commissioner at my council would hold a veto in the Emperor's name, and the illustrious prince, whoever he proves to be, would administer my inheritance as his own. I have seen what imperial protection did for the towns of the Upper Rhine. Two of them are still paying for it."

Von Zollern's smile did not leave his face, which was how she knew it had never been a smile. "The alternative, madame, is France. The eagle does not eat what it shelters. The lily does."

"The eagle," said Jeanne, "also does not arrive when it is called. I will consider your master's friendship, which I value, and I will write to him in terms of the warmest respect. I cannot receive garrisons, commissioners, or princes, illustrious or otherwise, while my father is scarcely cold. You understand the proprieties."

He understood them perfectly; the proprieties were the only thing anyone in his trade ever understood. He bowed, perfumed the air a final time, and departed. Brive, who had been counting on his fingers, said, "That is two proposals of marriage in one morning, and the day is young."

"One proposal," said Jeanne. "The French one came with a forfeit. Next."

* * *

Zaccaria Dolfin came without a clerk, which was the first mark of the man. He was forty-eight, lean and dark and beautifully dressed in the Venetian manner, a single pearl at his ear and a ring on every finger that was doing work, not decoration. He smelled of eastern spices and clean linen. He bowed like a man who had bowed to doges and sultans and found them much of a muchness, and he accepted wine with the air of a man preparing to grade it.

"Duchess," he said, in French with only the ghost of an accent, "I will not offer you condolences. Condolence is a coin struck for people who cannot be of use. Permit me instead to offer arithmetic."

From his sleeve he produced a folded sheet, covered not in the flourishes of a court clerk but in the tight, mercantile hand of the Rialto. It was her duchy's financial condition, estimated, as far as she could see in two glances, to within five hundred écus of Colard's secret ledgers.

"Your ready cash, eleven thousand. Your Easter obligation to my house, three thousand. Your surviving garrisons, four months from mutiny. A new field army, twelve thousand the month before it fights at all. The French crown's cost for the Roches-Noires army, paid through the Lombards of Paris, roughly ninety thousand, of which perhaps sixty remains in the field. The Emperor's promises, priced at what they have fetched on the market these ten years, nothing at all." He folded the sheet away. "I state these things because flattery between borrowers and lenders is a tax on both."

Jeanne drank her own wine, which he had not yet graded, and watched him over the cup. "Your terms, Messer Dolfin. You did not ride two days through a war to admire my accounts."

"A loan. Forty thousand écus, in gold, in three installments, the first within the week. Security: the customs of Malemort and the Sainterre cloth duty for six years, administered by a joint board on which my house holds one seat of three. Interest at fourteen percent, which is generous given the cannon currently pointed at the collateral. A further clause: should the duchy's government change by conquest, my house retains its liens against the revenues regardless of the government. The French will honor that clause, Duchess. They have no choice; they borrow as I do."

"Fourteen percent is usury."

"It is the market. The Church will call it a service fee, for which I will pay the appropriate dispensations. Your own grandfather paid sixteen."

"He complained of it to his deathbed."

"A deathbed is the one place a Venetian banker is popular. Men forgive us their own debts there." Dolfin leaned forward, and for the first time the polished manner gave way to something like genuine counsel. "Madame, I will tell you what I tell every ruler with a good cause and a bad army. Do not borrow enough to lose. Borrow enough to win, and win quickly, because interest is the only army in the world that grows stronger every night. You need a husband with soldiers more than you need my gold. You need both, which is why I am here. The Lorraine treaty is the best instrument your council has produced in twenty years. Sign it, and my house will lend. Refuse it, and I will still lend, against the same liens, at eighteen percent, because the customs are good even under a conqueror. I am a patient man and a patient creditor. Choose."

He set down his cup, having graded it with a small, almost imperceptible nod.

Jeanne was quiet for a moment. In the ledger behind her eyes she wrote: _he wants the treaty because Lorraine's roads secure his trade route. He is not on my side. He is on the side of the road. That makes him reliable, as roads are reliable._

"I will give your house its answer with the others," she said. "But you may tell your partners in Venice that the Duchess of Vauréal does not intend to lose, and therefore does not intend to pay eighteen percent."

He smiled then, a real smile, brief and white. "Then I will tell them fourteen. Good day, madame. It has been a genuine pleasure; most audiences at this hour are conducted in tears."

* * *

The guildsmen came last, in the lower hall, as Foulques had ordained, six of them in the long sober gowns of city office, their boots polished to a defiance of the mud outside. Maître Coppens led them. Sixty years old, broad and grey and slow-moving, with the hands of a man who had worked a loom before he worked a guildhall, he looked like a farmer and thought like a pope.

He knelt, rose, and presented the articles engrossed on parchment, with the seals of the Sainterre guilds depending from it in a row like plump red fruit. Jeanne read them in front of him, taking her time, the hall full of guards and clerks and the deliberate theater of power.

The articles asked for a great deal: an elected council of aldermen, a city court with jurisdiction over trade and labor, a fixed toll schedule that the duchess could not alter without the council's consent, the city's own trained militia under its own officers, and a general amnesty for the assemblies of the past month. In exchange, the guilds offered renewed allegiance, a war contribution, and the keeping of the peace in the northern towns.

"You understand," Jeanne said, "that what you ask for is a charter, and that the granting of charters is the one act by which a ruler may be quietly dismembered while the bandages are applied by the patient himself."

Coppens did not flinch. "Madame, what we ask for is what the cities of Flanders took with pikes and what your grandfather sold us by the yard at twice the price. We would rather buy. Buying leaves both parties standing."

"It leaves me standing shorter."

"It leaves you standing, madame. There are men in Sainterre who will tell you that a duchess without an army is a clerk with a seal. Willem de Key has said it in the cloth hall, to four thousand men, and he did not say it unkindly. He said it as arithmetic." The old guildsman's eyes were steady. "I am here because I believe a charter keeps the peace and de Key believes a charter is only the first wall to come down. Between us, madame, you have perhaps six weeks before his arithmetic becomes the city's."

Jeanne rolled the parchment. "I will answer the articles in writing. I give you my word the answer will be an answer, not a delay dressed as one. In the meantime, Maître Coppens, you will tell your guilds that the duchess does not negotiate with armed assemblies, and you will tell de Key that if the cloth hall fills again with pikes, the first man to answer for it will be the dean of the guild, which is to say yourself. I am told you keep excellent order. Keep it."

It was a threat and a confidence in one sentence, and he heard both. His bow, when he made it, was deeper than the envoy's had been.

When the hall had emptied, Foulques said at her shoulder, "You were excellent with the Frenchman. You were wise with the German. With the Venetian you were magnificent. With the guildsman, madame, you were a ruler."

"I was a ruler who refused nothing and granted nothing."

"That is what ruling is, at the beginning." He hesitated over his papers, a hesitation so unlike him that she looked up. "The last audience remains. And after it, my lady, there is the Lorraine question, which will not survive another week unanswered. The duke's own courier arrived this morning. He is not sending a proxy."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean, madame, that the Duke of Lorraine is riding to Aubecourt himself. He will be at the gates inside of ten days."

* * *

The solar had been arranged for grief and softened for seduction, though no one would have been so coarse as to call it that. A fire of applewood, candles against the afternoon gloom, the best tapestry on the wall, the one showing the hunt of the white stag rather than the one showing the siege. Oriabel was indeed in the adjoining chamber, audible in small domestic clashes of linen presses, and two of Jeanne's women sat near the window with their sewing, present as furniture is present.

Gui de Hautperthuis came in black, which suited him, and knelt with the elegant gravity of a man who had practiced the gesture in two reigns. He rose tall at forty-five, his beard threaded with grey, his eyes light and clever and set in a face whose sorrow looked almost genuine. He had been, Jeanne remembered, one of the handsomest men of her father's generation. He had also, her father's ledgers said, been late to every battle and early to every tax exemption, and the ledgers were not given to flattery.

"Madame my cousin." His voice was low and warm, pitched for a deathbed. "I have buried my own wife, and I know the particular winter of these first weeks. If I had a hundred lives, I would spend the first of them in keeping this house from adding to your burdens."

"My burdens are the duchy's, cousin. They cannot be kept, only carried. Sit."

He sat, accepted wine, and did not drink it, a man too sensitive for his appetites. For ten minutes he was perfect: he spoke of her father with the precise affection of a kinsman who had genuinely admired him without liking him; he described the mourning in his own lands; he asked after her sleep, her appetite, the cathedral's arrangements, all of it with the tenderness of an uncle and none of an uncle's rights. Then, by a road as smooth as a paved causeway, he arrived at himself.

"The vassals are frightened, madame. Frightened men do foolish things, as the Sainterre assembly shows. What they need is a visible hand, a sword at the council table, a name they know. I do not seek office. I seek to serve. But if the council were to name a guardian of the marches, a constable for the duration of these troubles, I would not refuse the labor. Your father would have wanted someone of the blood to stand between you and the importunate."

There it was, laid on the table like a ripe fruit: the constableship. A constable commanded the armies. A constable with the blood of the old dukes in his veins, admitted to the council and the fortresses, became, in a season or two, the man to whom the towns sent their keys and the envoys their letters. She had watched her father explain this very mechanism to her at fourteen, over a dish of pears, using another ambitious lord as the example.

"My father," she said, "kept the constable's office vacant for eleven years because he said a sword at the council table soon learns to point at the chair. I believe I will honor his judgment. The marshal has the military council; I have the seal; between us we shall muddle through. But I am grateful for the offer, cousin. Such offers are rare. So is the forty-man train you brought with you. The Haut-Tertre has rarely housed so many of your people at once."

A flicker, gone almost before it arrived, the candle of his self-love guttering in a draught. Then the warm smile was back, adjusted. "A kinsman's precaution, madame. The roads are not safe for a small party."

"No. They are not." She set down her cup. "Which is why I have a service to ask of you rather than an office to give. Your lands lie closest to the fords. I should like the Hautperthuis tenants to repair the road between Pont-d'Ortelle and the border, at your charge, and to provision the fort there through the summer. A work of peace, cousin, and a visible one. The whole marches will see the name of Hautperthuis on every sack of grain."

It was brilliant and he knew it. Refusal looked like disloyalty; acceptance put his money and his tenants at the service of her fortress and bound his interest to the road he might otherwise have opened to the French. For a long moment the light eyes went cold, and she saw the man under the performance, calculating the cost. Then he bowed his head.

"The duchess does me honor. It shall be done."

"Good. Then we are both served." She rose, ending it. "Give my greetings to your household. And cousin — the repairs by Easter. I keep accounts of promises as of everything else."

When he had gone, Oriabel drifted through the connecting door on the feet of a woman who had been listening at it. "He wanted the army."

"He wanted the chair. The army was merely the key."

"Will he do the road?"

"He will do half of it and bill me for the whole, and he will write to Paris before the month is out. Have Lisette's people watch his couriers. I want names, not suspicions."

* * *

Night came early in April, or she had grown older. The council had dined and gone; the household had performed its evening rituals of coals and curtains and prayers; the Haut-Tertre settled around her the way a great animal settles, full of small creaks and distant warmth. Jeanne sat in her father's chamber with the Lorraine treaty spread under the lamp, and Foulques's commentary beside it in the chancellor's careful hand.

It was a dry document and a desperate one, and she read it as she read everything, twice: once for what it said, once for what it wanted.

It said: a defensive league and a marriage between Jeanne, Duchess of Vauréal, and Thiébaut, Duke of Lorraine. It said the spouses would retain full and separate sovereignty in their own dominions, neither reigning in the other's lands. It said Lorraine would maintain, at Vauréal's expense, a field force of twelve hundred lances and three thousand foot for the duration of the French war. It assigned her a dower of revenues in Lorraine, and him a lifetime interest in a ring of Vauréal border manors, reverting to her crown on his death. It said the children of the marriage would inherit both houses. It said, in a clause Foulques had marked in the margin as _without precedent in our favor_, that in the event of her death without issue, Vauréal would pass according to its own succession law and never into the keeping of the Lorrainer.

What it wanted was simpler than all of that. It wanted her money and his soldiers on the same side before Graçay dried the spring roads. It wanted a son to solder two patchwork dominions into something France could not swallow in a mouthful. It wanted, in the oldest language of her trade, a gate between two houses, and the gate was her body, and everyone had signed except the gate.

She thought of the boy she had known. The grammar of the treaty kept turning, in her ungovernable mind, into the grammar of another document, seven years old: a boy's face in the torchlight of this same palace, carefully empty, saying words he had learned the way one learns a part. She had not spoken his name aloud in this chamber since. She made herself speak it now, into the lamp-lit silence, just above a whisper, to prove it had no power.

"Thiébaut."

It had power. It had all the old power, and she hated it for that, the way one hates a door that will not stay shut.

Ten days. He was on the road with his twelve hundred lances' worth of promises and his careful treaty and his seven years of silence, riding toward the Haut-Tertre, and she would have to meet him at the gate in her father's place and give him her hand in front of every vulture in Christendom, and not one of them would know that the hand being given had once, at fifteen, been promised differently in a storm.

She folded the treaty along its creases and put it into the casket with the unsent letters, because that was where documents of that kind belonged. Then she drew the fresh ledger back across the table and wrote the day's entry: four audiences, one loan in prospect, one charter to answer, one constable refused, one road bought, one marriage to be made to serve her.

At the bottom of the page she added a line smaller than the rest, the line no clerk would ever copy.

_He is coming. Be the duchess first. The girl can be dealt with afterwards._
