# Chapter Fifteen — The Frost March

The cold came down from the Vosges like an invading army, and by the second day in the Harchies the men were calling it the third enemy.

Thiébaut rode at the head of the column with his hood up and his breath pluming, watching six hundred lances and mounted archers diminish into the long white corridor of the forest road. There was no sound but the muted iron of hooves on frozen ruts, the creak of leather, and the endless, patient sifting of snow through bare branches. No trumpets, no banners unfurled, no fires except the small careful ones at the midday halt; a winter march was a thing done in whispers, because noise carried across snow the way it carried across water, and the gascons were somewhere in these woods with the same arithmetic and different orders.

"Smugglers' country," Jean said, cantering up from the rear where he had been checking the stragglers. His eyebrows were white with it. "The charcoal burners have three governments: the duke's, the forest's, and their own, and only the last one collects. I've sent two of them ahead with silver. We'll know every French picket before it knows us."

"How are the horses?"

"Twelve thrown shoes, two foundered. Better than I feared. The biscuit's holding. The men's boots are not; I've got the cobblers working at every halt. A march in February is mostly a question of feet." Jean looked sideways at his brother. "You're thinking about her convoy."

"I'm thinking about whether ten days was enough, and whether the western track exists, and whether I've put my wife's gunners in a forest no soldier would choose."

"Your wife, as you keep discovering, is not a soldier. That's her advantage." The Bastard grinned, white-mustached with frost. "The track exists. I've used it. And she didn't put the gunners anywhere; she asked for volunteers and got twice the number, because every man in the army wants to be in the story where the duchess's guns come through the snow. You married a legend, brother. Try to look pleased."

They made eight miles on the third day and nine on the fourth, threading the charcoal roads, sleeping in their cloaks around buried fires, the cold so deep that the sentries were rotated every hour and the wine froze in its skins. On the fifth morning the forest scouts came back with the first hard intelligence: the French brigade investing Sarrebourg had posted a screen of light horse in the eastern wood, four strong pickets, and, the thing Thiébaut had feared, a company of francs-archers working their way down the same road he was on, meaning Graçay's officers had not entirely forgotten the Harchies.

"Then we don't give them time to finish remembering." He spread the scouts' bark map on a shield. "We go around their screen on the north track, hit the archers in their camp at dusk, and keep moving. No pursuit past the road. Jean, the van is yours. I want prisoners with tongues and no noise after dark."

* * *

The francs-archers had made their camp in a clearing where two tracks met, the confident camp of men who believed the war was elsewhere: poor sentries, big fires, their pikes stacked and their tents up. The snow gave the attackers in their white surcoats the only gift winter ever gave, which was invisibility, and Jean's van was among the tents before the French trumpeter got his instrument to his lips.

It was a short, ugly business in the failing light. Thiébaut came in with the second wave and found the fight already breaking into its components: a knot of French infantry making a brave, useless stand around their captain's banner, men running into the trees, the Lorraine archers shooting the runners down with the cold efficiency of huntsmen. The captain himself, a young Norman with a sword in each hand, sold his banner dearly and took a Lorraine man-at-arms through the throat before Jean's hammer blow to the helmet put him in the snow, and then it was over, and the dark wood was full of the sounds of wounded men freezing.

Thiébaut was taking the count, four dead of his, eleven French, twenty prisoners, the rest scattered, when the scout he had posted on the eastern track came in at a dead run through the snow with the news that turned the evening: the picket screen had not been routine. A full squadron of French men-at-arms, two hundred lances, was moving up the forest road behind them, warned by the camp's first trumpet, and they were perhaps forty minutes out.

"Battle in the open, in this snow, with their weight against our tired men?" Brive's kinsman, the captain of the van's second company, was shaking his head. "We should draw off into the west."

"West is the convoy's track, and we don't lead two hundred fresh lances onto fifty gunners and four sledge guns." Thiébaut was already looking at the ground, the soldier's automatic inventory: the clearing, the stacked French pikes, the narrow road with its high snowbanks, the bend where the trees met overhead. "We don't run. We take their charge. Jean, the pikes — all of them, French and ours, across the road at the bend, butts braced in the frozen ruts. Archers in the trees on both flanks, dismounted. The horses to the rear. They'll come up the road at a trot because the snow won't allow a gallop, and they'll come two files wide because the banks won't allow more. A charge that wide is a procession. We receive it, we break it, and then the archers take it in the flanks."

It was the right ground and the right answer, and the men knew it, and they moved with the will of soldiers who have been spared a decision. The pikes went up across the bend in a bristling hedge, the archers melted into the snow-laden trees, and Thiébaut dismounted with the rest and took his place at the center with a poleaxe in his hands and the bad shoulder already warning him.

The French came out of the grey dusk at the trot, two hundred armored men on armored horses, lances lowered, the breath of them white in the road. At fifty paces the first rank saw the pike hedge and the narrowing banks and understood the trap, and could do nothing about it because the ranks behind were still pressing. The charge met the pikes at the walk it had been reduced to, and the road became a screaming wall of men and horses and steel.

Thiébaut's war narrowed to the length of his poleaxe. A lance shattered against his shield; he killed the horse with the axe's hammer side and got the rider on the way down. The snow underfoot went red and slippery. He could hear Jean roaring on his left, the archers' arrows thudding into the flanks, the French trumpets blowing recall that no one could reach. The French front was folding, but the press behind kept shoving, and a dismounted man-at-arms got inside his axe's reach with a short sword, and the blade went through his left forearm's weak place in the harness, and the shoulder screamed, and he took the man's jaw with his gauntleted fist and went to one knee in the snow.

The pike meant for his throat came out of the tangle without warning, a Frenchman's weapon lifted high over the press, and there was no time and no angle, and then Jean was there.

The Bastard threw himself sideways into the line of the thrust with no weapon interposed, taking the pike through the meat of his own upper chest and shoulder, and his weight carried both him and the Frenchman down into the bloody snow. Thiébaut saw his brother's face go white, the eyes still open and furious, and something cold and enormous moved in him then, colder than the forest, and he got his axe up and killed the pikeman and the next man and the one after that, and the French center, which had been bending, broke.

After that it was the road again, the long butcher's pursuit that winter makes slow and terrible. By full dark the squadron was gone in fragments into the trees, leaving sixty dead and as many prisoners, and the Lorraine lances held the bend and the road and the night, and Thiébaut, bleeding from the forearm, knelt in the trampled snow beside his brother.

* * *

Jean was alive. The surgeons said it three times, as though the fact required reassurance. The pike had gone in below the collarbone and come out by the shoulder blade, missing the great vessels by the width of a finger, which the chief surgeon, a man who had seen every wound the century produced, called the work of a guardian angel with steady hands. They got the shaft out with two men holding him down and a leather bit between his teeth, and Jean did not scream, though his did, and then they cauterized and bound and gave him wine, and the Bastard lay in the captured French tent with his face the color of the snow outside and said, through the wine, "Did we win?"

"You know we won. Lie still."

"Then I'm delighted. Tell me I didn't take a pike for a draw." He closed his eyes. "Thiébaut. It was coming at your throat. I'd do it again, which is the worst thing about being an honest man."

"The surgeon says if the wound fever takes you, I'll kill you myself for making me write the letter to your mistress."

"Which one? Be precise; the letter must be precise." But the grin was weak, and the fever was already there by midnight, the hot dry skin and the wandering talk, and Thiébaut sat by the brazier with his own arm bandaged and the campaign's arithmetic turning coldly in his mind: the French screen broken, the road open, the garrison at Sarrebourg still holding, and somewhere to the west, on a track that might or might not exist, the convoy he had staked his wife's gunners on.

They marched at dawn with the wounded on sledges and Jean among them, and reached the heights above Sarrebourg on the seventh day, and looked down on the French siege lines in the valley: the gascon camp, the zigzag approach trenches, the two light guns already emplaced against the fort's southern wall, and the fort itself, battered but flying the Lorraine standard, which meant the garrison still had breath.

"The investment is loose," Thiébaut said to his captains, glass to his eye. "They never expected a relief in February, and they've built for comfort, not siege discipline. We hit at dawn tomorrow from the wooded slope. If the convoy's guns have arrived, we'll hear them on the western road. If they haven't—"

"If they haven't," said the van captain, "we do it with what we have."

"With what we have, then."

* * *

They came through the snow at first light, six hundred dismounted lances and archers in their white surcoats, down the wooded slope onto the flank of the gascon camp, and for the first ten minutes it was the francs-archers all over again: surprise, trumpets, tents going down, the enemy running for his weapons. But the gascons were better men than the francs-archers, and their commander was a professional, and within the quarter hour the French had formed a proper square around their guns, and the attack stalled in the frozen mud of the camp approach, and Thiébaut, one-armed and raging, could see the numbers beginning to tell.

Then, from the western road, the unmistakable rolling thunder of artillery.

Four short-barreled guns on their sledges came through the trees at the trot of their draft horses, with fifty mounted gunners in Vauréal red and silver around them, and the crews unlimbered and loaded with the drilled speed Van Brock and Hautemer had spent the winter teaching, and the first round went into the closed French square at close range, and the second, and the square became a thing with a hole in its side, and the Sarrebourg garrison, seeing the relief on the heights and the guns in the road, sallied out of the southern gate with the remnants of its strength.

The gascon brigade did what a professional brigade does when the ground disappears under it: it withdrew in good order, spiking its two guns and leaving its camp, its stores, and its wounded, and by noon the valley was Thiébaut's, and the Lorraine standard over the fort was answered by the joined arms on the relief column, and the snow was being trampled into a road between the two positions by men laughing and weeping in the same breath.

He found the convoy's commander, a young Grisay gunner named Leclerc, and said only, "The track. Was it as bad as they said?"

"Worse, my lord. But we had charcoal burners as guides, and the duchess's orders, which said the guns were to be at Sarrebourg on the eighth day, and it is the eighth day." The man allowed himself a small, frost-cracked smile. "She also said, if we were late, we needn't bother arriving."

* * *

She arrived in the afternoon, at the head of the convoy's rear guard, on a horse that looked as exhausted as she did, in a fur-lined leather coat with snow crusted on the hood and the ducal ring glinting on its chain over the leather. Thiébaut, who had believed the worst of every courier for eight days, saw her come through the captured camp and stood very still for a moment, because the alternative was doing something unsoldierly in front of six hundred men.

"You came yourself." He met her at the edge of the tents, and the words came out more roughly than he intended.

"The track was passable, and I wanted to see the sledge guns work." She dismounted without waiting for a hand, stiff-limbed, and looked him over with the clerk's eye he had learned to fear. "Your arm. And Jean. Take me to him."

Jean was feverish but himself, and he saw her and said, "Madame, I have been shot with a pike in your service, and I intend to spend the fact for at least a year," and she sat beside him and gave him the willow-bark draught herself and said he might spend it for two if he lived, which made him laugh and then gasp, and the surgeon said the laugh was the best sign he had seen.

The storm came at dusk, the true February storm the forest had been promising, wind and driven snow that closed the valley within the hour and made the camp tents into a world of their own. The command tent went down in the first gust, and in the confusion the charcoal burners who had guided the convoy offered their hut in the trees, a low smoky structure of stone and turf with a central fire and room for four, and Thiébaut and Jeanne took shelter there with one brazier and the guards posted outside in pairs, rotated every half hour.

It was warm in the hut, after the cold; the heat was extraordinary and close, smelling of smoke and resin and the drying wool of their clothes. They sat across the fire from each other with their boots off and their frozen feet toward the embers, and for a while there was only the storm's noise and the fire's, and the long, trembling relief of two people who had expected to lose each other and had not.

"I read your dispatches every night," she said. "The ones that reached us. The gaps were the worst part. There was a day with no word at all, the day of the road fight, and I spent it doing the accounts, and I remember thinking that if I stopped doing the accounts I would start something I couldn't stop."

"The road fight was close." He looked at the fire. "Jean took a pike that was meant for me. In the press, I didn't see it coming. He did. He's been doing that since I was twelve. I don't know how to be a man whose brother keeps his accounts in blood."

"Then keep yours in something else. The book of names. The pensions. The things you can pay, while you can't pay him back." She was quiet a moment. "Thiébaut. Why were you twenty years old in my father's court with no one in the world but a hostage's oath? I know the treaty story. I mean the true one."

It came out of the snow and the warmth and the day's nearness, and he understood that she was asking, at last, about the country behind the wall; not the whole of it, perhaps, but the road into it. The fire popped. Outside, the guards stamped their feet.

He nearly told her.

He had the words ready; he had carried them for seven years, polished and re-polished in the dark nights of Lorraine, the full account: the dawn in the solar, the chancellor's face, the old duke's terms, the threats against the maid and the convent, the written renunciation and the public performance, the price of speech set at other people's ruin. He could have said it all in this smoky hut, with the storm to keep the world away, and watched her seven years of belief about him fall into the fire and change.

And Foulques's face rose in his mind instead, the clerk who had been the instrument, who had grown old in the same court as the woman he served, carrying his own case of documents and his own guilt; and the rule he had made himself in the years of silence, that he would not unload the truth at a time and place of his choosing, when the telling might break the chancellor before she had the whole of it, might turn the court against an old man who had, in the end, kept the evidence. And beyond even that, the deeper thing: he would not take the story as a gift from the storm. A confession made in the relief after battle, in a warm hut, when she was shaken and he was wounded, was a confession smuggled past her judgment. She deserved to receive it standing, in her own capital, with every paper in front of her, and decide what to do with both of them.

So he said, instead, "The true one is that my father died with the duchy mortgaged and my regents selling the furniture, and your father's court was the only school in Europe that would take me on credit. I learned everything there. Latin, gunnery, how a court eats a man who trusts it. And one lesson I never learned how to unlearn." He held her eyes across the fire. "Jeanne. I am going to tell you the whole of the old story, properly, with the papers and the names and the witnesses, and soon. Not in fragments, not in a storm, not because we might have died today. You will have it whole or not at all. Will you give me until the spring?"

She searched his face, the firelight moving on it, and he let her look. "You're asking me to keep carrying the question."

"I'm asking you to trust that I'm not dodging it. You've taught me the value of a document over a rumor. This is the one case where I agree with your own methods."

That surprised a small, unwilling laugh out of her. "Very well. Until the spring. But Thiébaut — when the spring comes, I want every key. I have a green case of my own to open, and I think they're the same lock."

The cold went through him then, a different cold from the forest's. "What do you know of a green case?"

"I know my chancellor keeps one he won't show me, and that it concerns a girl who did nothing, and that he drinks alone at night because of it. I've had him watched since Christmas." Her face was steady. "Whatever it is, we open it together. That's my condition."

"Together. Agreed." He reached across the space between them, and after a moment she put her hand in his, and they sat like that while the storm spent itself on the hut, the fire making its small domestic sounds, two people bound by paper and war and the promise of a truth that would either weld them or cut them free.

* * *

In the morning, while Thiébaut made the rounds of the camp's frozen sentries, Jeanne sat with Jean in the wounded tent, where the Bastard lay propped on furs with the willow bark at his elbow and a face the color of old parchment.

"He didn't sleep," Jean said, without opening his eyes. "He sat by the fire and watched you breathe, the way a man watches a fuse. You have no idea what you are to him, and that's probably for the best, because if you did you'd be impossible."

"You're feverish."

"I'm lucid, which is worse for you. Madame, I'll tell you a thing I've never told him I noticed. In the year after he came home from your father's court, he didn't play at cards, didn't hunt for pleasure, didn't look at a woman. He worked. He rebuilt the garrisons like a man building walls around something. We thought it was the duchy. It wasn't only the duchy." The Bastard opened his eyes, bright with fever and certainty. "Whatever happened in that house, he has never once in my hearing said a word against you. Not when it would have been easy, not when his own lords called the marriage a folly. Whatever he's carrying, it isn't blame of you."

Jeanne said nothing for a while. The tent's brazier ticked in the cold. "He's promised me the whole truth by spring."

"Then he'll give it. He's the only man I know who treats a promise the way other men treat a wall: load-bearing." Jean winced, settling against the furs. "Now. Since I've been shot and am permitted speeches. Take care of him out there. He thinks he's made of the same stuff as the fortresses, and he isn't. The shoulder's worse than he lets on, and he hasn't slept a full night since Sarrebourg the first time. A man who doesn't sleep eventually makes decisions for a tired brain and calls them courage."

"I'll take care of him on the condition that you take care of yourself. I have no intention of explaining a dead Bastard to your mistress, whichever one the letter would have gone to."

That earned a genuine, gasping laugh and a wince. "Cruel. Ducal and cruel. I begin to see why he watches the fuse."

She left him with the bark refreshed and the tent flap secured, and found Thiébaut at the horse lines, stamping circulation into his booted feet, and when he turned at the sound of her steps she looked at him with Jean's words still in her ears, the man building walls around something, and said nothing of them, only gave him the hot cup she had brought, and their gloved fingers touched, and the camp went on with its morning around them.

* * *

By the third day the storm had passed and the valley was a white dazzle under a hard blue sky, and the work of the relief could be done properly. The French stores fed the garrison through the rest of the winter; the captured guns were sledged back to Sarrebourg's walls; the gascon prisoners, paid for out of the Vauréal treasury, went east under guard to the prisoner cages at Nancy. Thiébaut wrote the dispatches himself, naming the Vauréal gunners first and his own lances after, and saw to it that the story rode every road before the army did.

Jean's fever broke on the fifth night, sweating and swearing and demanding solid food, and the surgeon said he would live and probably argue, and the camp breathed again. They carried him south on a sledge lined with furs, and he complained about every mile of it, which the men took as the finest possible omen.

The march back was slower and lighter, the forest no longer an enemy but a road they had proved. At the edge of Vauréal, where the watchtower stood with its beacon lit at last as he had ordered on his wedding journey, Thiébaut reined in and looked back once at the white wall of the Harchies.

"A month ago the French thought the winter belonged to them," he said.

Jeanne, beside him, looked ahead to the southern fields and the faint smoke of the first villages. "Nothing belongs to anyone in February. That's the lesson of the snow. It all comes back in the spring." She touched heels to the mare. "And in the spring, my lord, we open the green case."

He followed her down the road toward the light, with six hundred men and four famous guns and a wounded, grumbling brother behind them, and the long war ahead, and the promise of the truth waiting like a second spring under all the frozen ground.
