# Chapter Eleven — The Weight of Bodies

They came for Saint-Vaast in the third week of May, because the river was low enough to ford and high enough to keep the French supply wagons chained to the one bridge, which was the arithmetic of the whole affair.

The fort stood at the end of its stone bridge like a fist on an arm, a square castle with a walled town huddled under its guns, and the French had spent six weeks making it uglier: new earthworks at the bridgehead, a ditch cut across the only meadow, two bombards on the keep whose barrels the scouts could see glinting in the sun. Its commandant, a Gascon knight named de Noailles, had four hundred men and the confidence of a garrison that had never yet been attacked by an army it could see.

Thiébaut laid the siege in the careful, unromantic way he laid everything. "No assault until the walls are worth assaulting," he told the war council in the command tent, his finger on the sketched plan. "We batter the bridgehead earthworks first, because they're new and raw, and we do it from across the river where their guns can't answer at full weight. When the earthworks are gone, the town's river wall is open. _Marguerite_ and her sisters make a breach. The infantry goes through at dawn, when a garrison's watch is at its thinnest. The cavalry crosses the bridge behind them to stop any relief from the south. Nobody enters the keep before the gunners have its gate covered. Questions?"

Brive had one, about the honor of the first assault, and got the answer he expected: the forlorn hope went to a mixed company of Vauréal and Lorraine men under Jean's command, because the duke's brother leading in the first wave was worth a hundred speeches, and because Jean would personally have murdered anyone given the task instead.

Jeanne sat through the military details with the map open on her own table, and asked the questions the soldiers forgot. "The townsfolk. Four hundred hearths inside those walls. What are their provisions?"

"Three weeks', at French rates of seizure." Thiébaut did not look up. "Which means their own stores are already empty. The commandant is feeding his men on the town's winter grain."

"Then the town wants him gone. I want proclamations, before the first gun fires, in the vernacular, nailed to the bridge posts and shot over the walls on arrows. The duchess's army makes war on the French garrison, not the town. Any townsman who keeps to his house is safe in person and goods. Any soldier who surrenders before the breach keeps his arms and his ransom rights. Any man who harms a civilian answers to my provosts. Make the words plain. Foulques, I want no Latin in it anywhere."

The chancellor bowed. "A siege won in the proclamations before it's won at the breach."

"A siege won at all," she said. "Write it."

* * *

The proclamations went out at dawn, nailed to the bridge timbers and wrapped around arrows shot into the town in high arcs, and by noon the answer came back, not from de Noailles but from the town: the parish priest, out under a white cloth on a mule, with a frightened alderman behind him. They were shown through the lines to the command tent, and the priest, a stout man who had clearly decided that courage was his only remaining profession, spoke for both of them.

"The town has three days' bread, madame. The French took the rest for their stores. They have also taken the bells from our church to make gunpowder measure, and twelve men for forced labor on the earthworks. The commandant says your proclamations are lies and that any man who speaks of surrender will hang. The town would like to know whether the proclamations are lies."

"You have my word, which is sealed." Jeanne pushed the fair copy across the table. "Read it yourself, Father, in the square, with my herald beside you. Houses and goods untouched. The French garrison alone is our quarrel. Open the river gate to my men when the breach is made, and the town keeps its charter and its grain and earns a remission of this year's war contribution. The alternative is the assault, and in an assault no proclamation in Christendom can guarantee a single house."

The priest read it twice, moving his lips. "And if the commandant prevents us?"

"Then keep your people indoors and your doors barred, and wait for the duke's provosts. They'll be the second men through the breach, and their first work is to hang looters." She paused. "Father. What is the garrison's true temper?"

"They're Gascons, madame. Good soldiers, bad guests. They believe the relief column comes from the south before the month ends. They do not know what I learned in your lines, that your horse holds the bridge road. If they knew, half of them would be on the walls arguing with the other half."

"Then see that they learn it. The truth is in the proclamations; print it on their dinner tables. A town that talks itself into surrender saves us a breach and saves itself a sack. Go with the herald. And Father," as he rose, "the twelve laboring men. When this is over, send me their names. I keep a book of them."

When he had gone, Thiébaut, who had said nothing through the interview, leaned back in his chair. "You're besieging them with paperwork."

"Paper is cheaper than powder. Every man in that town now knows the terms and knows his neighbor knows them. De Noailles has lost the one thing a garrison needs: the certainty of the men behind him." She folded her copy. "If it works, the breach opens an empty wall. If it doesn't, the guns do what guns do. Either way, the town has been told the truth, which means whatever happens, the next town believes my proclamations."

"The next town," he said, "is already studying this one. Yes. I know. That's the part they don't teach in the romances."

* * *

The guns spoke for two days. She watched the first day from the battery line, at Thiébaut's side, and learned what her practice-range shots became when they reached stone: the slow, patient mathematics of destruction, stone balls striking the bridgehead earthworks at measured intervals, the earth faces crumbling, the French working parties rebuilding at night and the Lorraine gunners undoing it again by morning. On the second day the bombards turned on the river wall, and then it was possible to watch a wall die, block by block, the medieval stone no match for the new bronze throats, until by dusk a ragged breach gaped like a wound and the French drums could be heard inside, mustering the defense.

That night the camp was quiet with the particular quiet before an assault, men writing letters and sharpening steel and pretending to sleep. She walked the lines after the last watch, with Lisette behind her and two guards at a distance, and saw the things no proclamation could reach: a young soldier teaching his friend his home village's name in case a letter had to be sent; a veteran methodically greasing every rivet of his harness; Jean, by a fire, shaving his head with a knife so no one could grab his hair in the breach, and grinning at her when he saw her.

"Can't sleep either, madame?"

"I'm reviewing the hospital tents. The surgeons have asked for more linen and more vinegar, and I've given them the household's table linen rather than wait on the baggage. I want you to do something for me, Jean."

"Name it."

"Send the wounded to the rear the moment they fall. No heroics about staying in the line. A man down in the breach is a man who bleeds out before the surgeons ever see him. I've put twelve stretcher-bearers with the first wave, and they answer to me, not the captains."

The Bastard's grin faded. "You've done this before."

"I've read the surgeon's accounts of the Roches-Noires. Three hundred men died of wounds who needn't have, because there was no one whose work it was to carry them. My father's army did not number the wounded. Mine will." She held his eyes. "Bring my husband back, Jean. That's the other thing."

"Madame, I've been keeping him alive since he was twelve. I'm not stopping for a French wall."

* * *

The assault went in at first light, through the mist off the river, and she heard it rather than saw it from her post at the rear: the trumpets, the roar of three hundred men running, the sudden, deeper sound of the breach defense opening fire, the guns of the keep answering. The hospital tents stood two hundred paces behind the battery, already prepared, the trestle tables laid out, the surgeons in their leather aprons sharpening with small, terrible sounds, the coals glowing for the irons.

The first wounded came within the quarter hour, carried by her stretcher-bearers, and then there was no time for anything but the work.

She had imagined she knew what battle was. She had read the chronicles, seen the lists, watched the probe at the ford from a windmill. None of it was this. The smell was the first thing, the iron-and-bowel reek of opened men, and under it the vinegar and the coals and the strange, sweet stink of a body the surgeons could not save. The sound was the second: not the trumpets and glory of the camp stories but the noise human animals made when their bodies were failing, screaming and praying and cursing in four languages and sometimes, worst of all, calling for their mothers in voices that had not finished changing.

She had intended to oversee. Instead she worked, because there was more work than hands, and her hands were steady, and a duchess in a bloodied apron was worth two surgeons' boys who froze. She held legs for the saw and turned away her face only the first time; she poured vinegar into the long slashing wounds the French bills made; she threaded needles and fetched linen and learned which screams meant a man was dying and which meant he was merely feeling it. A man with his face half gone by a gunstone gripped her wrist with his one good hand and would not let go, and she knelt in his blood and said the prayers with him until the grip loosened, and she closed his eye herself because no one else was free.

The boy came at midday, carried by two bearers who were themselves splashed with someone else's red. He was fourteen, a page in the household guard, an Aubecourt boy she knew by sight, the one who had poured her wine at the Easter feast and blushed when she thanked him. A crossbow bolt had gone in under his ribs, and the surgeon looked at the wound and looked at her and gave the smallest shake of the head, the economy of a man who had learned to spend his time where it could win.

She went to him anyway. They made a place for him at the edge of the tent, away from the worst of the noise, and she knelt and took his hand, and he knew her, which was the thing that would come back to her in the nights.

"Madame." His lips were white. "I ran. In the breach. I want you to know I didn't stop running."

"I know you didn't. I have the reports. You were the third man through." She had no idea if it was true; she made it true by saying it, and watched it steady him. "What's your name? Tell me again, so I have it exactly for the letter."

"Philippe. Philippe Aubert, of the lower town, my lady. My mother keeps the chandler's by the Mercer's Row."

"Then listen to me, Philippe Aubert. I will write to your mother myself, in my own hand, and she will have the pension and the shop's freedom from tolls for her life, and the whole lower town will know what you did at Saint-Vaast. Do you hear me? These are the duchess's words, and the duchess keeps her accounts."

He heard her. Something like a smile crossed the grey face, and he said, "I ran so fast," and then, a while later, with her hand still holding his, he stopped breathing, and one of the bearers came and closed his eyes with a coin in them for the ferry, and she stayed on her knees because her legs would not immediately do anything else, and Lisette put a cup in her hand and said, "Drink. It's wine. Drink, my lady," and she drank.

* * *

The town fell before the afternoon. Jean's company was through the breach at the third attempt, the French commandant's banner came down over the keep by vespers, and de Noailles himself was taken alive in the gatehouse, wounded in two places and furious. The danger then was the old danger, the danger every army carried into every taken town like a plague: the sack. Soldiers who have fought their way through a breach believe, with the deep conviction of their trade, that whatever they can carry is theirs.

Thiébaut had written the orders before the assault. The provosts went through the gates with the second wave, and the gallows went up in the market square before the flags did, and three men caught looting a goldsmith's shop were hanged from the town's own signboard within the hour, in front of the townsfolk they had been about to rob. A fourth, who had struck a woman, was flogged through the streets by his own company. It was brutal and it worked; by evening the town's bakers, after a guarded conversation with the army's commissary, were baking bread for both garrisons, and the proclamations on the bridge posts were being read aloud in the taverns by men who had expected to be murdered.

De Noailles was brought to her in the town's own guildhall, which the French had used as a guardroom and which still smelled of their tobacco and wet wool. He was a man of fifty with a bandaged thigh and the manners of an older, chivalric war, and he bowed from his chair because he could not stand.

"Madame la duchesse. I offer my parole and my ransom, according to the customs."

"Granted, on the usual terms, and your surgeons may attend you with mine." She sat across from him with the articles on the table. "Before we discuss your ransom, commandant. Your garrison seized the town's grain, its bells, and its labor. The grain and the bells are the town's and go back. Your ransom is yours, and I will not inflate it to punish a defense that was, until the end, correctly conducted. One question. Why hold a town whose wall could not survive a week of guns?"

"Because my orders said hold, and a soldier who rewrites his orders is a merchant." He met her eyes. "And because I did not believe the army of a duchess new-made and a duke half-bankrupt could bring a siege train at all. I was mistaken about the guns. I was more mistaken about the proclamations. My men spent the last two nights arguing in French and Gascon about whether to kill me and open the gate."

"Then remember the argument when you're exchanged. Tell the Sire de Graçay what it cost him." She rose. "You'll be held at Aubecourt, in the officer's tower, not a common cell. My chancellor will write the ransom terms. Conduct yourself as you have today, and they'll be generous."

After he had been carried out, she sat alone in the guildhall with the clerk and wrote the letter she had promised, in her own hand, slowly, because it had to be exactly right: to the chandler's widow of the Mercer's Row, Aubecourt, the news of Philippe Aubert's death, the manner of it, the third man through the breach, the pension, the toll freedom, the gratitude of the duchess in words a chandler could read aloud in a tavern and be proud of. She wrote three drafts and kept none of the first two, and the clerk, who had watched her draft state papers in a single pass, said nothing at all.

"One more letter," she said when it was sealed. "To all parish churches of the duchy. The names of the dead of Saint-Vaast, to be read at Sunday mass from the pulpit, every name, in every church. No rank, no order of precedence. They died in the same levy; God can sort them."

She found Thiébaut in the breach itself, at dusk, standing with Brive and the engineers, his armor blood-spattered and his face the color of ash. The left shoulder was being favored, though he would not say so. When he saw her come through the rubble he excused himself and met her in the shelter of a broken wall, and the two of them looked at each other for a moment without words, because there was nothing in the language for the day they had separately spent.

"Casualty lists are being drawn," he said. "Forty-one dead of ours, perhaps ninety wounded. French dead over a hundred, the rest prisoners. No civilian deaths that the provosts can find. The proclamations held."

"The boy Philippe. The page." Her voice did not quite work. "He died in the hospital tent. I was with him."

"I heard. Jean sent word." He reached out, slowly, as though touching something fragile, and put his hand on the bloodstained apron she had forgotten she was wearing. "Jeanne. You're covered in it."

"So are you."

"I'm a soldier. It's expected of me."

"It's expected of me too. It's my duchy and my war and my taxes pay for every bolt in that breach." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and the hands were stained, and she made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "He was fourteen. He poured my wine at Easter. He kept telling me he ran."

Thiébaut did not say any of the soft useless things. He said, "The camp's ready. The tents are up by the river. Come away from the wall. There's hot water, and food you won't want, and no one who needs anything from you until dawn."

* * *

Dawn came grey over the captured town, and she kept her word about the living and the dead. The book was a plain ledger taken from the chancellery cart, and she opened it in the market square under a guard's pavilion, with the provost and the surgeons and the company clerks, and the names went in one by one: the forty-one dead of the assault first, with parish and next of kin against each name, then the wounded and their wounds, then the French dead where they could be identified and the prisoners in their companies. It took four hours. The square filled and emptied and filled again around her, soldiers and townsfolk drifting up to see the duchess of Vauréal sitting in the rain-light doing a clerk's work, and by the time she closed the book the army had its new custom, though no one had decreed it: a man who enlisted under her banners knew his name would be kept.

The stretcher-bearers she decorated herself, twelve common soldiers kneeling in the mud of the square while she put a small silver badge on each man's sleeve, a hand bearing a lamp, and raised their pay by two gros a month from the privy purse. The veterans of the old duke's wars watched with the faces of men revising everything they believed about rulers.

"Your father would have spent that silver on a banner," Brive said, low, at her shoulder.

"My father's men died unnamed in a river crossing and the account says only 'losses moderate.' I read it last winter. Banners don't write home to widows."

"No, madame." The old marshal was quiet a moment. "They don't."

Thiébaut watched the ceremony from the guildhall steps with the Bastard, and Jean said, "She's buying something you can't buy with pay."

"She's keeping accounts," Thiébaut said. "That's what she calls it."

"You know what I call it? I call it why the next levy will be oversubscribed." Jean shook his head. "We're going to have to become better men to keep up with her. I resent it already."

The command tent had a copper bath, a campaign luxury Oriabel had insisted on, and she washed the day off in water that went pink and then grey, and put on a clean shift, and sat by the brazier with her hair loose and her hands shaking now that they were allowed to. The tent flap opened and closed, and Thiébaut came in washed and out of his armor, in a shirt and hose, with the shoulder wrapped, carrying a flask and two cups.

He set them down and looked at her, and the restraint he carried like a second skin was visibly at war with something else. "If you want to be alone, say so. I'll take the other tent."

"I don't want to be alone." She said it into her cup. "I want not to be in my own head for an hour. Is that a thing you know how to do?"

"It's the one thing soldiers are good for." He set his own cup down and came to her chair, and knelt in front of her the way she had knelt by the boy, and took both her hands. "Jeanne. Look at me."

She looked. His eyes were tired and steady and entirely without the careful distance of the council.

"Tell me to stop and I stop."

"Don't stop."

He kissed her then, and it was nothing like the wedding night, nothing careful or asked in stages. It was urgent and rough and close to grief, two people who had spent the day in the presence of death clutching at the proof of life, and she answered it with the same desperation, her hands in his hair, his mouth on her throat, the brazier's heat and the wine and the distant camp sounds all running together.

They made it to the camp bed with most of their clothes still on, and there was nothing graceful in it, no instruction and no sequence, only need. He was rough when she wanted rough and gentle the instant she made a sound of pain, his hand finding hers in the dark of the tent and holding it the way he had the first night, the one fixed point. Her body, which had learned him in the slow nights at the Haut-Tertre, knew what to do even while her mind was full of the hospital tent, and somewhere in it the strangest thing happened: the boy's face and the gunsmoke and the blood began to recede behind the living warmth of the man over her, the beat of his heart against her breast, and she clung to that as though it were a rope thrown into dark water.

Afterwards they lay tangled in the sheets, both slick with sweat, the tent glowing faintly with the brazier's embers. He did not withdraw into his own side as he usually did, but stayed close, his head against her shoulder, careful of nothing except her.

"Philippe Aubert," she said into the dark. "Write that name down tomorrow. I won't trust it to anyone else's ledger."

"I'll write it with you." A pause. "And the others. All of them. We'll keep the book together. Every name."

"Every name." She stared at the tent roof. "My father never kept a book of the dead. He kept a book of the victories. I used to think that was strength."

"It's a kind of strength. It's just not yours." His arm tightened around her. "Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we count the living, which is harder."

She slept, eventually, with the weight of him beside her and the names of the dead already arranging themselves in her mind in the clerk's neat columns, and outside the tent the army of Vauréal and Lorraine kept watch over the town it had taken and not sacked, and the river went on under the broken bridge as though no one had died for it at all.
