# Chapter Ten — South to the Frontier

The army turned south on the first of May with the river at its left hand and the new charter riding in the chancellery cart under three seals, and Jeanne learned in the first hour that an army on the march is less a weapon than a traveling town with stricter opinions about dung.

Three thousand men, twelve hundred horses, forty guns on their new carriages, the ox trains and the baggage and the long tail of camp followers and sutlers and engineers, all of it stretched over a league of road in an order Thiébaut's sergeants enforced with curses in three languages. Scouts went first, light cavalry fanning through the woods. The vanguard followed, Lorraine lances under Jean's banner. Then the guns and the pikemen, then the standards and the chancellery, then the infantry and the baggage, then a strong rearguard, because every soldier who had ever been surprised lived or died by his opinion of rearguards. Start and stop were marked by the same trumpets every day, and the camp went up and came down in the same order every evening, and Jeanne, riding at the center with the household guard, understood after three days that this monotonous discipline was the actual Lorraine military secret, not courage or plate.

"Your father's armies moved like weddings," Thiébaut told her when she said so. "Everyone in his best clothes, no two men agreed where the wine was. Mine move like a brewhouse. Boring, early, and impossible to hurry."

They made nine leagues a day when the roads were good and five when they were not. She had expected discomfort and found instead a strange, exhausting freedom. At the Haut-Tertre every hour was an audience; on the road the state shrank to a pavilion, a folding table, and the people who could keep up. She rode the grey mare in a divided skirt and a leather jerkin over her gown, with the ducal ring on its chain and a dagger at her belt, and the soldiers, who had expected a court lady in a curtained litter, revised their opinions audibly in the ranks. She heard one Lorraine man-at-arms tell another that the duchess sat a horse like a sergeant's widow, which Brive considered an insult and she considered the highest praise available.

The country changed as they went. The rich northern plain gave way to the marches: thinner fields, more stone, watchtowers on every rise, villages with the walls and the watchful eyes of places that had been raided within living memory. The burned hamlet Thiébaut had found on his road was rebuilding, two new roofs among the blackened beams, and the woman with the child was at her door when the column passed, and she looked at the standard and then at the duke, and gave him a nod that was not a bow. He returned it, equally level, and the column moved on.

"She got the purse," he said, when Jeanne drew level. "And the garrison letter. I asked after her. She used half of it to buy a watchdog and half to buy a crossbow. I consider that a successful policy."

"You make policy by purse?"

"I make most things by purse. Battles are the failure of accounting."

* * *

Mont d'Or rose out of the frontier hills like an argument: a great rock with a fortress crowning it, its walls following the stone, the river road passing directly under its guns, the one hard point between Graçay and the soft belly of Vauréal. The commandant met them at the outer gate with his sixty-one men drawn up as though for inspection, and Jeanne saw at once why Thiébaut had wanted the man kept.

Captain Valvert was fifty, thin as a fence post, with a face weathered to the color of old leather and the kind of calm that comes from having decided exactly how one intends to die and finding the decision restful. His garrison had been paid, reinforced, and re-gunned, and the fortress stores were stacked with the obsessiveness of a man who had spent four months expecting the worst. He knelt on his own parade ground and rose at her gesture and said, without preamble, "The guns cover all three approaches, the well is full, the cisterns are full, and I've mined the outer ditch in two places, madame. If the French want this rock, they may have it at the same price I'd charge the devil."

"Show me," she said, and he showed her.

For two hours she walked the walls with Valvert and Thiébaut and Van Brock, and the education she had been getting in camps became an education in stone. She saw the difference between a wall that was merely high and a wall whose flanking towers could sweep every inch of its ditch; she learned why the old round keep was death in the age of guns and why the new low angle bastions were the future; she put her hand on a siege crack in the northwest tower and felt, through the stone, the slow patient patience of artillery.

"The French have eight bombards with the vanguard," Valvert said. "My scouts counted them on the kingdom road. At that rate they'd need six weeks to breach, and they know it. They won't besiege me properly while your field army exists. They'll try to make you fight a battle to save me, on ground they choose. That's the whole of their plan."

"Then we will be so obliging as to refuse it." Thiébaut leaned on the parapet, looking south toward the smoke of the French pickets. "We reinforce, we provision, we make the rock unassailable, and we keep the field army mobile. Graçay can't march past a full fortress to his front; his supply line runs under these guns. So he sits. And while he sits, his master pays for an army in the field, and the Regent's treasury is not bottomless. This war is a clock. We are the slower clock."

Jeanne looked from the fortress to the green hills and the distant smoke, and felt the campaign settle into shape in her mind the way a ledger did when the columns began to agree. "Then our work is to make the clock expensive for him. Raid his convoys. Keep his scouts blind. Make every day in the field cost a week's pay."

"Exactly." He turned to her with something like surprise, as though he had forgotten she had not been doing this all her life. "You should have been a soldier."

"I am a soldier. I hold the treasury that keeps you one."

* * *

The gunnery lesson happened the next morning, on the practice range behind the camp, because she had asked for it plainly and Thiébaut had learned that when she asked for a thing plainly she meant to have it.

Hautemer himself laid out the instruments: the gunner's quadrant, the powder measures, the sheaf of range tables worked out in Lorraine and copied in the chancellery hand. _Marguerite_ sat on her low carriage with her muzzle elevated toward a butt of packed earth two hundred paces off, and the gunners moved around her with the unhurried swagger of men who owned a rare and dangerous craft.

"A gun is a contract," Thiébaut said. "So much powder, so much elevation, so much weight of stone, equals a predictable result. The poets write about swords. Wars are decided by clerks who can do sums in the rain." He showed her the powder scoop. "The first mistake of amateurs is courage. They overload to make a bigger noise, crack the breech, and kill their own crew. The measure is the measure. Now. The quadrant."

She knelt in the mud in her jerkin and learned to read the plumb line against the graduated arc, and then the range table, the columns of figures that translated distance into elevation, and then the strange, satisfying logic of it: a problem in geometry with a city wall at the end. When the crew had loaded and primed, he stepped back and offered her the linstock himself, the slow match smoking at its end.

"Your gun, madame. Touch hole. Don't flinch; flinching pulls the match wide."

She took it. The crew had their hands over their ears, and she made herself breathe out the way he had taught her on the archery range of her childhood, and touched the match to the priming.

The world became noise and smoke and a deep, chest-filling concussion. The stone shot went away in a long flat arc and struck the butt low and to the right, throwing earth in a fountain, and the crew cheered, partly for the duchess and partly because the gun had not exploded, and Thiébaut said, "Right by two paces, long by none. A gunner is born. Do it again, and correct."

She did it again, and again, and the third shot struck within a pace of the marker, and by then she had powder on her face and smoke in her hair and a grin she could not have suppressed with both hands, and when she turned he was watching her with an expression she had not seen before, not council and not strategy, and the look lasted only a moment before he nodded to Hautemer and said, "Mark her ladyship down as qualified. We'll make her load next time; the nobility of war consists mostly of carrying things."

That night at the campfire, with the stars out over the frontier hills and the sentries calling the watch in the dark, the lessons turned into stories. Jean, full of the army's rough wine, told the tale of the Sarrebourg relief, the fight that had given Thiébaut his shoulder, and acted out the parts with a stick for a lance while the headquarters circle laughed.

"It was winter, the snow to the horse's belly, and my brother here decides—"

"I decided nothing. The garrison had four days' bread."

"—decides to charge an entire Burgundian company with forty lances because they were warm and he was cold." Jean struck a pose. "The lance broke on the first man, the horse went down over the second, and he lay in the snow for an hour pretending to be dead while the battle walked around him. A man-at-arms finally recognized his shield and pulled him out, and the first thing he said was, 'Did we win?' because his shoulder was pointing the wrong way and his priorities were flawless."

"We won," Thiébaut said. "The shoulder was put back by a farrier. I recommend against the experience."

Jeanne looked at the left shoulder, invisible under the leather, and thought of the nights in the great bed when the cold woke it and he never complained, only changed his position in silence. "Why forty lances? Why not wait for the rest?"

"Because the arithmetic said the garrison fell on the fifth day, and the rest arrived on the sixth." He poked the fire with a stick. "There's a notion that command is about choosing the good option. It's usually about choosing which disaster you can afford. I could afford forty dead men. I couldn't afford the fortress."

She was quiet a while, turning her cup. The firelight moved on the faces around them, the easy scarred faces of men who had made these choices and slept after them. "You never told me. In all the nights at the Haut-Tertre, you never told me how you got it."

"You never asked."

"I'm asking now."

He considered the fire. "My father died when I was eleven. Lorraine was run by regents who stole the revenue and called it administration. I came home from your father's court at twenty with a title, three fortresses that were still loyal, and no money. The first winter I ate in the garrison hall because the palace had no cook. I learned the shoulder and everything else because there was no one else. That's the whole story. No sagas. A series of small, cold, expensive decisions, most of which I made badly the first time."

It was the closest he had ever come to giving her the seven missing years, and she took it as it was offered, without pressing toward the locked country beneath it. Somewhere beyond the camp a trumpet sounded the last watch, and the fire settled, and Jean took himself off to his tent with a parting yawn, and they sat alone together in the embers' light, two people in a hostile country at the edge of a war, and for a little while neither of them needed to say anything at all.

* * *

She held her first field court on the third evening, under an oak at the camp's edge, because the petitioners had followed the army for two days and the sergeant of the guard had run out of reasons to refuse them. A field court was not the chancellery; there was no bench, no usher, no Latin, only a folding chair, a clerk with a board, and the long light of a frontier sunset. Thiébaut sat his horse twenty paces off and did not approach, which she noted and approved.

The cases were the raw material of rule, small and ancient. A miller from a burned village sued a sutler for the price of a stolen donkey. Two widows disputed a field boundary and produced dead husbands' contradictory charters, which Foulques would have loved and she had to untangle by asking who had paid the tithe. A boy of fourteen, caught with a stolen dagger from a Lorraine tent, knelt in the grass white as milk while his mother offered everything she owned, which was a goose and two silver gros.

"The theft of a soldier's weapon in wartime is a hanging matter," the provost said, with the satisfaction of men who know the rule.

"So it is." She looked at the boy, who was shaking. "And if I hang him, his mother becomes a charge on the parish, and the army loses a strong pair of hands. Provost, the next levy needs drummer boys and latrine diggers and boys who can carry powder. Take him into the pioneer company, under the master of works, at a soldier's half pay. If he runs, you hang him then, and I'll sign the warrant. If he serves the campaign out, the theft is struck from the record." She turned to the mother. "The goose goes to the company mess. The two gros are the court fee. Go and make him a bundle; he marches at dawn."

The woman's thanks followed her back to the pavilion, loud and incoherent, and Thiébaut rode up alongside as though by accident. "The provost thinks you're soft."

"The provost may think whatever he likes. A hanging would cost the army a worker and the parish a pauper's keep, and buy me three hours of fear. The boy's service buys me the same fear, a pair of hands, and a story that travels faster than any proclamation." She glanced at him. "Don't tell me you've never done the same."

"I once made a sheep thief into a scout. He's now a sergeant, and steals only from the French." His mouth twitched. "I was beginning to think you kept all your mercy in the privy purse, the way you keep your pensions."

"Mercy is a coin like any other. It has to be budgeted."

That night, for the first time on campaign, the two retinues ate at one fire rather than two. It happened without an order, the way the important things usually did: Jean carrying his cup to the Vauréal side, Brive's captains answering in the rough French of the camps, the soldiers' wives sharing out a stew between the two horse lines. She watched it happen over her own portion, and said nothing, and marked it down among the campaign's victories, which were never the ones the songs chose.

* * *

The French came at the misty hour before dawn, the hour they had chosen because every sensible man in every war is at his stupidest then. Jeanne was woken not by a trumpet but by the camp itself changing sound: the sentries' calls shortening, the horses picking it up, the whole animal machinery of an army coming alert before the human mind had caught up. She was dressed and armed with her dagger before Lisette got the taper lit, and outside the pavilion the tents were already coming down in the grey fog, the companies forming by beat of drum rather than voice.

Thiébaut was already mounted at the center, fully armed, his breath pluming, his captains around him receiving their orders in short sentences. She saw the method of him now, the same method as the march: no shouting, no heroics, a place for everyone and everyone put into it before the fog showed the enemy's numbers. The vanguard to the river bend. The pikes across the road. The guns unlimbering on the left rise, where the mist would thin last. The baggage and the pennons pulled back behind the hill.

"Madame." He turned the horse to her, and in the half-light his face was all business, and she was grateful for it. "It's a probe, not an attack. Two or three hundred gascons and light horse, testing our pickets at the south ford. I don't intend a battle; I intend them to go home with correct information. You'll take the standard to the windmill on the rise with the household guard, where you can see the field. If the mist closes, stay there; the guard has its orders. Don't come down."

"And if it goes against us?"

"It won't. But if it does, you ride north with the chancellery and the seal, and you don't look back, because a duchy without a duchess can still fight and a duchy with a captured one cannot." He said it without heat, a piece of logistics, and then, lower, "Also I would prefer you whole. Both reasons."

"Both received." She wheeled the mare toward the rise. "Bring me prisoners. I want their paybooks."

The windmill was a black skeleton against the lightening sky, and from its rise she could see the whole theater: the river shining in loops through the fog, the south ford where the French were crossing in disordered files, the camp's campfires now abandoned, and the dark ordered blocks of her army waiting in the mist like reefs under water. Brive joined her with the standard guard, armed cap-à-pie on a great bay, and gave her a look of approval for being on the rise rather than in the pavilion, and said nothing.

The French came through the fog at a trot, gascon infantry with their white crosses and a screen of light horse ahead, and they met the Lorraine pickets at the ford and pushed them back exactly as Thiébaut had predicted they would, and then they came up the road in the confidence of men chasing a rear guard, and the fog thinned over the left rise, and the guns were there.

She had fired _Marguerite_ herself on the practice range. She had not heard a battery open in war. The difference was the difference between a door slamming and a house falling: a rolling, continuous thunder, the guns firing by platoons down the road, and the road dissolving into smoke and screaming horses and bodies thrown sideways by the stone shot. The French light horse broke first, as cavalry will against guns it cannot reach. The gascons, better men, tried to spread off the road into the meadows, and found the pikemen there, the long Swiss weapons lowered in a hedge, and the Lorraine lances coming around the flank in a controlled canter, not a wild charge, her husband's hand visible in every careful piece of it.

It was over in the time it takes a fog to burn off a field. By full sun the French were running for the ford in groups, the lances herding rather than slaughtering, because prisoners and information were worth more than corpses and Thiébaut had said so in his orders. The guns fired one more round at the last formed company and then fell silent, and the smoke drifted over the trampled meadow and the dead and the small miserable sounds of the wounded.

Brive let out a long breath. "Textbook. Your husband, madame, does not make exciting war."

"Good. I am not paying for exciting."

* * *

They brought the prisoners to the command tent by midday, forty-one of them, bound and gray-faced, and the paybooks and letters found on the sergeants were laid on the folding table still wet with river water. Thiébaut interrogated the officers himself, briefly, with food in front of them and no threats, because hunger and courtesy had extracted more information in his experience than cords. Jeanne sat to one side with Foulques, taking her own notes, and watched the method again: the quiet voice, the unhurried questions, the way he let a man talk himself into telling the truth because silence began to feel worse than speech.

The news matched Valvert's estimate. Graçay had eight bombards and perhaps four thousand effectives, with the forfeit fiefs being digested by garrisons rather than field troops. The probe's work had been to count the Vauréal army and, if possible, take a prisoner who knew whether the duchess was with it.

"Which they now know," Foulques murmured.

"Which I wanted them to know." Thiébaut stripped off his gauntlet. "A duchess in the field with her army and her guns is a duchess whose vassals get the correct story. Let Graçay hear she watched his probe break. He'll move slower. He's a man who respects evidence."

The last prisoner was a young French gentleman, an ensign, wounded in the arm and doing his best to be brave. He had a letter on him, not military, from his mother in Orléans, and when Thiébaut saw it he pushed it back across the table. "Keep that. The sergeant will dress your arm. You're paroled to the surgeons' tent; give me your word not to leave it, and I'll take no bond."

The boy stared. "My word, my lord?"

"You're an ensign. Your word is all you own that's worth anything." A pause. "And tell the Sire de Graçay, when you're exchanged, that the duchess's gunnery improved his road. He'll understand."

When the tent had cleared, Jeanne said, "You were gentle with the boy."

"I was an ensign once, in another country's war. Someone was gentle with me, and I've never forgotten it, and I've never been able to find out who." He sat down across from her and rubbed the bad shoulder, which the morning's armor had woken. "Also gentleness to the captured is a payment into a fund I may need myself. The day I'm taken, I want it on record how I behaved."

"You don't believe you'll win, then. Not truly."

"I believe we'll win this one. I don't believe any soldier is ever truly safe, and I'd be a fool to act like it." He looked at her across the maps. "Jeanne. You stayed on the rise. You didn't come down. You watched the road take the guns without making a sound. Brive says you didn't even blanch."

"Brive flatters. I shut my eyes during the first volley, behind the standard, where no one could see."

"That's the second-best thing a commander can do. The first is not letting anyone know." He almost smiled. "The army will have its own version by evening. In the army's version, you directed the battery."

"Let it stand. Rumor is cheap, and I have a press now." She rose, and the long morning moved in her bones, the fog and the thunder and the prisoners and the wounded, and she found she was not tired, or rather that the tiredness was the good kind, the kind of a day that had earned its night. "My lord. The dispatches. Sainterre needs the story by the weekly sheet, the Haut-Tertre needs the casualty lists, and the commandant at Mont d'Or needs to know the French just lost their best chance to count us cheaply. Then we eat. Then we sleep. Tomorrow we make Graçay's clock more expensive."

He stood with her, and for a moment, in the tent's dim light, the council faces fell away and there were only the two of them, muddy and smoke-smelling and bound by paper and by something newer that neither had named. "Tomorrow," he said, "and every day after, until they price this war beyond Paris's purse."

Outside, the army was burying its dead in the captured meadow, and the detail fired a volley over the graves, and the sound rolled across the river toward the French hills like a message, which was exactly what it was.
