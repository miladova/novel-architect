# Chapter Twenty-Five — The Peace

Peace, Jeanne discovered, was a war with different ledgers and the same hours.

The armies went home, and the work that remained was the work no ballad celebrated: roofs and roads and the long repair of everything the war had eaten. The treasury's peacetime accounts came to her table in the same hand as the wartime ones, and the columns were even more unforgiving, because there was no longer an emergency to excuse them. Ninety-four thousand écus to the Venetian house and its partners, the annuities running out six years ahead, the war taxes voted for three more, and against them the reopened river trade, the Sainterre customs rising month by month, the Grisay iron flowing again to the forges and the foundries. Dolfin, reviewing the first peacetime quarter with her over spiced wine, pronounced the patient in excellent health for a patient who had technically been at death's door two winters running, and raised no objection when she redeemed the first of the pledged revenues at par, as the contract allowed.

"You're the only client I have who reads the redemption clauses," he said.

"I'm the only client you have who was raised by clerks. The others were raised by tutors."

She made the spring circuit herself, as soon as the roads would carry the wagons, heavy with the child and tired by every afternoon, and the journey became a second, slower kind of campaign. At Mont d'Or she inspected the new angle bastions and the battery on the west spur, the work that had killed Hautemer and learned his lessons afterward, and Rostaing paraded the garrison for her with the clerk's efficiency she had once punished him for lacking, and said nothing at all about the old days except, when she asked, that the men's boots were good and the well was full. At the rebuilt villages of the southeastern marches she sat in new cottages and ate the bread of women whose names were in the pension books, and at Sainte-Florence she opened the hospital of twenty beds and saw the looms running again, four hundred of them, with the twelve-year-old's name carved over the gate beside the charter's date.

Sainterre was the great stop. The city had rebuilt the eastern quarter wider than before, with straight streets and a proper water conduit the aldermen had voted themselves, and the cloth hall stood whole again with its glass replaced. The new council, elected under the charter's law without a riot or a disputed return, met her in the guild regalia, and Coppens, old and greyer but unbowed, presented the city's first peacetime budget in a speech of such length that even the aldermen began to look at their shoes. The charter was re-sealed in the square where de Key's broadsheets had once blown about, with the Treaty of Heurtois's recognition clause read aloud beside it, so that every person there could hear the king of France himself enrolled among the guarantors of their liberties.

"Ten years from now," Coppens said, walking her through the rebuilt weavers' quarter, "they'll say the charter came down from some old duke in grandfather's time. No one will remember the siege."

"They'll remember the bread price stayed fixed. That's the part that matters." She stopped at the corner where the first stone had struck. "Keep the news sheet going. Cities that read their own accounts don't lose them."

Verhoeven's press, she noted, had sprouted two competitors, and the licensing court was busy, and she counted that a victory too.

* * *

Back at the Haut-Tertre, the last weeks of the pregnancy became the court's whole occupation, whether she permitted it or not. Oriabel had the lying-in chamber ready by Easter, with the tapestries chosen to calm the mind and the midwife installed in the palace like a visiting general. Lisette ran the intelligence service from a stool beside the bed. Claire came with swaddling cloths and the hard-won knowledge of women who had lost a child, and Sister Margriet traveled from Sainterre at the duchess's own expense and took command of everything medical with a serenity that reduced the court physicians to fetching water.

Thiébaut, who had faced gascons and bombards with perfect composure, was frankly useless and knew it, and spent the time in a flurry of displaced energy: inspecting every guard rotation twice, drafting pointless memoranda on the Lorraine roads, and once, caught in the corridor by Lisette, asking whether the baby would know him if he spent too much of the year at Nancy.

"She'll know your step," Lisette said, "if you'll stop wearing out the floor. Sit down."

The labor began at dusk on the twenty-eighth of April, in a thunderstorm, which everyone over thirty remembered was the weather that attended the great events of the duchess's life, and the midwife and Sister Margriet closed the chamber doors and the court settled in for the ancient, powerless vigil of men in corridors. It was long, and hard, and the child came near dawn, and the cry that went up from the chamber was answered by the bells of the cathedral and then the bells of the lower town, tower after tower, rolling across the river the way the mourning bells had rolled two years before, in a different key.

It was a girl. Strong, red, furious, with a fine fuzz of dark hair and lungs of remarkable authority. Sister Margriet laid her on Jeanne's chest in the first quiet, and Jeanne looked down at the small, furious stranger and thought, with the clarity of exhaustion: this is what all the ledgers were for.

"Her name," she said, when they brought Thiébaut in, white-faced and trying not to show it, "is Aveline. For my mother. The rest of her names can be settled by the heralds, but the first one is mine."

He sat on the edge of the bed with the care of a man handling ordinance, and looked at his daughter the way he looked at a new gun: with awe, arithmetic, and a private terror. "She has your temper."

"She has nothing yet but opinions. That will be called the ducal temperament." Jeanne shifted against the pillows. "The succession is secure in the female line, the charter law covers it, and the treaty's reversion clauses name her inheritance. I had Foulques check everything twice. She will inherit everything, in her own right, and no one will ever call her a ward."

"No wardships. No locked rooms." He said it like a vow, which it was, the vow they had made in the cloth hall at Sainterre. "I remember."

* * *

She was up too soon, of course; the court said so, the midwife said so, even Thiébaut said so, and she ignored all of them in the measured way she ignored advice she had already discounted, and on the tenth day she had the green case brought to her father's old chamber, with Foulques and Thiébaut and the fire.

The chancellor, thin and careful and restored to his papers, had carried the case like a reliquary. "The sealed full account is in the private archive, madame, as you ordered. These are the originals: the renunciation, the convent order, the threats. What would you have done with them?"

"Open the case. Leave us."

He left, and she sat with Thiébaut by the open window where the spring air came in off the river, and she went through the documents one last time, the handwriting of two dead men and a living one, the machinery of a girl's imprisonment. The renunciation she read in full, the words she had carried like knives for seven years, and they looked different now on the parchment: young, fierce, driven into the paper by a hand that had been shaking.

"He wept signing this. Foulques told me, and I didn't believe it for a long time. I believe it now."

"He did." Thiébaut's voice was quiet. "I have never not believed it, which was the particular difficulty."

She fed the renunciation to the fire first, and then the convent order with its blank date, and then the list of threats, and the three documents curled and blackened and lifted up the chimney in fragments of ash and light. The copies stayed in the archive, because the state needed its memory; the originals, the instruments themselves, she burned, because the people did not.

"Foulques's account remains sealed," she said. "For the historians. I won't have my father remembered as a villain in a pamphlet, and I won't have you remembered as a villain at all. The whole truth, with all its names, for whoever governs after us. The instruments, never again."

He watched the last of the paper go. "And the girl? The fifteen-year-old. What becomes of her?"

"She becomes me. That was always the only possible outcome. She just didn't know it yet." Jeanne closed the empty green case and set it aside. "I thought for years that love was the thing that made you weak, because the first time I felt it, it was used against me. I've had to learn it's the other way. The weak thing was the silence. Not the loving."

It was the closest she had ever come to the word, and he heard it as the word it was, and did not press for more, because he had learned her grammar in the two years of war. He put his hand over hers where it lay on the table, and they sat like that while the fire ate the past and the town went about its morning below the walls.

* * *

She went down to the cathedral vault on a soft May morning, with Aveline carried by Lisette behind her and Thiébaut at her side, and stood between the two sarcophagi, her mother's small one and her father's great one, and had the conversation she had been preparing for two years.

"I used to think you were the standard." She spoke low, in the way one speaks to the dead, who have all the time in the world. "Everything measured against the high chair and the ledgers. I've kept the ledgers. I've even kept the chair. But I've changed what gets written in them. You recorded victories. I record names. You built a duchy out of marriages and debts and the careful ruin of your neighbors. I've built one out of charters and banks and the city voting its own taxes. I don't know yet which one lasts longer. I know which one I'd rather be ruled by."

The vault was quiet. Somewhere above, a bell began, and the sound came down through the stone like water.

"You were wrong about him. I want that on the record, even though the record here is just me. He was the best instrument you ever bought and you never knew it, because you thought people were the same as castles, things to be taken and held. He wasn't. Neither was I. The convent order is ash now. I burned it myself. You can keep whatever you think of that; you're dead, and the living get the last word, which is the one rule even you couldn't get around."

Thiébaut said nothing, and Lisette said nothing, and the baby made a small, speculative noise, and Jeanne put out her hand and touched the cold carved face of the sarcophagus the way she had touched the ransomed hand in the first week of the reign.

"Thank you for the Latin and the siege manuals. I'll see that Aveline gets them too, and a great many other things you never thought to teach a girl. Rest. The duchy is in better order than you left it, and that's the only elegy you ever wanted."

They climbed the stairs into the May light, and she did not look back, and the vault door was closed behind her with the sound of a long account finally settled.

* * *

The summer settled into the rhythm of a reign that had survived its founding crisis and was becoming, by slow degrees, ordinary. The council met at the old hours. Foulques's chancellery turned the emergency machinery into standing law. The news sheet came out every week from three licensed presses. The orphans' school for girls, named for the late duchess, enrolled its second forty, and the bishop's objections were met with the published examination results and faded of their own accord. The Guild of Saint Margaret drilled on Sundays after church, and small girls in three cities announced their intention to grow up to carry crossbows, and were generally indulged.

She visited the orphans' school in June, with Aveline carried before her on a cushion and the whole court in train, because Foulques had asked her to examine the girls in person and publish the results, and she had learned long since that her chancellor's requests were usually commands wearing a veil.

The school occupied the old pilgrim hospital by the eastern gate, a long, light building that smelled of chalk and wax and the herbs the sisters strewed against fever, and forty girls rose when she entered, in their plain wool gowns, daughters of dead soldiers and burned villages and the siege of Sainterre, curtsying with the precise gravity of people who understood exactly what the place had cost. The eldest, a sharp-featured child of thirteen whose father had died on the wall, read a passage of Cicero and then translated it into the vernacular without stumbling, and a girl of ten did sums on the slate board that would have shamed half the treasury clerks, and Foulques, thin and upright at the mistress's side, wore the face of a man watching a long investment mature.

"They're better at the Latin than the boys' school," Jeanne said to him afterwards, in the garden where the children could not hear.

"They're better at everything, because no one ever told them they were allowed to be bad at it." The chancellor folded his hands. "Madame, I have a request of a different kind. I am sixty-three years old, and the heart has its own calendar. I should like, while I can still teach, to spend two days a week here myself. The chancellery can spare me; Colard is ready, and the archives are in order. Let me be the one who teaches them the law. A girl who knows the law is a girl no man can decide things for, in the words you used when you sentenced me."

"You remembered."

"I remember everything. It is my affliction and my trade."

She looked across the garden at the children at their games, the noise of them rising in the June air, and thought of the convent order with the blank date that had once waited for a girl of fifteen. "Two days. And the school gets a new wing, in the name of the chancellor, since you won't permit it in mine. That's a ducal command; don't argue, I've seen what your arguments cost."

He bowed, and for the first time in her memory the bow was not a courtier's instrument but a man's.

Dolfin went back to Venice for the first time in years and left a factor in his place, with a letter for her that said, in essence, that he had made more money and had more interesting conversations in her dominion than anywhere in his professional life, and that his house's credit would remain open against all reasonable and most unreasonable events. Jean the Bastard, healed of everything except his opinions, went home to Lorraine to keep its roads, and wrote long, scandalous letters that arrived by every post and were read aloud at supper to the court's delight. Claire de Sombernon recovered her manor in the final peace and ran it from a distance with the ferocity of a woman who had decided never to be a refugee twice. Perrin got his spurs at a ceremony in the courtyard, very pale and very proud, and was attached to the duchess's own household, where his competence with cups became a byword.

And the two of them, the duchess and the duke, settled the architecture of a marriage that had begun as a treaty and become, through the ordinary chemistry of shared danger and kept promises, something the court had no documents for. He spent his seasons between Nancy and the Haut-Tertre, and the roads between the two capitals grew safe and busy and rich, and wherever he was, the letters went back and forth, the state ones in the clerks' hands and the private ones in their own, and the private ones grew longer and less guarded and, in places, foolish, which was perhaps the surest sign.

She kept the connecting door open always now. The key hung on its chain in the jewel casket, a museum piece she showed Aveline once when the girl was old enough to ask what keys were for, and explained, in the simplified version mothers use, that some doors exist to be found open.

The child ruled the nursery with the same absolute opinions she had displayed at birth, and the palace adjusted itself around her the way it had once adjusted around the war. Jeanne nursed her for three months, against the court's advice and with Sister Margriet's backing, and sat through the audiences of that season with her daughter at her breast and the envoys carefully studying the tapestries, and no one ever quite found the protocol to object. Thiébaut, home from a summer on the Lorraine roads, walked the floor with the baby at the colicky hour and sang her the old Aubecourt songs, the ones from the gallery and the frozen canal, badly and without shame, and Jeanne, pretending to read in the next room, learned them again through his voice.

"She knows you," Lisette said, watching the small fist close around his finger. "They always know the voice that was there in the dark."

"She knows the accounts better already. She screams whenever Colard visits." Jeanne set the letter down. "What she will need, when she's older, is everything we weren't given. Truths delivered on time. A mother who doesn't keep a casket of unsent letters. A father who has never once renounced her in a hall."

Thiébaut looked up from the baby's face, and the old thing passed between them, the memory that no longer wounded but had not vanished, and would never quite vanish, which was correct; wounds that healed without a mark had taught nothing. "She'll have it," he said. "We wrote the charter for a city. We can keep one for a daughter."

They had Aveline christened in the cathedral on a bright July day, with Brive as her godfather and Claire as her godmother and the whole court in its best, and Coppens came up from Sainterre with a gift of the first cloth woven in the rebuilt eastern quarter, and the bells that had announced her birth rang again, and the lower town got a holiday and a double ration and a story it would tell for a generation: the duchess's daughter, born in a peace her mother had built invoice by invoice.

* * *

The court dined together that evening, the whole strange household the war had made, and Jeanne sat at the high table with her daughter asleep against her shoulder and watched them all the way she watched the ledgers, counting what had survived.

Bride, older and greyer and back on a horse against every physician, was telling the young officers the story of the ford probe for the dozenth time, improving the French numbers as was his right. Jean, stiff-shouldered and incorrigible, had the far end of the table in tears over the Harchies bandits and the brandy-smuggling days. Claire and Oriabel ran the household between them with the smooth ferocity of women who had discovered they agreed about everything that mattered. Perrin, spurred and solemn, sat his first high-table place and blushed when the company drank his health for the cup of wine. Foulques ate little and watched the orphans' school accounts under the table, because he could not help it. Verhoeven's papers, Dolfin's factors, Rostaing's dispatches from the southern rock, Coppens's interminable letters, the Guild of Saint Margaret's Sunday drills: the invoices of a reign.

Thiébaut leaned close enough that only she heard. "What's the ledger showing?"

"That the most expensive thing in the world is other people, and the only thing that holds a country together." She shifted the sleeping child against her shoulder. "I began with eleven riders in a wet courtyard. I thought rule was a high chair and a seal."

"It isn't?"

"It's a table with room enough for everyone you can't afford to lose." She looked at him, at the grey at his temple and the stiff left arm and the hazel eyes that had watched her from a gatehouse window two years before. "Keep your chair close to mine. That's a standing order."

"It has been since the wedding. You just didn't know you'd issued it."

The hall went on with its noise, and the baby slept through all of it, and outside the summer dusk came down over the river and the walls and the long, repaired, imperfect, peaceful country she had been given in a cart and meant to hand on in a charter.

* * *

On the last night of September, she sat late at the table in her father's chamber, the same table at which she had planned the funeral and the marriage and the war in the first black week of the reign. The ledgers were open, the day's dispatches answered, the child asleep in the next room with Lisette outside the door, and through the open window the autumn air came in off the river, and the bells marked the hour in their long, patient conversation.

She was writing the year's summary, the private one in the hand no clerk copied, and the page was full: the peace, the debt and its redemption schedule, the charters and the schools, the garrisons and the roads, the names of the dead in their book, the names of the living in their offices, the whole complicated estate of a small rich country that had refused to be anyone's easy meal. The fire burned low. The candles stood at their marks.

The door opened without ceremony, and Thiébaut came in from the council side, in his shirtsleeves, with a cup of wine for her and the shoulder held in the slight stiff way it kept in cold weather, and he set the cup down and drew the second chair close to the table, as he had done a hundred times, and reached for the second quill.

"The Mont d'Or returns?" he asked. "I have the figures from Rostaing in my pocket."

"Under the stoneworks line. And don't tell me your shoulder is fine; I can see how you're holding it. Sister Margriet left a liniment; use it or I'll have her write you a lecture."

"Yes, madame." He dipped the quill, and for a while there was nothing in the room but the sound of two pens on the paper and the river outside and the bells, and the long, unremarkable happiness of a government that did not need to raise its voice.

Once, near the end of the second page, she paused and looked up, and found him looking at her, the hazel eyes greenish in the candlelight, the same eyes from the solar and the great hall and a hundred battlefields of paper and powder, and he said, "What is it?"

"Nothing. I was remembering the first night. The gate bell, and the eleven riders, and the new ledger. I thought it was the beginning of a siege."

"It was." He bent back to the page. "This one just has better terms."

She laughed, low, and turned to the book again, and wrote the last entry of the day in her own hand, and below it, in the margin where only the two of them ever looked, she drew the small joined mark they had begun using for the things that belonged to neither ledger alone: the two arms, Vauréal and Lorraine, intertwined. Then she sanded the page and closed the cover, and the two quills went back into the same cup, and they sat a while longer over the cooling wine, not speaking, while the fire settled and the bells of the peacetime city struck the hour across the dark, slow river.
