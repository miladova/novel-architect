# Chapter Thirteen — The Preacher's Fire

Grisay announced itself before it was seen. The iron hills were red with it, a rust-colored country of mines and charcoal stacks and muddy roads churned by ore carts, and the sky above the valley wore a permanent brown haze through which the winter sun hung like a worn coin. The smell came next, cinders and sulfur and the hot-metal reek of a hundred forges, and then the sound, the deep rhythmic hammering that gave the city its pulse and never entirely stopped, day or night, weekday or feast.

Jeanne came down the valley road with two hundred lances, a deliberately small force, and Thiébaut's army a full day's march behind at the river crossing, according to the plan. The city's charter aldermen met her at the gate in their new red gowns, and their relief was so poorly concealed that it bordered on insult.

"Madame." The senior alderman, a forge master named Bertout, kissed her ring with a sweating face. "The city is yours. It is merely, at present, of several minds about it."

"Then show me the minds." She dismounted in the courtyard of the ducal house that served Grisay as a hall of justice. "First the dead man, then the preacher, then the forges. In that order."

The dead man was a clerk of the chancellery, young, twenty-three, sent out in the autumn to audit the iron tithe. His name was Rémy, and his body lay in the chapel of Saint-Eloi, the smiths' church, preserved through the cold in a lead-lined coffin and watched by his fellow clerks in shifts, as though the watching could keep the manner of his death politically alive. The aldermen's account, stammered out on the road, was this: Rémy had been cornered in the tithe yard by a crowd stirred from Anselm's sermon on the damnation of tax-gatherers, chased into the church porch, and beaten to death with hammers and a piece of paving stone. No one had seen anything. Everyone had been there.

"Thirty witnesses and no culprit," Jeanne said, looking at the coffin. "That is not an investigation, Bertout. That is a city hiding."

"Madame, the mood—"

"The mood is my business. Yours is the law. Open the coffin."

They opened it. She had seen worse by now, the siege surgeons had seen to that, but she made herself look properly, the way she had taught herself to look at the ledgers she did not want to read. The boy's skull was crushed in three places. His hands were broken, raised in defense. On his thumb was an ink stain that would not wash off, the mark of his trade.

"Thirty people watched this," she said, more to herself than to the aldermen. "Thirty people who go to confession and take the sacrament. What is he preaching, exactly?"

* * *

Anselm preached in the great square of the iron market, because the churches had begun refusing him their pulpits and the square suited him better in any case. He was forty, lean to the point of emaciation, barefoot in the January frost in the Franciscan manner, with a face made for high balconies: deep-set burning eyes, a prophet's brow, and the voice of a man who could fill a square without effort. Jeanne went to hear him on her second morning, plainly dressed in a dark gown with a plain wool veil, Lisette at her side and twelve guards in ordinary cloaks spread through the crowd.

He was magnificent, which was the trouble. He preached on the rich man and the eye of the needle, and he preached it with a craftsman's knowledge of his audience's injuries: the tithe that took a tenth of the smith's profit, the toll that ate the carter's margin, the war taxes that came while the duke's soldiers ate the countryside. He named none of it wrong, Jeanne thought, standing among the smells of cinders and unwashed wool. That was his genius. Every grievance was real. What he did with the real grievances was the fraud: he stacked them into a staircase that led only upward, to the conclusion that all authority was demonic, all priests corrupt, all taxes the mark of the Beast, and the only Christian life the one that burned the account books and hammered the plowshares out of the guns.

"_Two masters_!" he cried, and the crowd answered him in groans. "_No man can serve two masters, and yet they make you serve the duchess's tax and God's tithe together, and call it peace! The Christ had no ledger! The apostles kept no toll! What fellowship has the hammer of the faithful with the cannon of the princes?_"

He saw her then. She had known he would; a preacher of his quality watches every rich cloak in his crowd, and her guards, plain as they tried to be, had the set of soldiers. His eyes locked onto hers across the square, and without a heartbeat's pause he bent the sermon toward her like a river finding a new channel.

"_And here is the age in little! A woman who rules what God gave to men, a Jezebel in a veil, come up from her painted palace to count the iron of the poor! Let her answer if she can: by what scripture does a woman hold the rod of the princes? By what right does she tax the bread of the children of the forge?_"

The crowd turned, a slow collective movement, four hundred faces swinging onto her, the hostility and the curiosity and the ancient, dangerous joy of a crowd that has been handed a victim. Lisette's hand found her sleeve. Under their cloaks the guards' hands moved.

Jeanne let the silence lengthen until it became uncomfortable, and then she did the thing she had planned in the cold logic of the night before: she walked forward, through the crowd, to the foot of the preaching cross, where everyone could see that she was not afraid and that she was alone, and she raised her voice without shouting, the trick of the Aubecourt square.

"Brother Anselm. I will answer, since you ask in public and the question deserves a public answer. Scripture first, since you chose it. Deborah judged Israel, and the book of Judges calls her a mother in Israel, and no scribe thought to strike the verse. Huldah prophesied to the king, and the king obeyed her. The queen of Sheba tested Solomon's wisdom and was praised for the journey. If you know a text that forbids a woman the rod, bring it to the scholars of Heurtois and dispute it; I will pay for the debate. But do not answer a text with a shout. That is what the heathens do."

The crowd's sound changed, the shifting noise of people who had been offered an argument instead of a spectacle and were not sure what to do with it. Anselm recovered first. "Deborah did not tax the forges! A woman's rule may be suffered as a scourge, but it is not a grace—"

"Now the tax. You say the apostles kept no toll. They also kept no forges, no walls, and no army, and the day the French come over your hills you will discover why. What does the tax buy, Brother? Ask the smiths of Saint-Vaast, whose town was taken and whose houses were not sacked, because the army you despise crossed a river and died in a breach to take it back. Ask the widows of this city whose husbands' names are read in every church because I keep a book of them. You call the tax a mark of the Beast. I call it the price of the walls, and the walls are the reason you are allowed to stand in this square and call me Jezebel instead of preaching in a cellar to a French garrison."

A voice in the crowd, a woman's, called out that the tolls were still too high, and Jeanne turned toward it at once. "Then petition your aldermen under your charter, because the tolls are their court now, not mine. That is what the charter says, and I sealed it with my own hand. The preacher offers you heaven. I offer you a court that can lower a toll by Friday. Which of us has the better answer for a Tuesday?"

The laugh that went through the square was the thing that broke him, she thought later; crowds that laugh are no longer crowds, they become audiences again, and Anselm's power was the power over a crowd. His face twisted with the pure, sincere fury of the man who has lost a room, and he raised his arms for the closing denunciation, and Jeanne, who had not finished, spoke over him.

"One thing more. Rémy the clerk died in the porch of Saint-Eloi, beaten with hammers, while thirty of this city watched. Whatever you think of taxes, Brother, a boy who kept accounts was murdered at the door of a church. If your gospel has no room for his widow and his mother, then your gospel is not the one the apostles kept. I am here to see his death answered. I will be at the justice house tomorrow. Any man who saw what happened in that porch may come to me there, under my hand and seal, and no hand in this city will touch him for the telling."

She turned and walked back through the crowd with Lisette rigid at her side and the guards closing like a drawbridge, and behind her Anselm's voice rose again, but the square was arguing now in little groups, and argument, as Thiébaut had said, was the one thing a demagogue could not survive.

* * *

The trouble came from the side, the way trouble always does. They were halfway down the street of the armorers, the crowd thinning, when Lisette said, very quietly, "Three behind. The one with the hammer. They've been with us since the square."

Jeanne did not turn. "The guards?"

"Too far. They're closing the honest people between us on purpose." The maid's voice was steady; she had been carrying messages through courts since she was twelve. "Walk faster. The chandler's shop, ten paces—"

The rush came with a roar of drink and dogma, three men with their hoods up, one indeed carrying a smith's hammer, and the crowd shrieked and scattered. Jeanne had her dagger out by training rather than thought, but it was Lisette who did the useful thing: she seized the heavy iron shop sign beside the chandler's door and swung it, chain and all, into the foremost man's knees, and he went down in a clatter, and the second tripped over him, and the third was met by the guards, who arrived in a storm of cloaks and steel and took him into the gutter with a professional efficiency that ended the affair before the street had fully understood it.

"Are you hurt?" Jeanne gripped her maid's arm.

"My heart, my lady. Nothing else." Lisette was white but her eyes were blazing. "They meant you, not me. They meant it in the square and followed the chance."

The captured men were bound and marched to the justice house under a doubled guard, and by evening the interrogations had their answer: three fullers' journeymen, drunk on cider and Anselm, who had decided among themselves that killing the Jezebel would be a work of merit. They named no order from the preacher, because there had been none, and Jeanne believed them; the dangerous thing about Anselm was precisely that he did not need to give orders. He manufactured the weather, and other men committed the storms.

* * *

The witness came at dawn, as witnesses do when the night has been spent in argument with conscience. She was a washerwoman, middle-aged, with the red hands of her trade and a face set for trouble, and she asked for the duchess by name and would speak to no one else. Jeanne received her in the justice house with Foulques and the aldermen and the city's own magistrate, because the arrest she intended had to wear the city's law, not the duchess's will.

"I was in the porch," the woman said, staring at her own hands. "I come for the priests' laundry, and I was early. I saw it. The clerk ran from the tithe yard and into the porch, and the crowd after him, and Brother Anselm at the back of it, and he called out—" She stopped.

"Called what?" Foulques asked, gently for him.

"He called, 'Let the account books be answered in blood.' Or near it. My lady, I can't give every word. But he said it, and the crowd went up the steps like the words had loosed them. He didn't strike him. He never struck him. But he stood at the bottom and watched them do it, and when the boy was down he turned away and went across the square, and he didn't send for the surgeon or the watch."

The room was quiet. The magistrate, a careful man, wrote it all down and read it back, and the woman made her mark, and Jeanne had the mark witnessed by three aldermen.

"Your name is protected under my seal," she said. "If any man troubles you, the city answers to me. You have done a hard thing. Go with the guard; they'll see you home."

When she had gone, the argument began exactly where Jeanne had expected. The aldermen wanted the three journeymen tried and the preacher left alone, because arresting Anselm meant facing the fullers, and the fullers meant the streets. Foulques wanted an ecclesiastical process, slow and distant, which would turn him into a martyr without burning the city. Brive, by express courier from the army, wanted him hanged from the highest chimney in Grisay as an example.

"None of those," Jeanne said. "Listen to the law, because I have spent two nights with the city's charter and the statutes, and this is why we keep them. The city statute against incitement to murder: any person whose public words directly move a crowd to a killing, when the speaker remains present and neither prevents it nor raises the watch, is held as principal to the murder. It's an old statute. Your own aldermen passed it after the grain riots of 'seventy-six, and the crown confirmed it. We try Anselm in the city court, under the city's law, for the killing of Rémy the clerk. Not for his sermons. Not for heresy, which is the bishop's business and would make a saint of him. For a murder in a church porch, under a statute the city wrote for itself."

The magistrate's mouth opened and closed. "It has never been used against a man of the Church."

"It has never been needed. The statute says _person_. If the bishop claims jurisdiction, show him the porch: a secular clerk killed on secular ground in a secular riot. Rome can have an appeal after the city has had its verdict." She stood. "Arrest him this morning, in the square, by the city watch, in the city's name, with the aldermen present. My soldiers stay in their billets. If the fullers riot, the charter militia answers it, not the duke's army. This city is going to govern itself in public, and find out what that costs."

* * *

They took Anselm at the third hour, in the middle of his morning sermon, and the scene was stranger than anyone had feared and much less bloody. The watch, thirty tradesmen in the city's red and white, climbed the preaching cross while the crowd roared, and their captain, a master smith with arms like oak beams, read the statute aloud in a voice trained over furnaces, and the aldermen stood at the front in their gowns, and the whole thing was done under so much law that the fullers, who had expected soldiers and martyrdom, found themselves with nothing to charge. Anselm went white with the genuine fury of a man who had expected to be struck down and discovered instead that he was being processed, and he shouted prophecy all the way to the city gate, and the crowd watched him go, and did not move.

De Key vanished that night. Jeanne had half expected it; the weaver had been in Grisay since the autumn, moving between the discontented trades, and Lisette's people had his lodging watched. He was gone before dawn with two companions, down the southern road toward the French lines, and when the watch searched his rooms they found copies of Paris letters and a sum of gold écus that no weaver could have earned, and the letters were read aloud in the market square by the magistrate, and the city learned in one morning that its most radical voice had been on the Sire de Graçay's payroll. The fullers' agitation did not survive the news. Nothing dries up a street movement like the discovery that its leader was a foreign agent.

The trial ran three days. Jeanne attended but did not preside, sitting with the aldermen as the city's court did its work, and the washerwoman gave her evidence again, and the journeymen were tried first, so that the chain from words to killing was laid link by link before Anselm ever came to the bar. He defended himself with passion and some learning, and he was entirely sincere, and he was also, under the plain text of the statute, guilty. The court condemned him to death by hanging, the city's punishment for murder, and the bishop's furious claim of jurisdiction arrived a day too late and was answered with a copy of the statute and the record.

Jeanne commuted the sentence. It was the most discussed act of the winter. She did it in open court, in front of the same crowd that had heard him preach, and she gave her reasons aloud.

"Anselm is guilty under your law, and the sentence stands as the law's sentence. But a hanged preacher is a relic, and I will not give the discontented a grave to march to. The sentence is commuted to perpetual confinement in the ducal house of correction at Malemort, at hard labor, in silence, with books of devotion and no visitors. Let the man who told the poor that work was a curse spend his life doing work for the poor's bread. If his faith is as real as his fury, God can still find him there. The city keeps its verdict. The city's peace keeps its preacher alive. Take him away."

* * *

She went to see him before she left, because a ruler who will not look at the people she has broken is a ruler who will break the wrong ones eventually. The house of correction stood by the lower river, a former granary with small windows and whitewashed walls, and the keeper led her through the workroom where the prisoners picked oakum in the cold to the cell they had given the preacher alone, for safety's sake.

Anselm sat on a stool in his coarse prison shirt, thinner than the man in the square, his bare feet on the stone, and he rose when she came in with the instinctive dignity of the clergy even in disgrace.

"Jezebel comes to view the captive prophet," he said.

"Jeanne comes to ask a question, and then she'll leave you to your silence." She stayed by the door, her hands in her muff. "Did you mean for them to kill him? Rémy. In the porch."

The burning eyes flickered. For a moment she saw the man under the prophecy, a clever, exhausted, frightened man who had discovered too late the size of the force he had summoned. "I meant the fear of God. I meant the books to burn and the clerks to run. I did not mean a boy to die. I have said so in my prayers every night."

"Then learn the arithmetic of crowds, since God apparently hasn't explained it. You don't get to choose what a crowd does after you've heated it. You may intend the books. The crowd intends whatever the cruelest man in it intends, and it does it in your name. That is why rulers keep law, Brother. Not because law is holy. Because it is cold, and cold things don't catch fire."

"The law is the rich man's shield."

"The law is the only shield the poor man has that the rich man also has to carry. Your crowd in that porch had no law in it at all. Ask the washerwoman who saw it." She turned to go, then stopped. "You'll be treated well here. Warmth, bread, the offices, books. If in a year you wish to write a recantation of the sermon, not of your faith, it will be read and you may earn a parish. I don't require you to be wrong. I require you to stop being dangerous. The rest is between you and your God, and unlike you, I won't speak for Him."

Outside, the winter air was a relief, and she stood a moment on the steps filling her lungs, and Lisette, who had waited with the guard, said, "You were kinder than he deserved."

"Perhaps. But I meant what I said about the cold." She pulled the muff tighter. "A hanged Anselm would be preaching still, in ten years' time, in a dozen market squares, by other mouths. A silent Anselm picking oakum is a story with no ending. Stories without endings don't spread."

* * *

The forges relit within the week, one by one, the great hammers taking up their old rhythm, and the iron tithe began to flow again, and the aldermen, who had lived a month in terror, were discovered to be suddenly taller. Jeanne spent her last days walking the workshops, because the guns and armor of the spring campaign were being made here and she intended to know how. She learned the difference between bloomery and finery, the smell of good steel and bad, the tempering colors of a gun barrel, and she promised the smiths' guild the same charter terms as Sainterre in exchange for a fixed price on ordinance for the duration, a bargain sealed over wine in the guildhall and written down by both sides.

On the morning she left, the washerwoman came to the ducal house with a parcel, a linen shirt of her own making, coarse and clean, and would say only, "For the road, madame." Jeanne took it with the same gravity she would have given a lord's gift, and wore it on the march, and put the moment in the ledger of the things that were not money.

Thiébaut met the column at the river crossing with the army's van, and fell in beside her horse, and said, "The whole camp knows the story. You argued scripture in a market square, arrested a prophet by his own city's law, and made him a weaver. Jean says you're the most dangerous woman in Europe and wants to know if you'll lecture his officers."

"Jean can be first in line." She looked back once at the red haze above the iron hills. "He was right about some things. That's the difficulty. The tolls are too high, the tithe bites, the clerks are sometimes insolent. A ruler who can't hear the true part of a demagogue's speech is a ruler who deserves him. I've asked the aldermen to review the toll schedule. Not abolish. Review."

"You keep giving ground and ending up taller. I've stopped understanding how you do it."

"It isn't ground." She turned the grey mare north. "It's rent. I pay a little of it on purpose, at my own time, so no one comes to collect the whole house."

He was quiet a moment, and then laughed, the low, rare laugh she had begun to listen for without admitting it. "We're going to win this war, Jeanne. I don't know when, and I don't know what it'll cost. But we're going to win it."

"Write that down," she said. "I keep accounts of predictions, too."

Behind them the hammers of Grisay beat on, steady as a heart, making the iron that would decide the spring, and the road north ran white and hard through the winter fields toward the court and the next fire, which was already, in the dispatches riding ahead of them, beginning to burn.
