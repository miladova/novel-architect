# Chapter Twenty-Three — The Fortress at Mont d'Or

They came south in the hard December of 1485, and the army that moved down the kingdom road was not the army that had marched the same road in the spring of the year before. The feudal levies had become contracted squadrons; the billmen had pikes and drill; the guns rode on their low carriages in a proper artillery train, and the gunners had a year of practice and the confidence of a city saved. The discipline held in the snow because it had been learned in the snow, in the Harchies, and the men believed in the command because the command had, so far, kept them alive and paid.

Jeanne rode with the column for the first four days, in a fur-lined saddle against the cold, seven months rounded and tired by noon every day, until the doctors and Thiébaut together staged a small, respectful mutiny and put her into a covered wagon on the frozen ruts, where she did the accounts and hated every mile of it. Her orders reached the van by courier, and the army knew she was in the train, and that was the part that mattered.

Mont d'Or rose out of the white hills exactly as she remembered it, the great rock with its walls following the stone, the river road under its guns, the fortress that Valvert had held with sixty-one men and that Gui de Hautperthuis had given away with a handshake. A French garrison of six hundred held it now, under a commander named Chastel, a professional sent by Graçay with enough provisions for the winter and the confidence of a man on top of a mountain. The traitor himself was inside, the reports said, with his household and his gold, having chosen the rock over the open country when the attainder rolled through his manors like a slow, legal tide.

"He thinks we'll sit here until the spring and go home." Thiébaut spread the siege plans in the command tent, the brazier glowing red at its center. "He's read the old books. Armies don't take rocks in January. Therefore we take it in January, because that is precisely what he has not paid for."

The investment went in over three brutal days. The camps went up on the four approaches, the circumvallation trench was cut through the frozen ground with picks and hot water, the river road was closed by a fortified bridge of barrels and earth, and the guns, the four big bombards and the sledge pieces and the new long culverins, were emplaced in battery positions looking up at the walls. The cold was the true enemy of the first week. The sentries rotated by the half hour, the horses were blanketed and walked through the nights, the powder casks sat in warmed tents with a guard who did nothing but watch for sparks, and the hospital tents had their own braziers and the convent women's knowledge of frozen limbs.

Jeanne established herself in the commandeered manor house at the camp's center, within earshot of the guns, and ran the civilian side of the siege from there: the supply convoys up the frozen road, the mail, the money, the relations with the countryside that made the convoys possible. The village mayors of the region, who had spent the year watching which army kept its word, brought grain and timber at fixed prices and went home alive with receipts, and the French on the rock watched the convoys arrive and understood that the besiegers were not going to starve first.

* * *

The countryside decided the siege almost as much as the guns. In the old wars the local people had fled from both armies and sold to neither; in this one, after a year of proclamations kept and receipts honored, they came to the camp instead. The mayors of the river villages met the commissary officers at the crossroads with grain and hay and the great ox teams needed for the guns, and the market that grew behind the siege lines had its own charter of prices, nailed to an oak and enforced by the provosts. A farmer who brought a load in December got the same silver as in June, and the story traveled, and the convoys went through where the French foragers found empty barns and closed doors.

Jeanne had the market visited every week by her own clerk, and she sat once, in her furs on an upturned cask, while a village elder explained the logic of it. "The French take, madame. They have the king's name and the king's manners, and both mean empty cellars. Your duke's men pay, and your provosts hang the ones who don't. A peasant can do arithmetic too. We're tired of being the arithmetic that loses."

"Then make sure your sons remember who kept his receipts. The peace will need remembering."

She had the exchange printed, of course, in Verhoeven's sheet, with the elder's name and village, and Dolfin's factor wrote to Venice that the duchy's army was being supplied by public opinion, a thing he had not previously classified as a military resource.

The camp itself became a winter town, the same disciplined rows as the Harchies march but larger: the artillery park with its guards, the hospital tents where the convent women treated the frozen and the wounded, the chapel tent with its two services a day, the sutlers' street where the soldiers spent their pay and the news sheet was read aloud by the literate to anyone who would buy the reader a cup. At night, from the rock, the French garrison looked down on five thousand fires and a city in the snow that was better supplied than their own fortress, and the deserters began to come over the walls on ropes, one or two a night, with their accounts of the dwindling stores and the quarrels between Chastel and Hautperthuis.

* * *

The gunnery duel opened on the eighth day, and it gave the new army its first lesson in what a rock could do.

Mont d'Or's walls had been built by her grandfather's engineers and improved since, and the French had added earth and timber behind the stone in the modern fashion, so that the shot that broke the outer face buried itself in packed earth instead of opening a breach. The fortress's own guns, mounted high, looked down into the siege works and made the battery crews pay for every round: a culverin on the keep tower enfiladed the main trench, and three gunners died there in the first morning, and a dozen men were wounded.

Hautemer, the Lorraine master gunner, commanded the batteries, and he came to the manor at dusk with a face of stone and the casualty list and a problem that the old war could not solve. "The keep gun has the angle of us, madame. I can silence the wall pieces, but not that one, not from the lower battery. We need a battery on the west spur, the high ground. It's a fortnight's trenching in the rock, or we don't take the place cheaply."

"Then we trench the spur, and we move the two sledge guns up there first, because they can be hauled where the bombards cannot." She had the map; she had studied every spur and watercourse of this rock in the months of planning. "And answer the keep gun with a counter-battery of the culverins at the angle where the tower's embrasure narrics its arc. Van Brock worked it out on paper in November. The gunners can see it; I'll send the drawing tonight."

The west spur became the siege's slow, grinding heart: a sap pushed forward through rock and frost, a yard a day, then two, the working parties protected by gabions and the covering fire of the archers, the guns dragged up behind them on platforms that had to be rebuilt every night against the cold. Jeanne went up to the spur as often as the doctors allowed, muffled in furs with her own guards around her, because the gunners worked differently when the duchess had stood in their trench, and she had learned the value of being seen.

It was on the spur, in the third week, that the shell came in.

The French gunner on the keep had found the range of the working platform at last, and a stone shot struck the gabion at the trench's head and went through it and through the platform beyond, and Hautemer, standing where Jeanne had stood an hour before, took the splinter of the platform timber through the chest and died before the stretcher-bearers could get him off the rock. The battery, with its captain dead and its sergeant's arm broken in the same strike, wavered in the full view of the fortress, and the French watch raised a cheer from the wall.

She came up the spur herself, through the working parties, eight months heavy and slow on the frozen ground, with the gunners staring and her guard begging her to keep below the parapet, and she took command of the battery the way she had taken command of everything: with the book.

"Van Brock's tables. Who has them?" A shaken young gunner produced the sheaf. "Then we follow them. You, the elevation, quadrant level with the embrasure lip. You, the charge, the measure and no more, because Hautemer trained you and I have seen your drill. The keep gun fires every nine minutes; time it. We fire between its shots, and we fire at the embrasure, not the tower. A gun that cannot show its muzzle is a gun that does not exist. Sergeant, the working party continues the sap, because the only answer to a death in the trench is the trench moving forward. Is that understood?"

It was. The battery went back to work under her direction, the gunners finding their nerve in the numbers, and by the end of the afternoon the keep gun's embrasure had taken three direct hits and its firing slowed to a trickle, and the sap had gained another yard and a half. When she came down the spur at dusk, frozen to the bone, the whole working line knew the story by the time she reached the bottom, and the story did the army's morale more good than a week of pay.

Thiébaut met her at the manor door with a face she had not seen since the church at Sainterre, and said, through his teeth, "If you ever climb a battery under fire again while you are carrying my child, I will have you carried off it by the guard, in front of the army, and take whatever the story costs me."

"You would not dare."

"I would dare precisely that, and you know it, which is why you came back down." He helped her out of the furs with hands that were not quite steady. "Jeanne. The gunners can do the work. You cannot replace yourself. Neither can I."

"Hautemer is dead. Someone had to hold the battery."

"The gunners would have held it. They're trained."

"Then I made sure of it." She put her cold hand to his face. "I am not being brave. I am being the duchess. There is a difference, and you taught it to me. Now tell me the trenching reports, because the child has decided to use my bladder as a pillow and I have perhaps an hour of useful work left tonight."

* * *

The fifth column made its last throw on a dark night in the first week of January, in the command tent itself.

The man was a groom, taken into the headquarters horse lines at the musters, a quiet worker with good references from a lord's stable that no one had troubled to trace because grooms were beneath the level of treason. His name was Colas, and the French had bought him at the autumn fair, with thirty écus and a promise of more, and his work was the duke's two best horses and therefore his routine: the saddling hour, the picket line, the evening water. The poison, a white powder the French called _poudre de succession_, went into Thiébaut's evening wine cup on the night before the planned assault, and it would have gone unseen if Perrin had not been fourteen and in love with the whole idea of being a squire.

The boy had made it his business to know every man who came near the headquarters tents, the way other boys knew horses or saints, and he had noticed that Colas the groom had no friends among the other grooms, that he went out alone after dark, and that his hands were too clean for stable work. On the night in question he followed him, watched the cup go down on the trestle and the groom linger by it with his back turned, and then did the thing that makes fourteen-year-old squires into legends or corpses: he picked up the cup, smelled the bitter almond no wine should have, and knocked it out of his master's hand in front of the duty guard, spilling the wine across the maps.

They had Colas within the hour, and under the provost's questioning he gave up the whole small thread: the fair, the écus, the contact in Hautperthuis's old service, the instructions to wait for a night when the duke's tent was busy and the cup unguarded. The contact, when they took him in the camp followers' quarter, had papers that named the chain back to the fortress itself, to Hautperthuis's steward, who had fled inside Mont d'Or with his master. The fifth column, the long network Lisette had mapped and the attainder had broken, had spent its last coin on a groom.

Thiébaut had the man tried by the army court and hanged at dawn in the horse lines, where every groom in the camp could see, and he made a point of standing beside Perrin at the execution with his hand on the boy's shoulder. Afterwards he said, "You're a squire no longer. As of this morning you're a man-at-arms, with the pay, and you'll take the spurs when this is over. And in the meantime, you sleep outside my door, where I can keep an eye on the only competent groom in the army."

Perrin, white and trying not to be sick from the hanging, managed the salute. Jeanne, who had watched from the manor doorway, said later that the boy's face was the face of every person in the war: frightened, promoted beyond expectation, and determined to be equal to it.

* * *

The mine was ready on the fourteenth of January.

The engineers had run two galleries under the southwest angle of the fortress, the miners working in relays through the frozen ground with the same patient silence as the men of Sainterre, and the chambers were charged with every pound of powder the train could spare, because a rock that had cost this much could not be allowed to survive a weak blow. The assault force, twelve hundred picked men under Jean's command with Rostaing's light cavalry dismounted in the van, waited in the forward trench through the dark morning, and the guns held their fire to keep the noise of the mine unexpected.

Thiébaut led from the second wave, because no order could keep him behind, and Jeanne watched from the west spur battery with the gunners and her own guard, heavy and breathless in the cold, her hand on the parapet.

At the first light the mine went up.

It did not sound like the guns. It sounded like the mountain itself being torn open, a deep, prolonged roar that came up through the soles of the boots, and the southwest angle of Mont d'Or lifted and folded and came down in a cataract of stone and earth and the men who had been standing on it. The dust hung over the rock like a grey curtain, and through the curtain the assault went in at the run, and the drums and the trumpets opened all at once, and the siege of six weeks became the battle of an hour.

The breach was worse ground than it looked, a chaos of shattered masonry with the French behind the inner retrenchment they had built against this exact possibility, and the first wave took it with pikes and grenades and the brute courage of men who had watched their friends die in the trenches. Jean went down in the first ten minutes with a stone blow to the helmet that cracked it and knocked him senseless, and Rostaing took over the van and carried the retrenchment at the second rush, and then the fighting was inside the walls, in the narrow lanes of the fortress, the old work of door and stair and courtyard.

Thiébaut's wave came through the breach as the sun cleared the hills, and he was in the lane below the keep when the French counterattack came down it, and that was where the shoulder went. A man-at-arms with a two-handed sword caught him at the edge of the press, and the blade came down on the left shoulder where the old Sarrebourg wound lived in the bone, and the harness held but the bone did not, and he went to one knee in the snow with the world going white, and killed the man anyway with his right hand and a dagger, and refused the stretcher until the keep gate was taken, because he had seen what happened to assaults when the commander left the ground.

From the spur, Jeanne saw the banner check in the lane and then go forward again, and she did not know whose blood was on it, and the next quarter hour was the longest of her life, and she spent it doing the only useful thing available to her: running the guns. The battery fired over the breach at the keep's upper works to keep the walls clear, round after round on the tables, the gunners loading and firing in the rhythm she had learned on the practice field, and when the joined arms standard finally went up over the keep gate, she sat down on a powder cask in the snow and discovered that her legs had stopped working, and Lisette and Sister Margriet, who had come up with the army against every rule, put her into the wagon with hot bricks at her feet and a great deal of unsentimental scolding.

* * *

They found Gui de Hautperthuis in the donjon's high chamber, under his ancestors' portraits, with his gold and his remaining letters and a cup he had not drunk from. He was alone; his French garrison had surrendered in the courtyard below, and Chastel the commandant had given up his sword in proper form, and the man who had been the richest lord in the dominion stood against the wall of his last room in a furred gown with the court smile still on his face, though it had slipped at the edges.

Thiébaut had him brought down to the manor at dusk, under guard, and Jeanne received him in the commandeered hall with the fire lit and the attainder papers on the table between them, and she did not rise.

"Cousin." The smile made one more attempt. "I see you are with child. The family continues, at least. Will you not sit with your own blood at the end of it?"

"My own blood is why I am standing, Gui. The man who dandled me on his knee sold the fortress that was supposed to protect my people, and his price was sixty cutthroats and a French pension. Valvert died at his gate. The villages of his marches burned. The letters in this room connect your steward to a poisoner in my husband's tent. We are past the part where blood is a courtesy."

"The law—"

"The law is in these papers, and it is going to take its course in open court, before your peers, with every witness and every lease. You wanted to be the regent. Instead you will be the example, which I think is the office you were always fitted for." She looked at him fully, the charm and the grey beard and the eyes that had calculated every room they entered. "You are held for trial at the Haut-Tertre. Your life belongs to the court. Your name belongs to the record. Take him away."

Chastel the commandant was brought in separately, under his parole, and the difference between the two prisoners was the difference between war and treason, and she meant the whole rock to see it. Chastel kept his sword, his purse, and his officers' table; his ransom would be set at the customary rate and his wounded treated by her surgeons. She received him standing, and said so to his face, and added that the defense of Mont d'Or had been correctly conducted and would be reported as such in the exchange papers. He bowed with the genuine surprise of a professional who had expected the treatment given to free companies, and the French officers with him took note, because officers always took note.

They found Valvert's grave behind the gatehouse chapel, a hasty mound with a wooden cross the French had permitted out of some residual chivalry, and Jeanne had the body disinterred in the thaw and sent home to Aubecourt with an escort of honor, and she ordered the gate of Mont d'Or rebuilt with a stone over the arch naming the captain who had held it with sixty-one men and died at its gate under a flag he had not opened. The garrison that took over the fortress paraded past the new stone on the day it was unveiled, and the old marshal said the dead man's name aloud in front of them, and the name became another of the duchy's small, durable currencies.

* * *

Thiébaut's shoulder kept him in the manor for a fortnight, in a sling and in the ferocious bad temper of an active man forbidden his horse, and the court of the siege camp rearranged itself around his convalescence. Jean, his own head wrapped against the concussion, came each evening to play cards and lose loudly, and Perrin, the boy who had saved the wine, was attached to the sickroom as a kind of honor guard, and took the duty with the solemnity of a bishop.

"The bone's set crookedly," Sister Margriet told Jeanne, in the flat way she delivered all medical news. "He'll keep the arm. He'll lose some of the lift and all of the sword in that hand for a year, perhaps forever. A man who fights left-handed will need a new teacher."

"He's been fighting left-handed since Sarrebourg. He just pretended otherwise." She watched him through the doorway, arguing with Jean over a hand of cards with his good hand and his face set against the pain. "Sister, between us. The shoulder or the child. Which of us did you dread more in the snow?"

"You, madame. The duke obeys orders. You rewrite them." The nun permitted herself a small, dry smile and went off to bully the surgeons.

That night he admitted, to the ceiling rather than to her, the thing the soldiers' code would not let him say by day. "I'm forty years old, and I have been wounded in the same place twice for the same patch of ground, and the second time my wife was on a battery under fire with my child inside her. Somewhere in the accounting there's a lesson, and I don't like its shape."

"The lesson is that the ground is ours now. That's the shape. Stop reading ledgers over my shoulder; it's my trade." She put her hand on the good arm. "When this heals, Van Brock wants you for the new cavalry drill. The gunners have ideas about men who can't raise a lance."

"I'm to be an experiment."

"You're to be alive. The experiments come after."

When the doors had closed, Thiébaut, his arm in a sling and his face grey with the pain the surgeons were managing badly, came and stood beside her at the fire. "Jean's alive. Concussion, nothing more. The shoulder's broken again, worse than before; they say I'll keep the arm and lose some of its motion, which is a bargain. The fortress is ours, the road is open, and Paris has just lost its last door into your country. Madame la duchesse, the war is over except for the paperwork."

"Paperwork is the part I'm best at." She leaned against him carefully, on the good side. "We have the peace conference, and the trial, and the baby, in roughly that order."

"Roughly in that order. Jeanne," he stopped, and said the rest to the fire, "when the banner checked in the lane, and you couldn't see, I want you to know I had every confidence in the gunners."

"The gunners had every confidence in the tables. I merely held them." She closed her eyes. "Take me to bed, my lord. The rock has fallen and I am extremely tired, and the paperwork can begin at dawn."
