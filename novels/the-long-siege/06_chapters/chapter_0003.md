# Chapter Three — The Old Wound

The muster field lay two leagues south of Aubecourt, where the river bent around a flat green plain that had been used for the assembling of armies since her grandfather's time. Jeanne rode out to see it on a bright cold morning with Brive at her left hand and an escort of twenty lances, and she asked the old marshal for the truth before they were halfway there.

"The truth, my lady, is that the truth will depress you."

"I have been depressed by better men. Ride level and speak."

He grunted, which with Brive passed for laughter, and gave her the musters as they came in. The great vassals sent their formal replies in the hands of formal messengers: quotas acknowledged, dates hedged, illnesses discovered, border duties remembered. Of the feudal host due by the twentieth of April, perhaps half would appear, and of that half a quarter were tenants dragged from plows with bills and hunting spears. The household men-at-arms were the steel in it, three hundred of them, plus the survivors of Roches-Noires filtering home in pairs and trios, hollow men who flinched at thunder. No cavalry worth the name. No guns beyond the wall pieces. No pike, because the Swiss contract had died unpaid, and a duchy that did not keep pikes in 1484 was a duchy that had not yet understood what century it was dying in.

They reached the ridge above the field. Below them, tents the color of old teeth straggled along the river, smoke rose from a hundred small fires, and a company of muddy infantry was being put through its evolutions by a hoarse sergeant, wheeling badly and catching its own billhooks. A trumpeter practiced the same four notes until even the horses looked weary.

"My father would have known this field's number at a glance," Jeanne said.

"Your father also lost the last army this field produced." Brive reined in beside her, and his old face was not unkind. "Do not mistake me, madame. I loved him, after my fashion. But he loved a charge the way other men love wine, and the French set the table for him. The next army of Vauréal will be an army of contracts and cannon, not of chivalry. Which is why your council is right about Lorraine, and why you know it, and why the knowing does not help."

She did not answer. Below them the trumpeter began again. On the road north, a cart turned in at the field gate, and another, and a file of mounted men with a banner she could not read. The host was coming, such as it was, rolling toward her across the green plain whether she was ready or not.

"Ride back," she said at last. "I have old rooms to walk through."

* * *

She went to the east wing after dinner, on foot, with only Lisette behind her, as though she were a girl again and not the reason two hundred carpenters were hammering the lower town into barracks. The east wing held the nurseries and the schoolroom and the long gallery where the children of the court had been allowed to make noise at stated hours. It was given over now to storage: old furniture under dust sheets, a broken falcon's perch, a cradle she recognized.

The ward had come in the year she turned four. She remembered him first as a noise, a boy's shout in the quiet courtyard, and then as a fact: the Duke of Lorraine's son, nine years old, handed over at the bridge of Pont-d'Ortelle in exchange for a peace her father had no intention of keeping longer than suited him. Thiébaut had been small for his age then, and stiff in new clothes, and he had cried on the first night in a room where someone could hear him, which was the schoolroom chimney, and Jeanne, who had been awake for reasons of her own, had heard him through the flue. She had told no one. It was the first secret they shared, though he did not know it for two years.

He grew into the place the way a sapling grows in a courtyard crack, determined and unconsulted. Her father's court raised him on the cheap, as wards are raised: fed at the lower table, taught with the ducal children because a clerk was already being paid, drilled in the yard by the same master-at-arms, forgotten at feast days and remembered whenever a treaty needed a signature. By the time Jeanne was seven he was twelve, long-legged and grave, and he had appointed himself her particular sentinel. He showed her where the stable cat had her kittens. He explained the siege diagrams in her father's books when the clerk's Latin went too fast. He taught her to skate on the frozen canal in the hard winter of '72, holding her wrists, and when she fell he fell with her so that she would not be the only one laughing.

The scar at her eyebrow came from the wall of the lower garden, which they had climbed together to watch a burning barn. She had been eleven, he sixteen. The coping crumbled; he caught her, which saved her neck, and the edge of the stone caught her brow, and he had carried her to the surgeon white and silent and swearing in a dialect of Lorraine French she had never heard him use. Her father had beaten him for it, formally, with a riding cane, in front of the guard, because a ward who endangered the heiress needed a lesson visible to the household. She remembered the sound of it, and the way Thiébaut had not made a sound himself, and the way he had smiled at her afterwards in the corridor with the sweat still on his face, as though the thing that mattered was that she had stopped crying.

"Careful," Lisette said behind her, low. Jeanne realized her hand was pressed to the scar.

The gallery ended at the door of the old solar, the small one, the one no one used now because the new one with the Italian windows had been built when she was thirteen. The key was in the lock. It turned easily; the room smelled of dust and old velvet and something else, some ghost of woodsmoke and boy. The tapestries were gone to safer walls. The hearth was cold. The window looked over the orchard where they had stolen apples, and the wall beyond, and the road south.

She made herself remember the rest.

* * *

She was fifteen. The storms of that November came one after another, three days of rain so hard the Ortelle rose and the lower town flooded its cellars, and the court was shut indoors with its embroideries and its dice and its boredom. She and Thiébaut had been changing for a year by then, the easy comradeship of children thickening into something that embarrassed the other courtiers and entertained them. He was twenty, nearly a man, nearly the end of his wardship, and the question of his return to Lorraine sat in every room he entered like a creditor by the fire.

They had talked, before, in the careless way children talk forever, of what they would do when he went home. That month the talk changed. She had begun to notice the shape of his hands and the exact color of his eyes, which were hazel and turned green in certain weather, and he had begun to watch her in doorways and stop mid-sentence when she came in. Neither of them had the vocabulary for it. They had only the old friendship and the new fire and the terrible confidence of the young that their case was unlike any case that had ever existed.

On the third night of the storms, the household went early to bed. She found her way to the old solar because she could not sleep, and he was already there, by the window, wrapped in a cloak against the draught, watching the lightning. She said his name. He turned, and she saw on his face the same astonishment she felt, that the other person should be there, as though the world had for once arranged itself on their side.

What passed between them there was nothing the court could have hanged anyone for, and everything it could have ruined them with. They sat on the old chest beneath the window, under one cloak, and talked until the rain thinned, and somewhere in the talk they said the words. That they would not be parceled out. That his duchy and her duchy and her father's treaties and the Emperor's marriages were not the owners of the two people in this room. That he would speak to her father when the time came, or that they would find another way, and she said she would give up the inheritance before she would be given with it, and he said she must never say that, never, not even here, and she said it again to make him laugh. He kissed her. It was the first kiss for both of them, clumsy and reverent and long, and then they kissed again, more surely, and the lightning lit the dusty room in white flashes, and at some point, clothed and clasped under the cloak, they slept.

Dawn came grey and pitiless. The door opened. It was Foulques, then forty-eight and already the duke's right hand, with four guards of the watch behind him and a face she did not recognize, a face like the face of a man watching a house fall. She would learn later that a sewing woman had seen the two candles and remembered the price of information. Thiébaut stood up at once, shielding her with his body, and said the one sentence she held onto for seven years: "This is my doing. She is innocent of everything but kindness."

Foulques did not answer him. He said, "The duke will see you both in the great hall after terce. Dress as you are. Do not speak to one another." And the guards took him away, and the women took her away, and the solar door was locked behind them.

* * *

The great hall had been full. That was the thing. She had expected judgment in a chamber, and found instead the whole court in its best clothes, the vassals who were at the Haut-Tertre, the clerics, the ladies, the knights, the servants at the back with their frightened eyes, an audience assembled as though for a mummery. Her father sat in the high seat under the arms of Vauréal, and his face was the face she knew from accounts of the executions he attended: attentive, impartial, bored.

Thiébaut stood in the center of the hall without a guard near him, which was worse than a guard. He had been made to put on his finest tunic, the Lorraine one with the alérions, as though the occasion were ceremonial. He looked at her when she came in, and then, with an effort she could feel across the room, he looked away.

Her father spoke first, pleasantly, about honor and wardship and the price of confidence. Then he asked Thiébaut, in the tone of a man discussing the weather, what he had to say for himself.

And Thiébaut said the words.

She had rehearsed the memory so often that the edges had gone soft, like the edges of a coin carried too long, but the words themselves never softened. He said he had been flattered by the child's infatuation and had not the gravity to put a stop to it sooner. He said nothing had happened that touched her honor, which was true, in the way a knife's edge is true. He said he had made no promises, held out no hopes, intended nothing; that he was grateful for the bread of Vauréal and would never presume on it; that boys and girls in a closed court would invent affections as closed rooms invented draughts; that he renounced any claim, suit, or understanding, present or future, between his house and the heiress of Vauréal, and would hold himself ready to depart for Lorraine at the duke's pleasure.

He called her a child. In the great hall, before everyone, he called the night in the solar a child's infatuation, and himself its amused and patient object, and not once did he look at her while he did it, and she stood at the front of the hall with every eye in Christendom on her face and understood that she had been the only person in the solar who meant a single word that had been said there.

Her father nodded, as though the answer were satisfactory and the matter closed. He made a little speech about the duchess's daughter being above such foolishness and the propriety of a speedy departure. The court murmured its relief. Thiébaut rode out before vespers with two servants and an escort to the border, and she watched from the east window of the gallery, and he did not turn once, not at the gate, not on the road, not where the road bent by the willows.

She had been fifteen. She had learned in one morning that love was a thing that could be performed backward, and that the safest place in the world for a person's secrets was inside herself, because no one else's keeping had ever been worth the rent.

"Jeanne." Lisette's hand was on her sleeve. The maid's face was furious and wet, the way it always was when the solar came up, because Lisette had carried the notes and Lisette had taken the beating for them and Lisette had never once believed the performance. "It's a cold room for old ghosts. Come away."

"In a moment." She crossed to the chest beneath the window and laid her palm on its lid, the place where they had sat under one cloak. The wood was cold and ordinary. "I am going to marry him, Lisette."

"I know. The whole palace knows. The scullions have odds on the date."

"Do you counsel against it?"

"I counsel against dying French, my lady, and against watching the guilds pull this house down brick by brick, and against every other thing that happens if you don't. I don't counsel about him. That one's between you and the girl you were." Lisette wiped her cheeks with the back of her hand, fierce and unbecoming. "Though if you want my plain opinion, he looked like a man being broken on the wheel that day, not a man getting off easy. I saw it. I was at the back. Nobody else was watching him. I was."

Jeanne said nothing. She had carried her version of that morning for seven years, polished and smooth and complete, and she had no intention of opening it now, ten days before the wedding that reason had already signed.

She locked the solar door herself and gave the key to no one.

* * *

The council met that evening over bread and eel pie, because the state would not adjourn for supper and supper refused to be postponed any longer. Foulques had arranged the marriage question on three sheets: the imperial alternative, the Lorraine alternative, and a third sheet which was almost entirely blank, headed _Other Means_.

"The imperial alternative," said the chancellor, taking up his pointer like a schoolmaster, "is an archduke. The Emperor's cousin, aged nineteen, amiable, currently resident at a court two hundred leagues away, possessed of a name and no money. Marriage brings the imperial alliance in theory, an imperial commissioner in fact, and no troops for at least a year, because the Emperor's troops are always somewhere else. It also places your inheritance under the feudal umbrella of a house that has never once kept an umbrella in repair."

Brive snorted.

"The Lorraine alternative is Thiébaut himself. A reigning duke, twenty-seven, unmarried, hardened by six years of holding a marchland against France with no revenue and no friends. His full strength, by our best count, is eleven hundred men-at-arms, two thousand mounted archers, and an ordinance company of pike and gun that has never been beaten when paid. He is also, and the council does not conceal this, a man who knows this court, this duchy, and yourself. Some would count that last fact as a danger."

"Some would," said Jeanne. She cut her eel pie into exact quarters. "Some would count a man who cannot be surprised by me as useful. Continue."

"The treaty as drafted preserves your sovereignty entire. Separate councils, separate seals, no Lorrainer holds office or garrison in Vauréal without your written appointment. His troop quota serves under his banner but at your pay, which makes them yours. The dower lands run to you for life. The reversion clause protects the succession. Madame, the document is almost suspiciously favorable. His own chancellor must have fought it."

"The Abbé de Viray," Brive put in, "is a priest with the soul of a sergeant. He would have fought it. Which means the duke overruled him."

"Why would he?" Jeanne asked the room. "Why give a rich duchy a marriage contract that asks for none of its government? A man in his position could have held out for the regency. Hautperthuis would have."

Foulques's pen paused a fraction too long. "Reasons may be suggested. He needs the money more than he needs the title. His duchy is poor and exposed; Vauréal's revenue is what keeps Lorraine on the map. A regency clause would have split your vassals and handed Graçay his pretext for a war of liberation. A light hand binds the cities; the cities hold the roads; the roads are what he is actually buying." The chancellor's voice was level. "Men at the ends of their resources, madame, make generous treaties. It does not require a romantic explanation."

Jeanne looked at him. There it was again, that small wrong note, the thing she had heard in his voice whenever the old subject came near. She filed it with the ledger entries and did not press. Pressing Foulques was like pressing a full cup in a dark room; one waited until one could see.

"The other risks," she said.

"Are real." Brive leaned forward. "He knows you. That cuts both directions. You will be sharing a bed with a man who can read your face at twenty paces, and a council table with a trained soldier who will want his say in the war that his army is fighting. The treaty says separate councils. Wars do not observe treaties about councils. You will have to fight him for every decision, every day, and do it smiling, because the vultures will be watching the marriage the way crows watch a sick sheep."

"And if I refuse him?"

The old marshal's face answered first. "Then he makes his terms with Paris, because Lorraine cannot stand alone another winter, and the French get his road instead of us. Or he takes the Emperor's money, and the Empire gets his road. Either way, madame, the map moves around you, and you become the thing maps are redrawn over: a small rich country with a girl's name over the gate."

The fire settled in the hearth. Outside, the wind from the river moved the tapestries.

Jeanne ate a quarter of the pie, methodically, while the men waited. She thought of the muster field and its billhooks. She thought of de Key's arithmetic and Dolfin's fourteen percent and the Regent's silky letters. She thought of the boy who had caught her on the garden wall and the man who had cut her loose in the great hall, and she set the two of them side by side in her mind like two ledgers that could not be reconciled. Then she thought of the line she had written on the first night: what cannot be refused must be made to serve me.

"Send the answer tomorrow," she said. "I accept the treaty, with four additions. First, the troop quota is doubled in the event of a direct French invasion, at my cost and his command, the command to be exercised jointly through a war council of which I am the head. Second, the dower revenues are paid quarterly into an account controlled by my treasurer, not his. Third, all patents of office in Vauréal remain mine alone, including over any Lorrainer serving here. Fourth," she paused, choosing the words like blades, "the marriage is celebrated at the Haut-Tertre, in my capital, before my vassals, and no ceremony, document, or custom anywhere shall describe me otherwise than as Duchess of Vauréal in her own right. He takes no title of mine. I will be called madame, and not Her Grace the duchess his wife, by any herald in either country. Write it down, Maître Foulques, in the plainest Latin your clerks can manage. If he signs, the gold for his first payroll leaves Malemort with the reply."

Brive stared at her. "You have thought about this."

"I have thought about nothing else for two weeks. I am merely the last person to be told my own decision."

* * *

The courier rode south at dawn with the sealed counter-treaty and the first gold convoy under a strong guard, and the Haut-Tertre turned itself to the business of a wedding with the grim efficiency of a fortress turning itself to a siege. Carpenters built stands in the lower courtyard. The cathedral chapter was consulted about flowers and processions. Oriabel produced, from somewhere in the bottomless arithmetic of her household, a length of cloth-of-silver and a dressmaker from Sainterre who was given a room and a deadline and, at Jeanne's insistence, no instructions about white.

"White is for virgins and queens," Jeanne said, standing still while the woman measured her shoulders. "I am a sovereign making a treaty. Make it the color of steel."

In the hours she stole from the state, she did what she had always done: she prepared. She read every dispatch out of Nancy for three years. She learned the names of his captains, the state of his fortresses, the debts his father had left and the ones he had added. She learned that the Abbé de Viray governed his conscience and the Bastard Jean governed his camp and that no woman had ever been named in connection with his court for longer than a season, which the gossips read as coldness and she read, unwillingly, as control. She learned that he had been wounded in the left shoulder at the relief of a town called Sarrebourg and that the shoulder still closed gates on him in winter.

She also, on the night before he was due, took the ribbon-tied letters from the casket one last time and read the topmost, the fifteen-year-old's letter, all the way through. It was worse than she remembered, fervent and certain and full of plans, and at the bottom it said that she would rather be Thiébaut's wife in a cottage than duchess of the world. She read it without expression, folded it, and put it back, and tied the ribbon with the same knot.

Then she wrote one more letter, short, sealed it with the ducal ring, and gave it to Lisette with instructions that it was to be delivered into the Duke of Lorraine's own hand when he arrived at the gate, before any ceremony, before any greeting, before he had so much as dismounted.

"What does it say?" Lisette asked, turning it over.

"It says the terms on which he enters this house." Jeanne fastened the chain at her throat, key and crucifix together. "It says that the Duchess of Vauréal receives the Duke of Lorraine as her ally and her contracted husband, in public, at the gate, at noon. And it says that if he has anything to say to the girl he once knew, he may save his breath, because the girl is not at home and has no intention of receiving."

Lisette's mouth twitched. "Not a word of greeting in it at all?"

"One. At the end." She reached for her cloak. "I signed it _Jeanne_. I thought I could afford him that much, since he is getting everything else."

Outside the window, far down the southern road, a trumpet called from the watchtower, and then another, the signal posts passing the word along the river toward the Haut-Tertre: a column was on the road, in Lorraine's colors, moving fast, with the standard of the alérions at its head.

He had come a day early.

Jeanne stood for a moment with her hand on the window frame and made herself breathe once, twice, while her heart performed the ancient, treasonous, ungovernable ritual it had clearly been saving for this occasion. Then she let the curtain fall and went to find the marshal, because there were not enough guards on the gate and she had an army to inspect before she had a husband to survive.
