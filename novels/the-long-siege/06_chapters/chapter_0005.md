# Chapter Five — The Road Between Armies

Dawn in the Harchies came down grey through the beeches, the kind of light that makes every man look already dead. The four decoy merchants came along the road at a mule's pace, leading two pack animals and arguing loudly about tolls, their purses clinking with the silver Thiébaut had given them, which each man had sworn on his mother to return. They were the four meanest men in Lorraine, as requested, and they were enjoying themselves.

In the trees on both sides of the narrows, the lances waited dismounted, steel dark, horses held a hundred paces back. Thiébaut stood behind a trunk at the bend with Jean and the scout captain, and watched the road through the mist that the night's frost had drawn out of the ground. The bad shoulder was stiff under the leather; he worked it in small rotations, silently, the way he did before any fight, as though the old wound were a dog that needed to know who was master.

They came at the merchants from uphill, as predicted: a rush of ragged men in stolen jacks and ill-fitting sallets, perhaps thirty-five of them, with a few genuine men-at-arms among the trash and a captain on horseback in a dented breastplate, shouting to kill the mules first. The merchants, who had been chosen partly for their sprinting, scattered off the road with remarkable conviction. The bandits reached the packs.

Thiébaut's horn blew once.

The lances came out of the trees on both sides in the same instant, not charging, because cavalry in a beech wood was a way to break horses' legs, but walking in fast in their steel, in ordered files, and the narrows became a pen. The bandits' captain understood the arithmetic in the first ten seconds and spurred his horse at the uphill bank to break out, and the scout captain, who had been placed exactly for that possibility, took him through the throat with a spear. After that it was the usual miserable work of a trap closing: men screaming in two languages, the wet sound of blades on jack-leather, a few shots from bandit hand-cannons going wild into the canopy, and then, quickly, the clatter of thrown-down weapons and the sound of Lorraine men shouting that lives were to be kept.

It lasted as long as it takes a kettle to boil. Of the bandits, eleven were dead, six more would die before noon, and the rest were bound with their own bowstrings against the beech trunks. Of the lances, one man had a broken arm and another a pike wound in the thigh, and the four merchants were winded and rich in their own silver and already telling the story at twice its length.

Thiébaut cleaned his sword on a dead man's cloak and went to look at the prisoners.

* * *

They picked the undercaptain for the questions, a man with a split lip and a sensible face, who watched the hanged bodies of the two men who had refused to talk with the resignation of a professional revising his rates. Jean did the asking, Thiébaut the listening; he had learned early that a man who stays silent in an interrogation gets told more than a man who shouts.

The undercaptain's name was Beraud, and he had been a soldier once, in a real company, before the peace put him out of work and the roads put him back into it. The band had formed in January, he said, paid through the winter by a factor who came out of Pont-d'Ortelle, a man with no badge and good French gold. The work was simple: keep the roads frightened, burn the villages that paid their taxes too promptly, make the duchess's new reign look like a season in hell. Lately there had been a special commission.

"Word came ten days back," Beraud said, watching the bodies swing. "A party was coming from Lorraine. Small, rich, maybe the duke himself. We were to hit it in the forest and make sure none reached Aubecourt. There was to be a bonus for the duke's body, and the factor said the proof wanted rings or a seal, not a story."

"Who is the factor's master?"

Beraud's split lip tried to sneer and failed. "My lord, factors don't tell road captains their masters. I can tell you he had the manners of a lord's steward and the money of the French crown, and both can be hired by the same man in this country. There's a lord on the southern marches whose tenants never get burned, have you noticed? Every village for leagues around is ash, and his stay fat and his mills turning."

"Hautperthuis," Jean said.

"I didn't say it. You did." Beraud looked at Thiébaut with the odd, level honesty of a man who had nothing left to sell but information and meant to get the going rate. "My lord duke, kill me or keep me, but I'll tell you one thing free. You're riding to a wedding in a house that's already got a thief in it. The French aren't at the gate of that duchy. They're at the council table. I've served lords half my life. I know the smell."

Thiébaut had him kept alive and bound for the Aubecourt cells, along with three others who confirmed the gist, and he wrote a short report in his own hand before the column moved on, because the duchess's letter had insisted on the terms of his entry and he meant to enter the gate with something worth more than courtesy. The name Hautperthuis he did not write down. Names written on the road were names read by the wrong courier; he would give her that one in a room with the door shut.

* * *

At the Haut-Tertre, Jeanne reviewed the wedding arrangements as though they were a campaign, which in her opinion they were.

She went through the hall kitchens with the head cook and cut the banquet courses from twelve to nine, because a court that feasted while its garrisons went unpaid would be judged for it by every spy in Christendom, and because she wanted the guild deputies who would be present to remember restraint rather than revelry. She approved the stands in the courtyard and then moved them twice, for sightlines. She made Brive show her the gatehouse guard rotations three times and added twenty picked men in the livery of her own household, none of them Lorrainers. She set the seating in the cathedral with Foulques and then unseated it and set it again, because the order of benches was a declaration of the order of the world, and her vassals were reading it like scripture.

"Twice you've moved the Bishop of Heurtois," Foulques observed.

"The Bishop of Heurtois can sit behind the Sainterre aldermen and consider the humility of the Church. The aldermen are the ones I need to watch the wedding and believe the marriage is strong. Where is the Lorraine train now?"

"Last night's rider puts them in the Harchies. Fifty lances, my lady, when we were promised a great train."

"Good. A great train is an occupation. Fifty is an insult to bandits." She paused over the lists. "Any trouble on the road?"

"Not yet reported."

"He's early by a day. A man who arrives early to his own wedding is either eager or anxious, and Thiébaut of Lorraine has never in his life been eager. Make sure the gate is ready at noon. If he comes sooner, he waits until noon. I will not have this court think its duchess was caught putting on her shoes."

By the morning of the fifth of April the whole Haut-Tertre had been scrubbed, dressed, and drilled into a theater of sovereignty. The lower town turned out along the processional way, because a royal wedding was free bread and a holiday and the sight of the man the rumors had been chewing for a month. Banners hung from every window, red and silver and the Lorraine alérions together, and the heralds had new tabards, and the choir had practiced until the precentor despaired.

Jeanne dressed in the steel-grey gown with the silver thread, and a veil so fine it seemed to float, and no maiden's white anywhere. Lisette laced her with the grim efficiency of a squire arming a knight.

"Your hands are cold," the maid said.

"My hands are always cold. It's a family trait."

"Your hands are shaking, which is not." Lisette tied the last knot and stepped back, and her face did the thing it did when she was choosing between truth and kindness, and truth won, as it usually did with Lisette. "He can't reach you, whatever he is now. Not in there, in front of everyone. The whole court is your armor. Walk through the day one trumpeter at a time."

Jeanne looked at herself in the silver-backed glass her mother had brought from her own wedding. A face she knew and did not quite recognize: the ducal daughter become the duchess, thin from three weeks of no sleep, eyes too large, mouth set. She thought of the girl in the solar, who had promised cottages and meant them, and she put that girl away the way she put away the casket, with a key and a lock and a chain at her throat.

"One trumpeter at a time," she said. "Send word when he reaches the lower gate."

* * *

He came up from the river road at the head of his fifty lances, and the watchers on the walls saw first the standard and then the man, and the noise of the crowd changed pitch, the way it does when a rumor becomes a person.

Thiébaut rode a grey warhorse with its tail braided for ceremony, and he wore black armor without ornament and a surcoat of Lorraine's arms, and he had his brother beside him and his banner behind, and that was all. No wagons, no crowd of courtiers, no display beyond the thing itself. Jeanne watched from the gatehouse window behind a curtain, as a duchess may watch before she is seen, and her heart performed its treason again and she refused it audience.

He was broader than the boy. Seven years had cut the youth out of him and left something weathered and spare; he sat the horse like a man born at that altitude. Even at this distance she could see the economy of him, no gesture wasted, the eyes moving across the walls, the gatehouse, the guard positions, reading the fortress as she read ledgers. Then he dismounted in the courtyard with the ease of long practice, and turned, and looked up at the gatehouse window with perfect timing, as though he had known she was there, and she let the curtain fall a heartbeat too late.

Noon. The trumpets went, and the great doors opened, and she walked down through the hall between the rows of her household, with Brive at her right and Foulques at her left and her train carried by two pages, and she emerged into the April sunlight of the upper courtyard where the Duke of Lorraine waited with his lords.

He knelt. It was the first gesture, and it was correctly done, the vassal-king of a foreign duchy kneeling to the sovereign of this one on her own ground, and the court noted it with the hunger courts have for small victories. Then he rose at her gesture, and they faced each other across two paces of flagstone, and seven years of silence stood between them like a third person.

Up close, his face was worse: hazel eyes greenish in the open air, the same eyes, lines at their corners that had not been there, a scar at the jaw she did not know, the mouth set in the formal curve of a man performing a public self. He smelled of horses and steel and road dust and, faintly, of the cold-water soap of camps.

"Madame la duchesse." His voice had changed, deepened, acquired the low register of camps. "I have the honor to place myself and my company at your service, in accordance with the treaty between our houses. I come as your ally and your contracted husband, and I thank you for the welcome of your gate."

She had prepared her answer in writing and rehearsed it twice, and it came out level. "My lord duke, the Duchess of Vauréal receives you gladly, and the more gladly for the speed of your coming. Your company shall be honored in this house as our own. The council and the hall await you. You have had a long road."

"Not so long as some roads, madame." A beat, half a beat, the faintest stress on the word, invisible to anyone but her. "I have something for your private ear, when you choose. The Harchies gave us a small morning's work, and the names in it concern you more than me."

"You may present it to the council, my lord."

"I would rather present it to you. Names written in council chambers have a way of migrating. I was taught that at this court."

She held his eyes. Behind them the whole courtyard held its breath, though it could not have said why. "Then you shall have a private audience after the welcoming feast, my lord, with the chancellor and the marshal present. We do not keep rooms in this house without witnesses."

"No," he said, and for one instant something moved behind the formal face, old and tired and entirely without surprise. "I remember."

Brive stepped forward with the courtesies, and the moment passed into ceremony, and the two of them walked into the hall side by side, not touching, at the exact distance prescribed by the master of protocol, while the heralds shouted their styles and the crowd strained to see.

* * *

They had put him in the best guest suite, which adjoined her own apartments through a small connecting room that had been used, in her grandfather's day, for the ladies-in-waiting of visiting queens. The door between was heavy oak, and it had a lock, and Jeanne had the lock changed before his arrival and both keys brought to her.

The feast went by in the disciplined blur of state occasions. She ate little and noticed everything: the way his men drank the wine and judged it; the Bastard Jean making the lady next to him laugh within five minutes; Thiébaut's careful attention to every man presented, his hand on the arm of the old Brive, his correct indifference to the watching Hautperthuis. He did not look at her more than courtesy required, and she did not look at him at all, and the court saw two sovereigns behaving like sovereigns and was slightly disappointed.

After the feast, the private audience in the round chamber, with Foulques and Brive and the door guarded. He told them about the Harchies band, the French gold, the commission against his person, the steward's manners, and the unwritten name. Brive's face went the color of old beef. Foulques made notes with a hand that did not tremble, though Jeanne saw his jaw work once. When the duke had finished, she asked him why he had not written the name.

"Because I intend to be certain before I say it in a room with paper." He looked at her directly, the first unguarded look of the day. "Madame, a man can be hanged on a road captain's rumor. I would give you proof, or nothing. In the meantime, watch the lords whose mills keep turning."

She inclined her head, and the audience ended, and the day ended, and the palace of the Haut-Tertre banked its fires and its gossip and went to bed.

In her chamber, Jeanne dismissed the women and sat alone in the dark, on the edge of the great bed, listening. Through the connecting wall she heard, faintly, the small sounds of another person settling for the night: a boot set down, a pitcher pouring, the creak of a chair. Then silence. She wondered whether he was also sitting in the dark, and she hated the wondering, because it was the girl's wonder and not the duchess's, and the girl was supposed to be locked away.

On the other side of the wall, Thiébaut sat with his head in his hands and the bad shoulder burning, and did not light a candle either. He had crossed half a country of burning villages to reach this room, and the hardest league of the journey was the thickness of one oak door, and he had no orders for taking it.

* * *

He did not sleep either.

He sat in the dark of the guest chamber with his boots off and his sword across his knees, the way he had learned to sit in the worst winters of Lorraine, and he listened to the small sounds that told him the room beyond the wall was occupied: a footstep, the faint creak of the bed frame, once the low murmur of a woman dictating to a maid at an hour when the court believed duchesses slept. The Harchies road and the bandits and the long ride seemed, in this room, to have happened to another man. Here he was twenty years old again, walking these same corridors with a ward's careful eyes, learning which boards creaked and which guards could be talked to and the exact hour the east wing went dark.

He rose after a while and went to the window, and the moonlit Haut-Tertre arranged itself below him exactly as he remembered: the lower courtyard, the well, the wall of the lower garden with its crumbled coping, the road bending south by the willows. Seven years of his life had been spent in the keeping of this view. He had learned his letters in the schoolroom wing, his swordcraft in the yard, his first and only lessons in the cost of love in the old solar at the end of the gallery. The palace had not changed. The girl in the next room had changed into a duchess who wrote letters as cold as charter law and signed them with his own heart's first word.

A man could be afraid of many things in this trade. French lances, unpaid mutineers, the winter road. He had schooled himself out of all of them. He had not, he discovered, schooled himself out of the sound of her voice saying _my lord_ as though he were an envoy from a distant country, and he stood at the window a long time, making his peace with the fact that the hardest siege of his life was going to be conducted in the same house as its prize.

When the moon went behind a cloud he said the soldier's prayer his mother's people had used, the short one, the one that asked for steadiness rather than victory, and lay down in his clothes with the sword unsheathed beside the bed, and at last, near dawn, he slept.

* * *

Dawn brought the bell for prime and the realization that a military man in the palace would not be kept in a guest suite like a piece of wedding plate. Thiébaut sent a request, correctly through the marshal's office, to see the muster and the walls. Jeanne granted it and came down to the inner courtyard herself, in a riding gown and a short cloak, to find him already there in a plain leather coat, talking horseflesh with the master of the stable.

Brive arrived a moment later, armed and armored for a parade, and the two soldiers went over the guard of the gatehouse together with the instant freemasonry of men who had both stood watches no one paid them for. The old marshal had intended, Jeanne could tell, to be unimpressed. Ten minutes in, he was arguing pike drill with the Lorrainer as though they shared a tent.

"Your wall pieces are good," Thiébaut said, running a hand along the breech of a wrought-iron serpentine. "Better than I expected. But they're mounted on the old carriages, high and fixed. The French guns at the Roches-Noires were low, on wheels, and they moved between shots. A gun that cannot be re-aimed is a bell."

"The master of ordnance, Van Brock, says the same." Brive looked sour. "He has said it for a year. The treasury says carriages."

"The treasury will find the carriages cheaper than a breach." Thiébaut turned to Jeanne, including her without turning the talk into a lesson. "Madame, if you permit, I'll have my own master gunner draw the carriage plans for Van Brock. The Lorraine foundries cast for that pattern now."

"You may. I'll have the chancellor note it as a gift under the treaty's military clause." She kept her voice even. "We keep accounts of gifts, like everything else."

"I remember." The ghost of something, not quite a smile. "You kept accounts of the orchard apples. I believe I owed you seven."

It was the first personal thing he had said, and it landed in the courtyard so quietly that only she caught the full weight of it, a stone dropped into still water. Brive had moved on to the next gun. The stable grooms were occupied. For two breaths they were alone in a crowd.

"Six," she said. "You ate seven, but one was sour, and I credited it."

"A generous reign."

"A precise one." She turned away before her face could do anything. "The council sits at ten, my lord. My chancellor will bring the final marriage contract, and I expect you will wish to consult the Abbé de Viray. I am told he is in your train."

"He is. He is also, at this hour, discovering that your palace keeps a better cellar than Nancy's. At ten, madame."

* * *

She took the long gallery back to the state rooms, the one with the dead dukes in the wall niches, and he was there before her, having taken the shorter stair, and they met beneath the carved lion of her grandfather with only a pair of pages at the far end to keep the proprieties.

"Madame. A word, before ten o'clock, where there are no ledgers."

She stopped. The pages were too far to hear, near enough to see. "My lord."

"Your letter." He had it, she saw, in the inner pocket of his coat, worn soft at the folds; he carried it. "I have read it until I know it by heart, which is more than it deserves, since it was written by a woman determined to be cruel in the most correct language available. I will not pretend it did not wound me. I will pretend, for the sake of the house and the treaty and every ear in this palace, that it did not. But I will not have us begin on a falsehood. The girl you say is not at home is the reason I am here. The duchy is the reason I signed. Both can be true. Men are permitted more than one reason, even in a letter."

Jeanne's hands were cold again. She folded them to keep them still. "You are permitted reasons, my lord. I am permitted walls. You of all people should know I had good teachers in the building of them."

That got through. She saw it get through, the small recoil behind his eyes, the disciplined face holding. "Yes," he said. "I did teach you that. Among other things. I hope the other lessons served you better than that one."

"They all served me." She looked past him, at the carved lion, at the painted glass, at anything but the greenish hazel eyes. "At ten o'clock, my lord. The treaty. Nothing but the treaty. Whatever else either of us is carrying, it does not sit at the council table. I find governments are easier than history."

"Governments can be amended. History cannot. But I accept your terms. They are, after all, your house." He stepped aside, against the wall, the gesture of a man yielding a passage, and she walked between him and the carved stone with her veil just brushing his sleeve, and did not hurry, and did not look back, and reached the turn of the stair before she allowed herself to breathe.

In her chamber she found Lisette laying out the council gown, and the maid took one look at her face and asked no questions, which was the highest service Lisette owned. Jeanne sat at the table and uncapped the ink and wrote at the top of a fresh page, in the hand she used for the state, the agenda of the ten o'clock council: dower, revenues, troops, patents, style. She wrote them twice, because the first hand shook, and the second did not.

Then she rang for the chancellor and went down to make a marriage with the only man in Christendom she had ever loved and the only one she had ever feared, and she meant to get the better of the bargain, whatever it cost her.
