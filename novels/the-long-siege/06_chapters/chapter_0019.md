# Chapter Nineteen — The Siege of Sainterre

Graçay moved in the first week of June, and he moved the way a professional moves, without drama and without warning: three columns out of the forfeit fiefs, converging on the river roads, the vanguard under strong cavalry screens that ate the scout patrols and left the duchess's army blind in the one week of the year when visibility mattered. By the time Thiébaut's dispatches reached the Haut-Tertre, the French were across the upper Ortelle, and the war everyone had planned for over maps had become the war of actual dust and hoofbeats.

Jeanne was already in Sainterre. She had come for the midsummer charter council, the first full meeting of the elected aldermen under the sealed charter, and Coppens had prepared a banquet and a speech and a set of figures on the river trade that he considered more moving than any poetry. The news of the French advance reached the city at the same hour as the first refugees from the eastern villages, their carts heaped with bedding and their faces set in the particular expression of people who have done this before and hoped never to do it again.

The council met in the cloth hall through the night, and there was a moment, only a moment, when leaving remained possible. The river gate still stood open, the road west was clear, and the duchess's guard had fresh horses. Coppens put the case himself, heavily, because the case was heavy.

"Madame, if you stay, you are inside a city Graçay intends to take. If you go, you remain in the field with the army, and the war keeps its head. A duchess in a siege is a hostage with a crown. The aldermen will say so, off the record, every one of them."

"And on the record?"

"On the record, the city will hold because you are in it. You know that, or you wouldn't be asking." The old guildsman met her eyes. "I won't pretend it's an easy thing I'm saying. It's the truth. The garrison is twelve hundred, the militia six thousand on paper, the walls sound, the cisterns full. What the city lacks is a certainty that the charter means anything. Your person in the streets gives it that."

She sent the guard's captain with the answer to Thiébaut before she gave the answer to the hall, because she had learned the order of things: first the person whose treaty gave him the right to be heard, then the room. The letter was short. _Graçay's vanguard is a day from the walls. I am staying. Not for romance; for the charter and the customs and the reason this city is worth an army. Do not attempt a relief against a prepared ring; we both know that road. Make his war expensive and wait for the moment the ground gives. I will keep the city. You keep the field._

His answer came back before dawn, written in the hand she knew from a hundred dispatches, and shorter than hers. _The treaty's worst clause is the one that makes me obey. I have posted riders to every garrison. Hold, and write by every arrow._

Then the river gate was shut and barred and bricked up at the hinges, and Sainterre became a world with walls for its skyline.

* * *

Outside, Thiébaut watched the ring close and did the only thing an inferior field army can do against a siege: he made himself intolerable.

Graçay had perhaps eight thousand men, the guns, and the supply trains. The duke had four thousand, more mobility, and the population's goodwill across the countryside the French had to cross. He refused the battle the French offered on the flat ground below the city and instead took the army into the woods and river valleys, cutting convoys, burning supply dumps, hunting the foraging parties so aggressively that a French detachment could not leave its camp under company strength. The messengers between Paris and the siege lines began to need escorts of cavalry. Every night the French saw his watch-fires on different hills, and every morning some party of their men failed to return.

"It's not relief," he told the war council in the field camp, over a map pinned to an oak. "It's a tax. Every week he sits in front of Sainterre, I make him pay for the privilege. The Regent's treasury is already bleeding. The question is which of us runs out of money first, and I have a Venetian and he has Paris."

The arrow post was the strangest instrument of the siege, invented out of necessity and refined into a small, grim art. Every night, when the wind was right, the city's archers sent letters wrapped around bolts over the French lines into the nearest pickets, and the army's archers sent them back, and the French never fully stopped it, because a picket line is a sieve in the dark and a letter weighs nothing. The correspondence between the pavilion and the cloth hall became the siege's secret heartbeat: numbers, morale, the state of the wells, the price of bread, and at the bottom of each, in a hand only the two of them could read, the private line, the sentence that no clerk copied.

His first letter reached her on the third night. _The ring is strong on the south and east, weaker west where the ground is wet. I have marked his two big bombards; they move on Thursdays. Are you well? Do not answer only in figures._

She wrote back: _The bombardment has not begun. I am well and unpopular with the aldermen, which is the correct condition. Stop asking about my health in letters a sergeant could read._ And then, after a long pause, in the private hand: _I sleep with the green case's copy under my pillow. The revision continues. We are not finished, you and I._

* * *

Outside the ring, the duke's war ran on its own dark clock. Jean, healed but stiff, led the best of the mounted archers in the night raids, and the brothers planned them in the field pavilion over the same maps by candlelight, marking the convoys the scouts reported and the weak points in the French escort system.

The biggest raid came in the second week, against a grain convoy from the south, forty carts with a guard of two hundred. Jean took the van through a wood at midnight, the lances went in from both sides of the road, and the escort broke in the first ten minutes, the kind of fight that is decided by whichever side believes in the dark less. By dawn the convoy was theirs: the grain redirected along forest tracks to hidden stores, the carts burned, the French prisoners bound and the oxen driven off, and not a single sack had reached Graçay's camp.

"His bread bill just doubled," Jean said, watching the smoke. "How long before Paris asks why an eight-thousand-man siege is being fed by foraging?"

"Paris has already asked. Dolfin's friends send the replies." Thiébaut turned his horse toward the hidden camp. "But none of this matters if the city breaks. Every convoy I burn buys a week. She has to sell me the weeks."

The scouts and the arrow letters were his only window into Sainterre, and he read each one with his face under control and his hand white on the paper. Jean watched him do it and said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and because he had watched his brother carry this particular dread since he was twenty years old. But once, after a letter that mentioned the crossbow bolt two feet from her head, the Bastard said, "She is the most capable woman in Europe. She is also your wife. You are permitted to be afraid."

"I'm not afraid. I'm calculating. Fear is a tax on attention."

"You're afraid. I know the difference; I've been afraid next to you for fifteen years." Jean clapped his good shoulder. "She'll hold. She has a city full of people who've seen her carry stones. What does Graçay have? de Key and an invoice."

Thiébaut said nothing, but that night his letter went in with a longer postscript than usual, and the archer who carried it reported that the duke had stood watching the dark walls for an hour after the bolt was away.

* * *

The bombardment began on the fifth day, and it began the way all artillery war begins, with noise that rearranges the body's understanding of the world.

The two big bombards, the ones that moved on Thursdays, opened at dawn from the eastern rise, and the first stone shot struck the wall above the Ghent gate with a concussion she felt in her teeth at the cloth hall, half a mile away. The walls held; medieval stone was thick and the new guns were still learning their trade, and the aldermen had spent the winter shoring the foundations with earth and timber. But the houses behind the walls did not hold, and the first casualties were civilians in the eastern streets, where a shot overran the rampart and went through a weaver's cottage and the family eating their morning pottage.

Jeanne went to the eastern quarter at once, with Coppens and the surgeon and the guard, and she worked the rubble the way she had worked the hospital tents at Saint-Vaast, carrying stones and binding wounds and saying the names when the bodies came out. It was the correct government and the correct example, and she knew it, and did it anyway, because there was no other way. By midday the quarter had a system: the houses nearest the wall emptied into the cellars and the church crypts, the fire-watch organized street by street, the children moved to the western cellars where the shot could not reach. The city, which had feared the guns as a beast fears thunder, began the long civilian labor of living inside their sound.

The rationing was Coppens's work and her signature. Grain was inventoried house by house in the first week, the merchants' stores entered on the public books at fixed prices, and the bread ration set by category: soldiers and laborers first, then the guild households, then the rest, with the children and the sick under a separate list that Jeanne supervised herself. Hoarding was punished by seizure and a public reading of the offender's name, which in Sainterre was a worse penalty than the seizure. The water was the deeper fear. The river ran outside the walls, and the French knew it; their archers covered the quays, and the city wells, though sufficient, were old and shallow. By the second week the first cases of the flux appeared in the lower town, and the cistern water was boiled whether people liked the taste or not, and the hospital of Saint-Géry became the busiest place in the city.

De Key came back to them in the second week, carried in through the French lines under a flag of truce, and the city discovered what its former prophet had become.

He arrived at the cloth hall in French-cut clothes, with a French clerk and a French paymaster's confidence, and he asked to address the aldermen and the guilds, and Jeanne let him, because forbidding the speech would make it sacred. He spoke in the same deep hall-filling voice, but the message had changed. The war was lost; the duchess's army could not break the ring; the city's trade was dying by the day; Paris offered the ancient freedoms, the tolls, the militia, everything the charter had given and more, under the crown's guarantee. He did not say that he held a French captain's commission. The French-cut clothes said it for him.

She answered him from the same platform, in the same voice she had used in this square before the riot, and she did not raise it. "He asks you to trade a sealed charter for a promise. I have the charter in this hall, under my hand and the city's seal. Where is the promise written, and who seals it? The Sire de Graçay? Ask the people of Sainte-Florence what his promises are worth. Ask the thirty-one militiamen hanged in their charter uniforms. This man wears French cloth and eats French bread and asks you to pay for both with your gates. The city will vote, as a free city may, and I will abide by its vote. But I will record it, and so will the history of this guild."

The guilds voted by roaring acclamation rather than ballot, and de Key left the hall under hoots, and Jeanne watched him go with the cold knowledge that the vote had settled the city's spirit but not its danger. A prophet who has lost his audience becomes a man with a grievance and a paymaster, and the French had uses for such men.

* * *

A siege, Jeanne learned, was not a battle but a household, the same work as the Haut-Tertre stripped of every ornament and enlarged to the scale of a city: water, bread, fire, sickness, the removing of waste, the keeping of watches, the morale of people who had expected to be ordinary. The aldermen ran the machinery; she ran the aldermen, and between them they built, in the first fortnight, the small republic of the besieged.

The women were its spine. The guild wives organized themselves by parish into crews under the duchess's own commission, sealed and dated so that no man could call it meddling: water crews for the walls and the fires, linen crews who tore the city's spare cloth into bandages, stone crews who carried rubble to the breaches, and the crews of the hospital of Saint-Géry who did the work no one else could bear. Jeanne worked beside them in the first days until Coppens, with the bluntness she paid him for, told her the duchess had better uses than a strong back, and after that she went the rounds every day on foot, and the rounds themselves became an institution, the woman in the plain grey gown appearing in every quarter at every hour, knowing the names, checking the lists, eating the ration bread in public so the city could see its duchess ate no better than a fuller.

Verhoeven's press ran in the cellar through the bombardment, the type rattled by the concussions, and the weekly sheet came out even when its printer did not sleep. It carried the true casualty figures, the ration schedules, the dispatches from the field army, and, at Jeanne's insistence, the names: every defender killed, listed by parish and guild, the book of the dead translated into print and read aloud at every market cross. A city that saw its dead named in print, she had argued, would not surrender their names to silence. The sheet also carried, in every edition, a short section of the charter's text, so that the people being asked to die for a document knew exactly what the document said.

The merchant class was the hardest problem. The great traders had the most to lose and the strongest case for an early accommodation, and two of them were caught in the third week sending price signals to the French by the arrangement of their rooftop cloths. Jeanne tried them before the city court, not her own, and let Coppens pronounce the sentence: seizure of the stock and expulsion through the river postern, under flag of truce, to go and live under the government they had been advertising. One of them, a cloth merchant named Vanisse, begged at the last for mercy, and she gave him the answer she had worked out in advance for the whole watching city.

"I do not punish opinion. You may believe the French will win; half of Europe believes it. I punish signals sent to an enemy's guns while your neighbors' sons hold the walls. Go. If you are right, you'll return in triumph and I'll be in a cell. If you are wrong, the city has your stock for the common bread."

The story was in the next sheet, and after it the rooftop cloths all hung in the ordinary way.

The flux took its own toll in the cellars, where the displaced families lived on boiled water and thin bread, and the hospital filled with the sweating, shitting sick alongside the wounded. There were no medicines beyond the old ones, willow bark and vinegar and the herbal lore of the convent women, and the dead of disease outnumbered the dead of the guns by the third week, as they did in every siege in Christendom. Jeanne's hardest daily work was the hospital, where the smell never left her clothes and the children recognized her step and called her by the name they had made up, the Grey Woman, because of the gown.

* * *

The first assault came on the twenty-first day, at the point the bombardment had been weakening for a fortnight: the curtain wall east of the Ghent gate, where the stone had crumbled into a ragged slope of rubble that the French had begun to fill with fascines and earth.

They came before dawn, in the old formal sequence: the crossbowmen to the edge of the ditch to drive the walls clear, the miners and laborers with their bundles, the storming columns behind with ladders and the new scaling stairs. The night sky over the eastern rise went red with the alarm fires, and the city's bells began, and Jeanne climbed the Ghent gate tower with the garrison commander because there was nowhere else the duchess could be on such a morning, and she had learned at the windmill by the ford that the watching was also work.

The fight lasted four hours and cost the city ninety-seven men.

She saw it in pieces through the smoke: the French crosses filling the ditch, the ladders going up and coming down, the militia holding the rubble with pikes the Lorraine captains had trained them to use, the gunners working the wall pieces at close range until two of the guns split their barrels. She saw a boy her own guard's age climb a ladder with a banner and be thrown off it by a stone, and the banner fall with him and be caught before it touched the ground. She carried water and bandages and the spare bolts along the wall-walk with the other women, and once a crossbow bolt struck the stone two feet from her head and whined away into the dark, and she did not scream, because she had decided in advance that she would not, and decisions made in advance are the only kind that hold at such hours.

By full morning the French had drawn back, leaving the ditch full of their dead and the ladders against the wall and the smell of smoke and blood over the whole eastern quarter, and the city's bells changed from alarm to the rough, exhausted sound of survival, and the militia on the wall began, of all things, to sing, a guild drinking song with the words changed for the occasion, and the song ran along the ramparts like fire along a fuse.

Coppens came up the tower stairs, bleeding from a cut on his scalp, and looked at her with the face of a man who has just discovered reserves he did not know he held.

"They'll come again, madame."

"Yes. They'll come again, and again, until the money or the food runs out. That's the whole of a siege." She looked out over the ditch and the French lines and the watch-fires of the distant army where her husband's banners moved among the hills. "So we make sure ours runs out second."

The cellar of the merchant's house became her school in what bombardment did to people who had no part in the war.

It was the cellar nearest the eastern wall, deep and vaulted, where thirty families had taken their blankets and their bread and their frightened children, and the aldermen had asked her to visit because the keeper of the ration lists had broken down in tears there the night before and no one could make the columns add. She came after the hospital rounds, with two guards who waited at the top of the stairs, and sat on a flour barrel in the lamplight and did the lists herself, and the cellar, which had gone quiet at the sight of the duchess, slowly found its voices.

A woman with a baby at her breast asked, without accusation, whether the charter would still mean anything if the French came through the wall, and Jeanne told her the charter was a document in three cities and a treaty in Venice as well as a paper in Sainterre, which was a better answer than the one she felt like giving. An old weaver, one of de Key's original guild, said he had voted against the war contribution and now watched his own grandson carrying sand to the ramparts, and he asked whether she found that amusing, and she said no, she found it the cost of being wrong in public, which he had paid and she honored. A girl of perhaps seven, with the flat, serious face of the chronically underfed, asked her whether the guns ever stopped, and Jeanne said they stopped when the city made stopping cheaper than firing, and the child considered this and said she would tell the others, as though the duchess had handed her a commission.

When the next bombardment began, close enough that dust sifted from the vault, she stayed. The cellar flinched as one body at each concussion, the babies wailing, the women holding the smaller children, and she sat on her flour barrel and held the hand of the nearest woman and said nothing at all, because there was nothing to say, and when the guns moved off at last the keeper of the ration lists came to her in the new quiet and said he could do the columns now, and did them, with his hands still shaking.

Coppens met her at the top of the stairs. "You'll be expected at every bombardment now. The whole cellar will be talking by morning."

"Then let them say the duchess sat in the dust with them. It's the cheapest propaganda in the war, and it happens to be true." She brushed the flour from her gown. "Keep the cellars provisioned and the lists in order. The walls are stone, but the siege is won in those rooms."

* * *

The treachery inside the walls showed its hand two nights later, the way treachery always does, through a small and greedy man. A watchman at the water gate, one of de Key's old weavers, was caught by the gate's militia sergeant in the act of oiling the barrier's bar and marking the postern with a chalk cross, and under the city's questioning he gave up the whole small web: five men, a plan to open the water gate on the next moonless night, and de Key's name on the pay, passed through a French deserter in the lower town.

Jeanne insisted the trial be the city's. The aldermen sat in the cloth hall with their charter on the table, the evidence was read aloud, and the five were condemned by their own neighbors under their own law, and hanged at dawn from the gate they had meant to open, in the red armbands they had kept since the year of the riot. The watchman, who had turned king's evidence, got the city's mercy and a place in the stone crews for the duration, and was the most thoroughly watched man in Sainterre.

"De Key's name wasn't on a paper he touched," Coppens said, walking back with her through the waking streets. "He'll deny it from the French camp."

"He will. But the city has now hanged its own traitors under its own charter. That's worth more to me than his neck. The aldermen didn't execute my enemies this morning. They executed their own. There's a difference, and the difference is why the wall holds."

The French answered with a second bombardment, longer and heavier, and a herald under parley who read out Graçay's terms at the Ghent gate: the city's life and goods and the ancient freedoms if it opened within ten days, and the fate of Saintes if it did not. The crowd heard him in silence, and when he had done, the militia captain on the wall shouted down the one reply the city had agreed in advance, and the whole gate watch roared it after him: "The charter!"

That night the arrow post carried his letter: _I counted the assault columns from the ridge. Your wall held, and I know who held it. The French lost, by my scouts, four hundred for a ditch. I have never been prouder of a document in my life. Sleep if you can. I cannot._

She wrote back: _The charter is holding the wall. I am merely keeping the books. Send powder. We have more courage than saltpeter._

And underneath, in the private hand, she added: _I have revised several more pages. When this is over, you and I have a great deal to say, and I intend to say all of it. Stay alive to hear it._
