# Chapter Six — The Treaty of Everyone But Them

She was at the table by seven, with the fire newly lit and Colard yawning into his sleeve, and the contract between them under a weight of the late duke's dagger. Foulques arrived as the cathedral bell struck, immaculate as ever, carrying a second folder tied with green ribbon.

"The history of the reversion clause," he said, sitting. "You asked for it in writing. Three precedents. The marriage of 'fifty-one, where the widow's duchy was swallowed entire; the Hainaut settlement of 'sixty-four, which our side should have copied; and the Brabant case of 'seventy, where the children's inheritance nearly cost a county a war. I have marked the passages."

Jeanne turned the pages by the window's grey light. "He drafted a reversion that protects me. His own chancellor must have bled."

"Viray bleeds professionally. The question, madame, is why the duke permits it. Councils do not give away claims their masters want."

"Perhaps he does not want claims. Perhaps he wants a working ally and knows a stolen duchy is the least working thing in Christendom." She closed the folder. "Or perhaps he is more patient than the rest of them and intends the marriage itself to do what the clauses do not. Maître Foulques, I have asked you before and I will ask you once more, because today is the last day I can ask cheaply. Is there anything about the Duke of Lorraine I do not know? Anything at all? You were my father's hand in the business of that year. If there is a debt, a woman, a promise, a written thing—"

The chancellor's face did not change. It never did. But she had learned to watch his hands, and his hands straightened the green ribbon twice. "There is nothing that touches the treaty, madame. The man is poor, hard-pressed, and honest in his public dealings, which in a ruler is nearly a vice. As for the rest," he selected the words like a man selecting stones, "some doors are locked by people other than the persons standing outside them. I would not have you marry under a mistake, but neither can I give you a key I was never permitted to hold. Today, negotiate the treaty. Leave the man for after."

It was the closest he had ever come to the wall he kept around that year, and she filed it, as she filed everything, and let the bell finish striking. "Very well. Colard, the dower figures again. If the abbé starts at fifteen percent, I will weep into my wine and accept ten."

Colard, who had never seen her weep over anything but her father's vault, bowed over his ledgers.

The ten o'clock council filled the round chamber with the particular silence of two sets of lawyers preparing to be difficult about money. On the Vauréal side sat Foulques with his clerks, Colard with his ledgers, Brive in steel as a matter of principle, and Jeanne in the high chair. On the Lorraine side the Abbé de Viray arranged his papers like a chess player, the Bastard Jean lounged against the wall with the air of a man attending for amusement, and Thiébaut sat upright on a plain stool with nothing in front of him at all, which Jeanne noticed because every other man in the room had armed himself with documents.

"Shall we begin with the spiritual clauses," Foulques said, "or the temporal?"

"Begin with the temporal," said Thiébaut. "The spiritual ones are only money wearing a surplice."

Jean laughed. Viray permitted himself a small pained smile. Foulques, who had prepared a speech, discarded it and opened the marriage contract at the military schedule.

For an hour they ground through the troop clauses. Lorraine's obligation: twelve hundred lances and three thousand foot for the duration of the French war, at Vauréal's expense, mustered at Pont-d'Ortelle by the feast of Saint John. The abbé, who had clearly been saving himself, objected to the mustering point, the expense schedule, the phrase _duration of the war_ ("a war, madame, may be ended by a peace that the duchy of Lorraine is not a party to"), and above all to Jeanne's added clause doubling the quota in the event of direct invasion.

"Direct invasion is the entire point of the instrument," Viray said, with the patience of a man explaining water to a fish. "My master's whole force is perhaps four thousand effectives. Your clause would strip Lorraine of every garrison it possesses and place the whole army of an independent duchy under a war council headed by a lady who, with every respect, has never campaigned."

"The duchess heads the war council because the war is in the duchess's country." Foulques's tone was silky. "As for her inexperience, the abbot will observe that the clause does not require her to swing a sword, only to hold the council. The duke of Lorraine retains field command."

"Jointly," Thiébaut said.

Every head turned. He had not spoken in twenty minutes.

"The clause says jointly, and jointly is what I will accept," he continued, looking at Viray. "Abbé, the alternative is her treasury paying an army it does not trust, and a duchess who spends the war watching her own back instead of the French. I will give the orders in the field, but no major engagement, no siege, no withdrawal without the war council's seal. Write it that way. It costs us nothing and gives them a lock."

Viray's mouth opened, closed, and made a small note, the gesture of a man filing a grievance for another day. Brive stroked his white beard and looked at the Lorrainer with the first thing resembling approval Jeanne had seen on the marshal's face.

* * *

The dower clause took longer. Viray wanted the dower revenues collected by Lorraine officers and remitted quarterly to her household. Jeanne's addition required payment into an account controlled by Colard, in Vauréal coin, at the Malemort counting house, with penalty interest for late payment and a lien on the Lorraine saltworks as security.

"It is an insult," the abbé said.

"It is a dower," said Jeanne. "Insults are free. Dowers are enforced. My lord abbot, you are a priest and a chancellor, and you know as well as I do that every widowed duchess of the last century who lost her revenue lost it through the courtesy of husband's officers. I will not be maintained by kindness. I will be maintained by contract."

"She has you there," Jean said from the wall. "Our own aunt was maintained by kindness for nine years. She died of it in a convent."

Viray shot his duke's half-brother a look that would have felled a smaller man and turned back to the table. "The saltworks lien is impossible. It touches the regalian rights—"

"The saltworks stay in." Thiébaut did not look up from the map he was studying. "Strike the penalty interest above ten percent. She'll accept that; it's the figure her own bank charges. Madame?"

Jeanne kept her face still. It was the second time he had read her clause as though he had written it, and in a sense he had, because he had already thought through where she would stop. "Ten percent. Agreed."

The patents clause followed, and here the room expected a fight, because it was the clause that made her sovereign in fact rather than in song: all offices, garrison commands, and patents in Vauréal to issue solely from her hand, including over any Lorrainer serving within her borders. Viray argued that a duke whose troops garrisoned her fortresses must name their captains.

"Then his troops may camp outside the fortresses," Jeanne said. "I do not garrison my own walls with men who hold their commands from another sovereign. The captains are mine, on my lord duke's recommendation, and I will not often refuse him, but the patent is mine. There is no compromise available in this clause, because it is not a clause about captains. It is a clause about whose country this is."

She had expected the Lorrainers to press. She had expected, privately, a long afternoon. Thiébaut merely said, "Granted," before Viray could draw breath, and then, to his chancellor, "Abbé, the next time a French army crosses our border, ask the walls whose country they are. The answer will be the same as hers."

The inheritance clause brought the only heat of the morning, because it was the clause about the future's dead. If the marriage had issue, the children would inherit both houses, with the actual government of each passing only at majority, under separate regencies; if it had none, Vauréal followed its own ancient succession and Lorraine its own, and neither house might claim the other's soil by marriage right. Viray wanted a joint regency named in advance, with the surviving spouse at its head.

"That is the Brabant error," Foulques said, and produced the green-ribbon folder with the air of a man unveiling a weapon. "A joint regency is an auction in which the child's crown is sold to whichever parent's council moves first. The Hainaut model: separate regencies, native councils, the child's person in the keeping of the duchy it currently resides in. It held for eleven years of minority."

"It held," Viray countered, "because the Hainaut dowager was a saint. Ordinarily, madame, a widowed mother in a foreign court is the weakest political creature in Europe. My master asks only that if the worst befall, he keep his child and his child's interests together."

"If the worst befall," Jeanne said, "I will be dead, and my lord duke's interests will be the security of Lorraine, which is as it should be. My child's interests in Vauréal will be the Vauréal succession, which cannot be governed from Nancy without becoming Nancy's. Separate regencies. Native councils. The child's person follows the Vauréal court until the age of seven, and the Lorraine court after, since the schooling of princes belongs with the father's house. I will give you that much, and the marriage contract will name the visiting rights in full. Is it enough?"

Thiébaut looked at Viray; Viray looked at the ceiling, seeking patience from a God who had dealt him many difficult mornings. "It is more than Hainaut. Write it."

The last addition was style, and here for the first time Foulques himself looked uneasy. She would be styled Duchess of Vauréal in her own right in both countries, in every document, on every seal and herald's tongue, never the duke's wife. No title of hers would pass to him. The marriage would create no regency, no guardianship, no curtailment.

"The precedent," Foulques murmured, "is unusual."

"The precedent is necessary." Jeanne looked around the table. "My vassals are deciding this month whether they are sworn to a person or to a marriageable property. Let them see the property cannot be married out of its own name. My lord duke? You have said nothing."

Thiébaut set down the map at last. "I have nothing to say against a thing I have already agreed to twice. My lords and my clerks, understand it plainly, so that no one misreports it in his own interest: I am marrying a sovereign, not acquiring a sovereignty. The duchess reigns here. I reign in Nancy. The treaty binds the two reigns. It does not fuse them. If that is not the document on the table, I will not sign the one that is."

In the silence that followed, Jeanne looked at him with the same feeling she had had in the courtyard: the disconcerting sense that the man across the table had already had this argument, alone, and won it against himself.

* * *

At midday they broke for bread and wine, and the clerks went to fair-copy the agreed clauses, and the principals were left with a short, carefully witnessed interval in the window embrasure, with Foulques and Viray ten paces away in deep and ostentatious conversation about cathedral music.

"You concede more than your council likes," Jeanne said, low. "Why?"

"Because my council is thinking of the wedding and I am thinking of the war." He kept his eyes on the courtyard below, where the Lorraine lances were being shown the well. "A marriage contract that gives me a grip on your government is a contract your vassals spend five years sawing through, while the French wait for the crack. I want a tool that holds, not a title that rusts. And, madame," he paused, choosing it, "there are cheaper ways to buy an obedient duchess. The market is full of fifteen-year-olds with empty claims. I came for the one who keeps her own ledgers."

"You came for the road."

"I came for several things, and I have told you that men may have more than one reason. The road is among them. So is the fact that your wall guns need new carriages and your marshal is sixty-three and your treasury borrows at fourteen percent and your nearest lord is writing letters to Paris. You need me. I need you. The only open question between us is whether we do this well or badly, and I do not intend to do anything badly for the rest of my life, which I expect to be short if this fails."

It was the plainest speech any man had made to her since her father died, and she did not trust it, which she also marked in the ledger. "And the past? You ask me to bind myself for life to a man who once stood in my father's hall and—"

"And told a room full of witnesses that you were a foolish child." His voice had gone lower still, almost to itself. "Madame, I cannot undo that morning. I cannot even explain it to your satisfaction, not here, not now, perhaps not ever. I will say only this, and then I will not say it again while the treaty stands between us: that morning was not what it looked like, and there are documents in this world that lie by telling the truth. If the treaty is all you can ever bring yourself to have of me, I will take it and serve it and keep your borders, and you will never hear the word _once_ from my lips. But I did not ride through a forest of hired killers to collect a document. You may decide what I came for, in the end. You get to decide everything. I made sure of it in the clauses."

She had no answer ready. She had prepared for every argument except the argument that left her the judge. The silence lengthened, saved by the return of the clerks with their fair copies and their fresh ink, and the moment went under the surface like a stone in dark water.

* * *

They signed in the afternoon, in the great hall, before the assembled vassals and envoys, because every signature in this country was a performance. The documents lay on a table under the arms of Vauréal and the alérions of Lorraine, and the seals went down in red wax, and the heralds proclaimed the league and the coming marriage, and the company applauded in the careful measure of people calculating how the news would land elsewhere.

Jeanne watched her court through her lashes while the trumpets sounded. Hautperthuis, in the front rank, wore the face of a man at a feast who has found a hair in his soup and intends to eat smiling; his gloved hands clapped twice and stopped. The Erquelines nephew clapped with the earnestness of a man composing a letter to his uncle. The Sainterre deputies, three guildsmen admitted for the occasion, clapped like merchants pricing a cargo. Von Zollern, the imperial, did not clap at all, and wrote something in a little book. Dolfin, at the back, smiled at her the way a banker smiles at a loan he has already decided to grant, and she understood that the Venetian had been certain of the signature before she had.

The formal betrothal followed at the cathedral door, per verba de praesenti under the bishop's eye, the words that made them contractually one before God while leaving the sacrament for the wedding. Thiébaut placed the ring on the book and then on her finger, and his knuckle grazed hers, and the contact went through her like a bell through a house. Neither of them looked at the other during the vows, which the court read as dignity and she read as self-defense.

* * *

Dolfin requested an audience before the sun was down, and received it in the treasury office, the room with no tapestries and one chair for the borrower, which was her father's little joke and now hers. The Venetian came with a single clerk and an iron-bound chest, and he did not waste the hour on congratulations.

"The first installment, ten thousand écus, weighed and sealed." He set the chest's inventory on the table. "The gold reaches Malemort by barge on the tenth, under my house's guard and my house's banner, which even the French do not care to rob, since they owe my partners in Paris. My congratulations, by the way, are genuine. A marriage treaty this sensible is rare enough to be a collector's item."

"Your house's terms improved the moment his signature went on it." Jeanne turned the inventory. "The interest fell from fourteen to twelve and a half before the ink dried. I had not yet written to accept."

"You had not yet needed to. The treaty changed the collateral. A Lorraine army in being is worth one and a half percent in Venice; I could show you the tables." Dolfin folded his hands. "Madame, permit me a second piece of arithmetic, since the first has served us both. The loan will pay the troops and the guns through one campaign season. It will not pay for a lost city and a long war. Whatever happens at Sainterre, end it quickly. Revolts are like pregnancies; they become much more expensive after the first signs show."

"You have a low opinion of cities, Messer Dolfin."

"I have a precise opinion of cities, which is rarer. Sainterre does not want freedom. Sainterre wants its tolls fixed and its own men in its courts, and it will call those things freedom and die for them cheerfully. Give it the things. Keep the war taxes. A contented city that pays is the best collateral in Europe; ask my partners in Florence, who own one." He rose. "I will also, if you permit, advance the wedding feast's costs against the next installment. A duchess who pays her fishmongers on the day of her marriage is a duchess the lower town believes in. Credit, madame, is only belief with a ledger."

When he had gone, Colard stared at the chest as though it might bite. "He gives advice like a member of the council."

"He gives advice like a man who owns a mortgage on the council's house. Write his every suggestion into the minutes. If his arithmetic works, we follow it and he takes the pride; if it fails, we have his handwriting." She locked the inventory away. "The gold to Malemort, and a strong escort of our own household alongside his banner. I want no story that Vauréal's war is paid by Venetian guard alone."

That evening the dispatches came, two of them, hard on each other's heels like dogs at a gate.

The first was from Coppens at Sainterre, and it was barely a letter at all: de Key's faction had taken the cloth hall, armed the weavers' guild with crossbows, and declared that no charter sealed to a foreign husband would be recognized by the city. Two of the moderates had been dragged through the streets. Coppens himself was under siege in his own house, and the city gates were held by the weavers. He wanted soldiers or he wanted instructions, and he made it plain he expected neither in time.

The second was from the commandant of the southern marches, shorter and colder: a French column under Graçay's vanguard had crossed the fords in strength, seized the bridge-fort of Saint-Vaast by the treachery of its own sergeant, and put the garrison to the sword. The road to Mont d'Or lay open. The commandant had forty-seven men and intended, he wrote, to die at his post if the duchess had no better use for him.

Foulques read both aloud to the council, and the room was very quiet.

"So much for the season of weddings," Brive said.

The argument began at once, the argument that every divided map produces. Brive wanted the feudal host, raw as it was, sent south to the fords before the French could push beyond Saint-Vaast. Viray wanted the Lorraine companies held intact and marched on Sainterre, because a city with crossbows and a printing press was a more dangerous enemy than a single captured bridge-fort. Colard wanted neither, because neither could be paid for past midsummer, and said so in the voice of a man reading his own death sentence.

Thiébaut let them run for five minutes, studying the wall map with his hands behind his back, and then he said, "The marshal loses the south, the abbot loses the north, and the treasurer loses the war. May I?"

He did not wait for permission. He crossed to the map and took the pointer from Foulques.

"Graçay is a professional. He will not march on Aubecourt from Saint-Vaast; he lacks the guns and the supply, and the Regent has not yet paid for a war of conquest. He means to digest the southern fiefs through the summer and make the war legal before he makes it large. The real danger in the south is therefore not the French army. It is the example. Every wavering lord with a road to Paris is watching to see whether the new duchess can make a French capture costly. Mont d'Or must be reinforced and visibly provisioned, which says to the waverers that the duchess still answers her mail."

He moved the pointer north.

"Sainterre is the war. A city of forty thousand that seizes its own gates in your first month is a story told in every guildhall from Bruges to Venice. If de Key holds it a season, Malemort follows, Grisay follows, and your revenue dies in its bed while Graçay is still writing polite letters. You cannot storm Sainterre; you have no siege train and the burghers would burn the cloth stores first. You cannot ignore it; the silence kills you. You go to its gates with enough force to be respected and enough charter to be accepted, and you let the city's own moderates win the argument for you. Which means, madame, that the wedding comes first, and comes fast, because only a married alliance marches into Sainterre without looking like a desperate woman's gamble."

He set the pointer down.

"The forty-seven men at the southern post get a letter, a purse, and a replacement sergeant with twenty good troops, and we make a ballad out of their commandant's fidelity, because fidelity is cheaper than castles and worth more this month. The host assembles as planned. The Lorraine companies, when the gold arrives, go north with us, not south. The south holds by reputation until the north is settled. Those are my recommendations. The war council is yours."

The room was quiet again, but it was a different silence. Jeanne looked at Brive, and saw the old marshal swallow his first plan whole and nod, slowly, like a man conceding a point of swordwork.

Jeanne looked at the map on the round chamber's wall, at the two new fires burning at opposite ends of her inheritance, and then at the man she had promised to marry, who was already on his feet, already thinking in roads and days.

"Can you be married by Sunday?" she asked him.

"The banns—" Foulques began.

"Special license. The bishop owes me three favors and I am calling in the largest. Sunday, in the cathedral, full court, every vassal who can be made to attend. A wedding is a muster with better clothes, and I will not go to war as a betrothed woman with half my lords pretending the alliance is provisional. My lord duke? Sunday?"

Thiébaut's mouth did the thing that was not quite a smile. "Madame, I have been ready since I was twenty. Sunday."

* * *

That night she sat late with the ledger, while beyond the connecting wall the new occupant of the guest suite read dispatches from his own war. She could hear his chair, his step, the low murmur of his voice dictating to a clerk. Once, through the oak, she caught a laugh, brief and dry, at something the Bastard Jean had said.

She wrote the day's entry: the contract signed, the terms won, the betrothal sworn, the revolt and the fort and the Sunday wedding. Then she sat with the pen over the page and added, in the small hand no clerk copied, the thing she would not have said aloud in any room: _He gave me every clause I asked for, and three I did not dare ask for. I do not know whether to be grateful or afraid. I think in this house the two are the same._

Beyond the wall, the murmuring stopped. A board creaked, close, as though someone had sat down on the other side of the door. She held her breath. Then the sound moved away, and his footsteps crossed the room, and she heard him begin to dictate again, steadily, the orders for the road north.

She closed the ledger and went to bed, and for the first time in seven years she did not lock the connecting door, though she left the key in it, turned against him.
