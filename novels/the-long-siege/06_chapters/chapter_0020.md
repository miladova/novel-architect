# Chapter Twenty — The Sallies

By the fortieth day Sainterre had eaten its horses.

The rationing held because the books held, but the books could not invent grain. The fixed price became a fixed hope; the bread shrank from a loaf to a wedge and from a wedge to a dark cut of bean-and-rye that tasted of the grit in the millstones. The cavalry mounts went first, by public decree, the duchess's own grey mare among them, and Coppens saw to it that the meat was divided by the parishes and not by rank, which single act of the knife kept the city's peace through a week when another city would have torn itself apart. Cats disappeared from the gutters, then the dogs, then the swans on the canal, and the news sheet recorded every measure in its flat clerk's voice, because a city that can read its hunger in print is a city that understands the hunger has a shape and an ending.

Jeanne's day had taken on the rhythm of the walls. Before dawn, the hospital of Saint-Géry, where the night's dead were counted and the morning's medicines issued. Then the wall-walk with the garrison commander, the inspection of the guns and the powder store, the numbers written in her own hand. Then the rationing courts, the disputes, the aldermen, the long middle hours of keeping a city civilized by willpower. Afternoon, the crews: the women's water-and-sand crews now three hundred strong, the stone crews, the boys' messenger corps, the old men's fire watch. Evening, the letters, the dispatches, the arrow post, the private line at the bottom of the page. And night, the few hours of sleep on a pallet in the cloth hall cellar, with the guns in her dreams and the list of the dead waiting for the morning.

She had not told anyone about the nausea. It had begun in the third week, the small reliable sickness at dawn and the strange, altered smell of the hospital, and she had put it down to the ration bread and the strain until the accounting in her own body caught up with the accounting in her mind: the courses that had not come in March, or April, or now June; the fullness and tenderness she had been ignoring through the campaign. The convent physician, an old nun named Sister Margriet who had delivered half the guildsmen of Sainterre and feared neither blood nor duchesses, confirmed it in a cellar room with one candle, in the plain trade language of her profession.

"Two months gone, perhaps a little more. The body does not keep precise ledgers, madame, even when its owner does. Strong heart, good color in spite of everything, and the sickness is a healthy sign, though you won't think so at dawn. What you must not do is starve it. I will see to a separate ration, and I will hear no argument about the example."

"Then we will make the example do double work." Jeanne sat with her hands folded in her lap and the knowledge settling through her like a stone through still water. "The city's children and its pregnant women eat from the same list, including the duchess. If my extra ration is known, it is known as the rule, not the privilege. And Sister — not a word beyond this room. Not to the aldermen, not to the guard. The city is carrying one fear already. It does not need mine."

The old woman studied her. "The father should know."

"The father is four miles away with an army and reads every letter I send. He'll know by the week's end. He has earned the right to worry at the same time as I do."

* * *

The second assault, when it came, was aimed at the river wall, and Marcenay led it.

He had arrived in Graçay's camp after the failure at Sainte-Florence with his eighty survivors and his undamaged self-regard, and the French commander, who had small use for the man but a use for the reputation, had given him the storming column and the point where the river wall had been weakened by a mine the French engineers had run under the outer ward. The ground above the mine was propped with timber the besiegers could fire at the chosen hour, and the chosen hour was the dark before dawn on the forty-third day, in the fog off the river.

Jeanne was woken by the ground moving. The mine went up with a deep, dull roar rather than the crack of the guns, and a section of the outer wall slumped into the ditch in a landslide of stone and earth, and through the smoke and the fog the French storming party came at the gap with their ladders and their fascines, and behind them, shouting in a voice the sentries would later repeat with loathing, Marcenay drove his men on.

The outer ward fell within the hour. It was not a wall of houses, only the lower enclosure, the store yards and the tanners' sheds and the church of the old foundation, but its loss put the French inside the first skin of the city, and the inner gate became the line, and the alarm bells of Sainterre rang from every tower at once, and the night became a chaos of smoke and running feet and the red light of the tanners' sheds going up.

The women's refuge was the crypt of the old church, and it was directly in the path.

She learned it from a breathless messenger boy, one of her own corps, who found her at the inner gate organizing the sand crews: the outer ward's fleeing civilians, the women and children and the wounded from the forward hospital, had been herded into the crypt, and Marcenay's free company, pouring through the ward, had found the church and put a guard on its doors. What happened in the next hour belonged to the logic of men who had done it before, at Sainte-Florence and a dozen unrecorded villages, and the messenger did not need to spell it out. The faces of the women within earshot did that.

Jeanne made the decision in the time it took to turn from the gate. The inner wall's defense belonged to the garrison commander and was in good hands; the breach could be held for an hour. The crypt could not.

"Coppens. The women's crews, every crew within call, and the militia reserve from the cloth hall. Bring pikes, knives, the fire buckets, and every crossbow from the armory that a woman can shoulder. We're going to the church."

"Madame, you can't—"

"I can, and I will, and the city will see me do it." She was already moving, the grey gown kilted into her belt, the dagger at her hip, and the word ran through the streets like a current: the Grey Woman was going to the church, and the women of Sainterre were going with her.

They came from the cellars and the wall-walks and the hospital, three hundred and then four, guild wives in their leather work-jerkins, servant girls with kitchen knives, the widows of the first assault carrying the pikes their husbands had used, and Jeanne put them into the street column the way the Lorraine captains had taught her to put men into a formation: the crossbows in front under the three militia women who could actually shoot, the pikewomen in the center, the fire-and-sand crews behind, and at the head, because there was nowhere else for her to be, the duchess herself with a steel cap she had taken from a dead man and a dagger rather than a sword, because she had never been taught the sword and refused to pretend otherwise.

"Listen to me." She spoke low, and the word passed back through the column. "We are not charging an army. We are taking back a building. We move at the walk, in order, and no one breaks for any reason. They are drunk and disorganized and do not expect a formed body coming the other way. At the church, the crossbows take the door guard. The pikes hold the street. The rest of you get the women and children out through the crypt's rear passage to the canal. I go in with the first file. Is that understood?"

It was understood.

* * *

The outer ward burned along its northern edge, and the fog and smoke made every street a room without walls. They moved through it in their dark clothes with the measured pace of a guild procession, and the first French foragers they met, three men with armfuls of stolen plate, simply stared at the column of women as though at a vision, and were taken from behind by the pikewomen before they could shout.

The church square was worse than the messenger had described. A dozen of Marcenay's sergeants held the doors, and through the broken windows came the sounds from inside, the sounds Jeanne had read in the Dominican's book and sworn she would not hear in a city of hers. The door guards had their backs to the street, watching the church, and the crossbow women's first volley took four of them down at thirty paces, and the rest turned in disbelief into the lowered pikes.

It was not a battle. It was the application of order to appetite, and order won as it usually does when it arrives in time. The sergeants were professional enough to understand a formed body and drunk enough to be slow against it; they gave ground, then broke, two cut down in the square and the rest fleeing up the ward toward the breach, while the pikewomen held the street in their drill and the fire crews went past them into the church.

Jeanne was through the doors with the first file. Inside, the old church was a ruin of overturned benches and smoking lamps, and the women of the refuge were on their feet in the crypt stair, the ones who had found weapons in the altar rail, and at the back a knot of Marcenay's men had hold of two girls and were trying to drag them toward the vestry, and the sight did something to the careful, clerkly ruler of Vauréal that no ledger would ever record.

She did not scream. She put her steel cap lower and ran, and the file ran with her, and in the close dark of the church her dagger found the thigh of the first man, and a pikewoman's weapon took him in the throat, and the rest let go of the girls and made for the windows with the city's women at their heels. One of them, the worst, she recognized from the friar's description, a sergeant named for the night at Sainte-Florence, and she had him marked before he went out through the broken glass, and she said his name once, aloud, to be sure of it.

The evacuation took an hour: the women and the children and the wounded through the crypt passage to the canal, under the sand crews' guard, while the pikewomen held the church and the square and the street beyond. By dawn the inner gate had done its work as well. The garrison, reinforced by the militia, had thrown the French column back through the burning ward and into the breach, and Marcenay's assault had cost him two hundred men and gained nothing but rubble, and the bells of Sainterre changed their note from alarm to the ragged singing of people who have survived the night.

Jeanne did not sleep. She went the rounds of the hospital and the crypt and the breach, and the city watched the Grey Woman in her bloodstained jerk in moving through it, and the story went ahead of her as stories do, and grew, as stories will, and she let it grow because there was no stopping it and because a legend in a siege was a kind of gunpowder.

* * *

She stayed in the crypt until the last of the women had been seen to. The two girls the sergeant's party had taken were twelve and fourteen, and neither had been harmed, though the older one could not stop shaking and the younger had lost the power of speech. Their mothers came through the canal passage at first light and the scene in the crypt was beyond the reach of any chronicle, and Jeanne held the younger girl for a long time because no one else was free, and said nothing, because nothing was the correct thing.

The women of the refuge organized themselves around their own with the competence of the unprotected. One, a dyer's wife with a face like a fist, had broken a Frenchman's arm with a brass candlestick and wanted it entered in the city's record, and Jeanne had the clerk take the name and the deed then and there, and saw in the woman's eyes the exact thing she had been building since the first charter: the knowledge that the city's defense was not the soldiers' property.

"They told us through the doors," the dyer's wife said, "that if we made a sound they would fire the church. I thought, the duchess is inside these walls. The duchess would not let a church burn with us in it. I thought it like a prayer, madame. And you came."

"The city came. I was at its head, which is my proper place." Jeanne gave her the steel cap to hold for a moment, because she had seen women handle holy objects and wanted the gesture of it. "Your name and your deed are in the book. When this is over, the city will owe its women a debt it can never pay in full, and I will make sure the ledgers keep trying."

The story needed no printing, though Verhoeven printed it anyway, in the next sheet, without names but with every detail the women chose to give, and the reading of it in the market crosses changed the mood of Sainterre. Up until that week, the women's crews had been auxiliary. After the church, they were part of the garrison, and the militia captains stopped arguing about crossbows for women and started issuing them.

* * *

The count came at midday, in the cloth hall, over the ledgers. Forty-one dead in the night, a hundred and twenty wounded, the outer ward gone, the river wall breached but the inner gate sound. And the powder: Van Brock's store, the two-key arsenal that was the charter's pride, had taken a shell in the first bombardment, and the true remaining stock was enough for perhaps ten days' hard fighting, no more.

"Then we make ten days into the war." Jeanne looked around the aldermen's table, the smoke-stained faces, Coppens with his bandaged head, the militia captains, Sister Margriet. "First, every ounce of saltpeter in the city, in the merchants' stores and the apothecaries' shops and the convent gardens, goes to the arsenal, paid for at fixed price, receipts in the book. Verhoeven prints the requisition tonight with my seal. Second, we cut the guns' firing to fixed rates, no more than the captains sign for; the wall pieces hold their shot for the assaults, and the small arms use the rest. Third, a letter goes out tonight by every route we have — arrow, swimmer, the canal at low water. The field army has to know the exact number, because a relieving battle without powder is a massacre, and with it is a rescue."

"The swimmers," Coppens said. "Three have been lost already."

"Then the fourth goes with the letter in a wax tube and a purse of gold and my personal warrant of a pension for his family. We will not stop sending because the river is dangerous; the river is why the city exists."

The council nodded, the slow nods of people who had stopped being surprised by their own courage. After it broke, Sister Margriet detained her with a hand on her sleeve and a look at her face.

"The fighting in the church. You should not have been in it. In your condition."

"No one else was, in the first ten minutes." Jeanne kept her voice even. "Sister, if this child is a girl, she will be told one day that her mother was in a church at four months with a dagger in her hand. I would rather that than tell her her mother stood counting linen while other women's daughters were taken. Watch the sickness for me, and say nothing. That is all I ask."

The old nun looked at her a long moment, and then said, "I have delivered four hundred babies, madame, including two in the middle of a flood. This one will be fine. You, I am less certain of. Sit down now and eat, or I will tell the whole city its duchess is more stubborn than its aldermen."

She sat, and ate, and wrote the letter.

* * *

The sallies were the garrison's answer to the guns, and by the sixth week they had their own grim tradition. Every third night, a chosen company went out through the water gate at the ebb, blackened with soot, to work on the French siege lines: cutting the fascines, spiking the emplaced guns with iron nails driven into the touch holes, firing the timber of the approach trenches, and coming home before the enemy's drums could form a response. The guilds competed for the places, because the sally companies got double pay and the best of the ration bread, and because a city that can hit back stops being afraid.

Jeanne authorized each raid herself, from the lists the garrison commander brought, and she made two rules of her own: no harm to the French wounded, who were stripped of arms and left for their own surgeons, and no looting beyond the actual tools of the siege. The first raid under the rules came back with a prisoner instead, a young engineer's clerk with a satchel of the trench drawings, and the drawings, sent out by arrow, told Thiébaut exactly where the French mine was being run under the second wall.

"He says the south mine will be ready in a fortnight." She traced the ink lines in the cloth hall cellar with Coppens and the gunner captains. "Then we have a fortnight to make the ground give somewhere else. Countermine here, under their sap, and keep it quiet. When their gallery reaches ours, we break through and flood the works with the canal water. The engineers can do it?"

"The miners say yes, madame. They want double pay."

"They get it, and a barrel each when the thing works." She set down the dividers. "A city that fights under the ground as well as on the walls is a city that believes it can win. Let's make sure it's right."

The countermine was dug in the cellar-dark over nine nights, the miners relaying in silence with their ears to the rock, and on the tenth night the two galleries met, and the Vauréal party broke the last wall of earth and let the canal in, and the French mine flooded with a roar the sentries heard on both walls, and the besiegers' best approach drowned in mud. Graçay's engineers started a second mine farther east, and everyone in Sainterre knew the calendar had just been rewritten.

* * *

The swimmer came back.

Three had been lost to the French archers and the river's cold, and the fourth was a Malemort waterman named Goujon, thin and sinewy and entirely without fear, who had spent his youth smuggling salt past the tolls and considered an army in siege just another kind of customs house. He went out at the ebb with the wax tube braided into his hair, and the city gave him up for lost through two black days, and on the third night a patrol on the water gate heard the agreed signal, three knocks under the stone, and hauled him out of the river blue and shivering and alive, with an answering tube tied where the letters had been.

Thiébaut's reply was terse and soldierly: the ground was giving; the relief was set; hold eight more days; and for her alone, the line in the private hand that made her read it twice. Goujon, wrapped in blankets by the guardroom fire, drank the hot wine they gave him and delivered the report his body could not: the French camp's ration riots, the deserters crossing at the fords, the convoys that never arrived. He had floated among the reeds for six hours on the return, he said, because a patrol was watering horses at the landing, and he had counted every horse and every man and memorized them, because his instructions were to bring back more than paper.

"He should be paid," Jeanne said to Coppens, "double the purse, with a receipt, and the city's mark. And he should never be sent out again. A man this lucky owes the river a life, and the river collects."

The old guildsman nodded. "There'll be no shortage of volunteers after him. The watermen are fighting over the next message."

"They can fight in line. Write the man a guild charter of his own while you're about it, the guild of the river watch, with the toll freedom as its fee. In a year's time they'll be the proudest company in Malemort, and no one will remember it began in a sewer."

She saw Goujon before he was sent home to recover, and he said the thing she had been carrying without knowing it: "The duke reads your letters twice, madame. I watched him by the picket fire. A man reads a dispatch once. He read yours twice." She gave him no answer she would have printed, but she wrote the detail in the private book that no clerk ever saw.

* * *

The arrow went out at midnight, and the swimmer went with it, and the letter said the things the clerks required, the breach, the ward, the powder number, the dead, and at the bottom, in the private hand, it said the rest.

_There is more. The physician confirms it, and Sister Margriet is certain. I am carrying a child, conceived, by the reckoning, in the winter, and I am well, and the child is well, and the city is well enough to go on. Do not do anything reckless with this news. That is an order, and I have discovered I enjoy giving them. The child will be born in a city that has kept its charter, because you and I between us are going to make that sentence true. I was in a fight today, in a church, and I was not afraid, which I think is what you taught me without ever saying it. Come when the ground gives. We will be here._

She signed it with her name alone, the way she had signed the first letter of all of them, the cold one with the gate terms, and the archer wrapped it around the bolt and loosed it into the dark over the French lines, and somewhere in the hills the picket line would pass it from hand to hand until it reached the pavilion where a man sat awake over maps, and she imagined his face when he read the last of it, and allowed herself, for one minute in the cellar dark, to be not the duchess but the woman.

Then she put out the taper and lay down on her pallet in her clothes with the steel cap within reach, and slept, because the morning had walls to defend and a child to keep and a siege to finish, and none of it would wait for her grief or her joy.
