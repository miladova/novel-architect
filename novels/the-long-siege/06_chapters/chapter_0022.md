# Chapter Twenty-Two — The Price of Crowns

A siege that fails is a ruin; a siege that succeeds is also a ruin, with better morale. Jeanne learned this in the first week after the relief, walking the streets of the city she had saved and finding that saving was the beginning rather than the end of the work.

The eastern quarter was a wasteland of shot-blasted houses where families lived in cellars under open sky. The tanners' ward had burned and still smelled of it. The canal was choked with the refuse of seven weeks and the bodies of the French horses, and the fishing boats would not put out until it was cleared. The mills had been hit twice, the guildhall's stained glass was gone, and the famous looms, the reason the city existed, had survived only because the guildsmen had taken them apart and hidden the frames in the crypts, an act of civic foresight Coppens considered the equal of any valor on the walls.

The people were thin, and the children were thinner, and the flux still ran in the lower town though the worst of it was over. What the city did not have, to its own astonishment, was the thing every besieged city in Europe expected: the famine price. The rationing books had survived the bombardment, and the fixed prices with them, and the grain that came in on the first boats was sold at the same rate as the last grain before the investment, with the difference charged to the ducal war account. Dolfin's factor, watching the boats unload, said it was the most expensive piece of benevolence he had seen north of Venice, and Jeanne said benevolence was a word for people who had never had to govern a bread riot, and the price was an investment in the next tax vote.

She was right, and the tax vote proved it within the month.

* * *

Dolfin came himself in July, when the roads were hard, and they did the new business in the cloth hall's upper chamber with the windows open onto the sound of rebuilding, because the treasury office at Aubecourt felt too far from the invoices. The Venetian had aged in the year, or rather the war had caught up with him the way it caught everyone, and he came to the point with even less ceremony than usual.

"The victory has a number, madame. Your total debt to my house and its partners now stands at ninety-four thousand écus, against customs and iron revenues pledged for six years. The relief campaign added the Landsknechts, the sledge guns, the emergency grain, and the city relief. Paris, by contrast, has spent perhaps a hundred and forty thousand on a war that produced one captured bridge-fort and a dead captain, and the Regent's new loan is being refused in three cities. You are winning the borrowing war. You are also, in absolute terms, running out of room."

"Then give me the rest of the arithmetic."

"The next campaign, the one that takes Mont d'Or and ends the war, will cost forty thousand. My house will lend it, because the collateral has performed and because a duchy that breaks Paris's credit is the safest loan in Europe. But the partners want two things. First, the Sainterre customs become a direct six-year annuity, administered by the joint board, not merely pledged. Second, the war taxes the cities vote under their charters are written into the loan covenants, so that the repayment does not depend on a future council's humor." He folded his hands. "I dislike saying the second part aloud. It is the price of the first."

Jeanne looked at the river traffic below the window, the long barges of the reopened trade. "An annuity on the customs makes your house a part of my government. The tax covenants make the cities' votes into paper your partners hold. You understand what you're asking me to mortgage."

"I understand better than you think. I am also the man who has watched you turn two restive cities into the strongest government in the marches by giving them charters and asking them to pay for their own freedom. Build the covenants that way. Let the cities vote the taxes themselves, openly, in their guildhalls, and then pledge the voted taxes. My partners get their security. Your aldermen get the dignity. Venice is famously indifferent to dignity, which makes it cheap for us and valuable for them."

It was the old Dolfin, the banker who understood that politics and ledgers were the same book. She thought it through the way she thought through a charter, clause by clause, and found the shape of it sound.

"One alteration. The annuity has a redemption clause at par after three years, and the board's Vauréal seat always holds the casting vote. And you will write to your partners in Paris that the duchess's credit is open for the Regent's inspection. I want Madame Anne to know exactly what victory is costing me, and that I can afford it."

The thin smile. "It will give her indigestion. Done."

* * *

The enlarged charter was her own answer to the debt, and it was presented to the Sainterre guilds in the same cloth hall where de Key had once preached surrender.

The terms were deliberately large. The aldermen's council became a permanent body with its own election law; the city court's jurisdiction widened to include all civil cases below a set sum; the tolls, fixed for six years under the first charter, were now fixed for ten, with any alteration requiring the council's formal consent; the city kept its militia, its two-key powder store, and its press under the licensing law. In exchange, the guilds voted, in full public session with the votes recorded by name, a permanent war tax for the duration plus three years, collected by the city's own officers under the city's own seal, and pledged under the loan covenants Dolfin had described.

The vote was not unanimous. It never is, in free assemblies, and Jeanne would have distrusted it if it had been. The weavers' guild, de Key's old ground, held out longest and voted last, and their new dean, a man who had carried a pike on the wall during the second assault, said the quiet thing aloud: that the old radicalism had ended on the French gallows, and a city that taxed itself was harder to conquer than a city that was merely ordered to pay. The vote carried, and the bells of Sainterre rang for it, and the news sheet printed every name on both sides, which was the most revolutionary thing Verhoeven's press had yet done.

The nobility's rage arrived at the Haut-Tertre in September, in the persons of seven lords and a great deal of inherited self-regard. They came as a deputation, and they were received in the round chamber with full ceremony, and their spokesman, an old baron with a face like a winter apple, said the thing as plainly as it could be said.

"The duchess is making the towns rich in rights while the blood that defends them pays the ancient subsidies. A charter is a grant of crown property, madame. Every toll fixed for ten years is a toll taken from the ducal domain, and every city court is a court taken from the lord's. If this continues, the lords of Vauréal will be tenants of their own aldermen."

She let him finish, and let the silence age, and then she answered him from the high chair with the state's whole voice, because this was a speech the whole dominion would read in the news sheet by the end of the week.

"My lord baron, the lords of Vauréal hold their lands by a charter too, the one my grandfather's sword wrote and my father's clerks engrossed. You call the city grants a loss of domain. I call them an exchange: fixed rights for fixed taxes, self-rule for self-defense. While this court debated in April, the weavers of Sainterre died on my wall holding pikes your levies never arrived to carry. The city voted its own money through a siege while three of your number were writing careful letters to Paris. I do not give rights away. I buy loyalty, and I buy it cheaper than fire. If the ancient subsidies buy you better government than the new charters buy me, bring me the receipts. Until then, the charters stand, and the lords who do not like them may audit their own contributions to the war."

The deputation withdrew in the order it had come, but noisier. Two of the seven would be careful ever after; the rest would grumble into their manors and pay their installments, because the alternative was the example of Hautperthuis, which now sat in a fortress under attainder with his name being read aloud in every parish. Fear and interest, her father had taught her, are the two levers; she had spent a year discovering that a third existed, which was pride in a government that kept its word.

* * *

The recognition of the women's companies was Jeanne's own ceremony, held in the rebuilt square of the cloth hall on the first dry day of September, and the aldermen argued against it for a week, which told her it was necessary. The crews paraded in their leather jerkins with their pikes and crossbows, four hundred of them, and the dyer's wife who had broken the Frenchman's arm with the candlestick walked at their head carrying the steel cap Jeanne had lent her in the church, and the duchess, six months rounded and standing on the hall steps with the joined arms standard behind her, gave each company a charter of its own: the Guild of Saint Margaret, with the right to keep arms in its hall, to train on Sundays after church, and to send two of its number to the city council in matters of defense.

"Armed women with a guild charter," Coppens murmured, beside her. "The historians will not know what to do with us."

"The historians are mostly clerics, so they will ignore us. The city will not, which is what matters." She watched the dyer's wife receive her sealed copy with hands that shook only slightly. "A city whose women know how to defend it is a city no one ever takes twice. Write that into the record."

The record got it, and the news sheet got it, and by the next assembly the other two chartered cities were asking, in careful, formal language, whether the privileges of Sainterre might extend to Malemort and Grisay, and she answered by sending them drill masters and a standard form of words, and said nothing at all about the speed with which an emergency custom could become a right.

Rostaing came to her in the same month, on crutches, thin and pale and re-commissioned, with the new captain's patent in his hand and the old arrogance burned out of him and replaced by something harder and more useful. He knelt in the cloth hall chamber with difficulty and said, "Madame. I am told the entry is marked in full. I want to say, in my own words, that I spent six months as a common man-at-arms and learned what the law costs the people under it. The village of Argnon has a new well with my back pay, if you want to know where the captain's pay went."

"I know. My treasurer told me; you didn't ask for the receipt." She looked at him the way she looked at a repaired gun, testing the seams. "Captain. I'm giving you the light cavalry company in the Mont d'Or siege, under the duke's direct eye. One more judgment without a clerk, and the next commutation will not exist. You may rise."

He rose, and his salute was the most precise thing in the room, and when he had gone Thiébaut said from the window, "You build weapons out of men the same way Van Brock builds them out of iron. I'm not sure whether to be impressed or afraid."

"Both. It's the correct response to a gun foundry."

* * *

The household closed around the pregnancy with the clucking efficiency of women who had survived a great deal and intended to survive this as well. Oriabel took over the lying-in arrangements from the moment the pregnancy was announced, producing from somewhere a complete inventory of linen and a midwife of ferocious reputation. Lisette ran the intelligence network from a stool beside Jeanne's writing table. Claire de Sombernon, who had lost her own first child in the year before her husband's death, became the pregnancy's particular guardian, grave and funny and entirely without rivalry, and the two women had long conversations about swaddling and sovereignty that the men of the council could not follow and did not attempt.

"Your court is run by women," Brive grumbled one evening, watching a dispatch leave in Lisette's hand while Claire audited the hospital accounts at the other end of the table.

"Your war is won by women, marshal. Keep up." Jeanne shifted against the cushions, the child heavy and active. "The siege taught me what the books only suggested: half of every population has been doing the work of government for centuries without the patents. I'm issuing the patents."

"She'll want the vote next," the old man said to Thiébaut.

"She has the vote. She has all of them. She's the duchess."

It was said as a jest, and the table laughed, and Jeanne laughed with them, and wrote in her own private book that night that the truest jokes in a court were always the ones delivered as flattery.

* * *

Othon d'Erquelines came in October, in the end, because the arithmetic of the spring had reached even him.

He did not come to submit. He came to bargain, with the imperial letter in one hand and the duchy's need for the eastern roads in the other, and the bargaining was exquisite and interminable and finally settled, as such things always are, over money. The count wanted his homage made cheap and his standing made high. Jeanne, who needed his roads for the Mont d'Or siege and could not afford a second enemy, gave him neither land nor office but a thing more dangerous and more useful: a twenty-one-year monopoly on the toll bridge at Erquelines, the crossing the imperial convoys used, with a fixed annual payment to the ducal treasury and a clause that the monopoly lapsed the instant his homage lapsed.

"You're making him rich," Foulques observed, when the document was sealed.

"I'm renting his loyalty with his own greed. A lord who profits from my roads keeps my roads. When the war ends, we audit the bridge and find reasons to shorten the term. Twenty-one years is a long time in a charter and a short time in the history of a toll." She set the seal down. "Also note the clause: his toll collectors are my commissioned officers. The monopoly is his. The law remains mine."

The chancellor, thin and careful since the heart seizure but back at his table, made the note, and said, "The orphans' school enrolled its first forty girls this month, madame. The bishop objects to the Latin."

"The bishop may teach the boys. Tell him I will personally examine the girls at Easter and publish the results. Competition in education, Foulques. It works for tolls."

The memorial was held in the cloth hall square on the first anniversary of the relief's eve, because Coppens insisted the city's grief had a calendar and meant to keep it. The four hundred and eleven names were carved into a stone wall the aldermen raised at the square's edge, the soldiers and the civilians together without rank or order, and the families processed past it through the morning, each leaving what the household could spare: a ribbon, a loaf, a candle, a child's drawing.

Jeanne came on foot, six months rounded and in the plain grey gown, and laid her own gift with the rest: a copy of the charter, sealed in bronze, set into the wall's center. Her speech was short, because the widows had already done the speaking in their processions.

"This wall is not a monument to a siege. It is a receipt. The city paid a price for its own government, and the state acknowledges the debt in the only durable currency it has: the names. Every charter this duchy grants from now on will be read within sight of this square's account. Go home, and tell your children the debt is remembered by the duchess as well as the dead."

The news sheet printed the speech beside the list of names, and copies went to Malemort and Grisay and the villages, and the phrase _the duchess's accounts_ began to appear in tavern talk with a meaning it had never had in her father's time: not what the ruler took, but what the ruler admitted she owed.

Thiébaut, walking back with her through the crowded, respectful streets, said, "Your father built tombs for dukes. You're building them for weavers."

"The dukes have the cathedral vault. The weavers needed a wall." She shifted her weight, the child heavy. "Also it's policy, if you want the unpoetic truth. A city that sees its butcher's name in stone beside an alderman's is a city that answers the next French herald with pikes. Grief is a fortification. I learned that from a book of names."

"I know. I watched you keep it."

* * *

The Regent's letter arrived with the first frost, carried by a herald of the old, careful school, and its silkiness had a new undertone, which Foulques identified at once as the sound of a treasury in pain. Anne de France wrote as one woman of business to another, deploring the "misunderstandings" of the campaigning season, noting the heavy costs borne by both crowns, and proposing a formal conference at Heurtois under the bishop's neutral roof, with full safe conducts and a view to a general settlement before the new year. There was no mention now of forfeit, garrisons, or the dowry of reconciliation.

"She wants out of the war," Thiébaut said, when the council had heard it. "Not permanently. Cheaply. A peace she can break when her books recover."

"All peaces are that. The question is the terms we write while she needs the ink." Jeanne turned the letter in her hands. "Answer yes, in the warmest language Foulques can compose, with the conference fixed for after the Mont d'Or siege. I will not negotiate with a fortress of mine still in enemy hands; the sight of our guns on the rock will improve every clause. And I want the city charters named in the treaty as recognized customs of the dominion. If Paris signs the charters, no future French king can champion their enemies inside my walls."

Foulques permitted himself one of his thin smiles. "She will dislike the charters clause."

"She will dislike all of it. She will sign the parts that cost her money and leave the parts that cost her pride. That is the whole art of negotiation with Anne de France: never ask for her pride when her purse is open."

The herald went back with the answer and a gift of Sainterre broadcloth for the Regent's own wearing, and Jeanne turned the council's attention back to the rock in the south, because conferences were for the end of wars and the war still had a fortress to take.

That night, alone with Thiébaut in the chamber where the connecting door now stood permanently open, she let the day's composure go and sat heavily on the bed's edge, five months rounded and feeling every day of it. He knelt without a word and took off her shoes, which was among the things he had learned to do without being asked, and put a stool under her feet.

"The court calls us joint sovereigns now," she said. "I heard an envoy use the phrase this morning, as though it were an old title."

"The court is catching up with the documents. Also with the facts." He sat beside her. "The vassals hate the charters, the cities love the taxes they vote themselves, Venice owns more of my wife's customs than is strictly comfortable, and the most expensive war in our history is being won on credit. Did I miss anything?"

"Only the part where we have an army in being, a capital no one dares besiege twice, and a traitor in a fortress with his price on his head. The usual season's work." She leaned against him, carefully, because of the child. "Thiébaut. When Mont d'Or falls, Paris will have to talk. I want the peace conference at Heurtois, and I want it before the Regent's treasury recovers. Can the army be ready by the new year?"

"The army can. The question is the snow. A winter siege of a rock like Mont d'Or is a thing old officers tell horror stories about."

"Then find me officers who tell stories about winning them. We have the sledge guns and the gunners and the men who drowned a mine. Winter is the season the French don't expect us, because they have never once expected us." She put her hand over his where it lay on the curve of the child. "Also I would like this baby born in a duchy at peace. That is a maternal sentiment, and I intend to use it shamelessly in council."

He laughed, low, and kissed the top of her head. "Then peace by the lying-in. I'll put it in the dispatches. The duchess's commands have grown more specific."

Outside, the October rain moved over the river and the darkened town, and somewhere in the chancellery the clerks were still at work on the attainder papers and the loan covenants and the charters and the endless, patient ledgers of a reign that had begun with a corpse in a cart and was becoming, invoice by invoice, a government.
