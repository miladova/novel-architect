# Chapter Seventeen — Treason's Market

The army moved within the week, because the one thing the fall of Mont d'Or could not be allowed to become was a story with a long first chapter. Jeanne had the host on the southern roads by the fifth day, the Lorraine lances and the Vauréal squadrons and the new Sainterre militia companies riding together under the joined arms, and the news sheet came out in the same week with the facts stated plainly and no panic at all, which was the first battle of the new war and was won at the printing press.

But an army could not un-take a fortress in a week, and the council knew it. They camped at the river junction below the lost rock, within sight of the French banner on its walls, and the war council in the command pavilion did the arithmetic that no ballad would record: a full siege train could not come up before midsummer, Graçay's field army was somewhere beyond the hills, and an assault on Mont d'Or now would be the Roches-Noires again, brave men dying on prepared ground at the enemy's chosen hour.

"We don't besiege the rock yet." Thiébaut put his finger on the map. "We besiege the road to it. Mont d'Or is only useful to Graçay if he can feed it and move through it. We put light horse across every supply route, we take the convoys, we make the garrison eat its stores, and we wait for the city war he's actually preparing. His objective was never a fortress. His objective is Sainterre. Mont d'Or is the door he opened; the room is the cloth cities."

"Then why did he pay Gui to open it?" Brive demanded.

"Because doors are cheap and sieges are expensive. Hautperthuis sold him a year's campaigning for the price of sixty cutthroats." Thiébaut's face was without heat. "The question is what we do about the seller."

That was the true business of the night, and it kept the council late.

Lisette's network, built patiently over a year of kitchen conversations and courier-watching and silver changing hands in tavern yards, had produced its map. The maid unrolled it on the table herself, a plain thing of ink and butcher's paper, and the lords of the council looked at it with the respect it had earned. There were the ex-French sergeants planted as tenants on the Hautperthuis manors, twenty-three households now, each marked with a name. There was the factor at Pont-d'Ortelle, the priest's clothes, the fair-day meetings. There were the lesser lords who had ridden to Gui's hunting lodge in the winter, seven names, and the merchants who held French coin on his account, four. There, at the center, in the careful script of a woman who had learned her letters from the duchess's own clerk, was the word _Hautperthuis_, with lines reaching outward like a spider's web.

"This is the fifth column." Jeanne looked around the table. "It has always existed; it is simply visible now. We will do two things. First, every name on this map gets a watch, and none of them gets a warning. Second, the tenants. The soldiers planted on the marches are not the lords. They're paid men with families and leases. I want the leases examined by the chancellery, not the provosts, and the ones that can be broken by law broken by law, with the families given time and the tenants offered places in the new works at Grisay. We do not create twenty-three widows for the French to recruit."

"Madame." Brive's voice was heavy. "In my grandfather's time this would have been a week of fire. Ride the manors, burn the roofs, leave the lesson. The army expects it. The army wants it."

"The army is commanded, it does not dictate." She kept her voice level. "Marshal, I understand the pleasure of the thing. Fire is easy and it feels like justice. But every cottage we burn without a trial is a cottage whose sons join Marcenay's company. Every tenant we make homeless becomes the enemy's best recruiter. The law is slower. The law also keeps. We attainder the lord, we prove the leases, and we protect the people, and in five years the marches are ours in fact rather than in smoke."

Thiébaut said nothing until she had finished, then: "She's right. I've fought both ways. The fire wins the month. The law wins the country."

Brive argued for another hour, and lost, and took the loss like the soldier he was, and the council moved to the attainder, which was the true instrument and the one the lord had never believed would be used against a man of his blood.

Foulques laid the precedents out himself, and Jeanne saw the chancellor's craft in all its cold beauty for the first time. A simple treason charge could drag for years through the privileges of a great lord: the right to be tried by his peers, the delays of Parlement, the appeals to the crown that would treat the whole question as a family quarrel among princes. An attainder was different. It was an act of the ruler in council declaring the bloodline corrupted, the lands forfeit, the name extinguished, and it moved at the speed of the state rather than the speed of the law.

"Use it wrongly," Foulques said, "and every lord in the dominion reads his own name into the document. Use it with a mountain of proof, and every lord reads instead the price of his own bad faith."

"Then build the mountain." Jeanne turned the drafts. "I want every lease, every witness, the fair-day meetings with dates, the planted tenants with their old regiments named. We do not attaint him for taking the wrong side in a war. We attaint him for the steward's office he ran under a false name while he ate my bread. The charge is corruption of the marches, and the proof is paperwork, because paperwork is the one thing a great lord cannot answer with a smile."

The letter from Hautperthuis arrived two days later, as though he had smelled the drafting ink. It was a masterpiece of its kind: grief at the "misunderstanding" of Mont d'Or, assurances that he had been visiting the fortress on the duchess's own business when "certain hotheads" acted without his knowledge, a reminder of their shared grandmother's blood, and a proposal that he and the duchess meet privately at the Heurtois border to settle all differences as became a family. There was no mention of Valvert's body, the French vanguard, or the banner over the rock.

She read it aloud to the council, and then answered it herself, in the clerk's hand of her state letters, and the answer was short. She thanked him for his concern. She noted that the hotheads included sixty of his own household men in his own livery, and that the French vanguard had crossed on a bridge his tenants guarded. She regretted that a private meeting was impossible while he held her fortress against her peace, and she summoned him, in the proper feudal form, to present himself at the war camp within thirty days to answer the council's questions, failing which the attainder would proceed in his absence.

"Give him the whole thirty days," she said, sealing it. "He'll spend them writing to Paris. By the time he understands the summons isn't a negotiation, the mountain is built."

"His blood claim will move the other lords," Thiébaut observed.

"Let it move them. I want the waverers to watch a man of the blood answer a clerk's questions with clerks' evidence. The lesson isn't for Gui. The lesson is for every Erquelines in the country."

* * *

The news from the western marches reached the camp on the third day, carried by a Dominican friar who had walked forty miles with the story and the survivors' names. He came to the duchess's pavilion in the rain, and he was shown in at once, and he told it standing, because he said he could not sit while the words were in him.

Marcenay's free company, four hundred men in the pay of Paris and the loose discipline of no one, had crossed the border ahead of the main army and taken the town of Sainte-Florence, an undefended weaving town with a charter and a militia of eighty, which had opened its gates on the promise of the soldier's word. The word had lasted until the gates closed. What followed was not war by any definition the friar could find in his books. The town was systematically looted over two days. The guild chests were seized, the church plate melted, the merchants' houses stripped to their timbers. The militia who surrendered were hanged from their own gatehouse, thirty-one men, in their charter uniforms. A group of townswomen who had taken refuge in the church were held there through the night by the company's sergeants; the friar gave the number, which was forty-two, and the number who had not survived the night, which was three, one of them a girl of twelve. The town's four hundred looms, the reason it existed, were smashed with hammers so that it would not make cloth for the duchess's war.

He had the names in a small book, written in his own hand, and he put it on the table, and the rain ran off his habit onto the pavilion floor.

No one spoke for a while. Jeanne opened the book and read the first page and made herself read all of it, because someone had to, and the someone might as well be the person whose war this was in name. When she looked up, the men in the pavilion had the various faces of grief: Brive's white fury, Foulques's open distress, the guards at the flap with their jaws set. Thiébaut's face was the worst, because it was not angry. It was cold, the cold of a man making a professional inventory of what he intended to do.

"Where is Marcenay now?"

"At Sainte-Florence still, madame. They remain because there is still food. The countryside for ten miles is empty; the people are in the woods."

"Then he's closer than his judgment, and his judgment was never good." Thiébaut turned to Brive. "Four hundred free company, drunk, in a town whose walls they've partly pulled down for their fires. A fast column of lances and mounted archers can be there in two days. I'll lead it myself."

"No." Jeanne closed the friar's book. "You won't. Not because you shouldn't, but because the next move is Graçay's and the field army's commander can't be two days away on a raid. Brive takes the column. Jean goes with him, since he's been asking for a reason since the pike. Orders: Marcenay's men are to be treated as soldiers under no lawful colors. No quarter for the sergeants who held the church; the rest may yield into bonds. The town's surviving people are to be fed and sheltered from the army's own stores, and the friar goes with the column to name the men he can name. And Brive."

The old marshal turned.

"No burning. No sack in answer to a sack. I know what the men will want. Tell them the duchess pays double bounty for every prisoner brought in and nothing for a roof put to the torch. Make the discipline pay better than the cruelty. That's the only sermon soldiers will believe."

* * *

The column's first dispatch came back in four days, Brive's hand, terse and soldierly. They had covered the ground in half the expected time, found Marcenay's scouts blind and drunk, and taken the town's outer barricades at dawn with one charge. The free company broke at the first real contact, the kind of force that excelled at churches and dissolved before lances. Marcenay himself had escaped with perhaps eighty men through the east gate, riding hard for the French lines, and Brive had not pursued past the river, per his orders. The sergeants named by the friar had not been so fortunate; six were taken in the cellars, and the survivors of Sainte-Florence had identified them in the square, and the provosts had hanged them from the same gatehouse as the militiamen, in the same number, which was the kind of arithmetic the towns understood.

Jeanne read the rest of the dispatch alone in her pavilion, because the rest was not for the council. The survivors' accounts, taken by the friar and the women of the town: who had been held where, and by whom, and which of the dead had names. Bride's note at the bottom, in a hand less steady than the military parts, said that the army's discipline had held under provocation he would not commit to paper, and that the double bounty had been paid in full, and that he had served thirty years under three dukes and had never been prouder of an order he had argued against.

She sat a while with the papers. Then she called her clerk and dictated the relief: timber and masons from the Grisay works for the town, a year's exemption from all dues, the rebuilding of the looms at the ducal charge, and a hospital of twenty beds endowed in perpetuity, its charter to name the twelve-year-old among its patrons. The money would hurt; Dolfin would raise an eyebrow and produce another lien. She wrote the order anyway, because some payments were the kind that showed up in no repayment table and in every tavern for a hundred miles.

Thiébaut came in as the clerk left, read the order over her shoulder, and said nothing for a moment. Then: "The army will hear of this by Sunday."

"The army is meant to. I want every soldier in this host to know the difference between his pay and Marcenay's. One serves a law. The other serves an appetite. The law builds hospitals. The appetite burns towns. Men will fight longer for an idea than for a purse, though they require the purse as well."

"Your father never spent a penny on ideas."

"My father is in a vault. Draw your own conclusions."

* * *

The Dominican brought her one more witness before the column rode, because he said his office required the whole truth be given to the person who could do something with it. She was a woman of thirty, a weaver's widow from Sainte-Florence, one of the forty-two, and she came into the pavilion with her eyes on the ground and her hands knotted in her cloak, and Jeanne dismissed everyone but Lisette and sat her at the table and poured the wine herself.

The woman told it without tears, in the flat voice of someone who has used them all. The church, the dark, the sergeants, the girl of twelve who did not survive the night. She told it in the plain vocabulary of a weaving town, and Jeanne wrote the names herself, because the clerks would soften them, and when it was done the woman looked up at last and said the thing she had come to say.

"Madame, they tell us you will answer with fire. My neighbor says the duke's men will burn every French village in the marches and it will serve them right. I'm here to ask you not to. The villages didn't take my husband or my girl. Marcenay's men did. Burn the villages and you make more Marcenays, and my girl died for nothing at all but a story that gets repeated."

Jeanne was quiet a moment. "What is your name?"

"Marthe, madame. Marthe Dubois."

"Marthe, I have already given the column the order you're asking for. No burning, no sack in answer to a sack, double bounty for prisoners, nothing that teaches the countryside to hate my peace. I gave it for reasons of state. You've just given me the better reason." She folded the paper with the names. "Will you let me keep these? The state keeps a book of its dead. Your girl belongs in it."

"She belonged to me first." The woman's voice cracked once, and steadied. "But the book can have her after. Put her down as Agnès Dubois, twelve years old, a weaver's daughter. She wanted to be a nun. She had the voice for it."

"I'll write that too."

When she had gone, Jeanne sat a long time with the list, and then she added to the column's written orders a line the soldiers would not understand for years: that every town taken from the French was to receive a copy of the proclamation of Sainte-Florence, so that the country might learn the difference between the two armies not from the priests but from the invoices.

She walked the camp alone after the council, with Lisette at a distance, and went among the hospital tents where the wounded from the skirmishes lay, and the new recruits were drilling in the mud, and the guns sat under their tarpaulins. The rain had settled into a steady drizzle, and the camp fires smoked, and the army did its evening work around her, the endless ordinary labor that made the atrocities possible to keep going at all.

She had believed, at twenty-two, that rule was a matter of ledgers and law, the things her father's clerics had taught her. She had believed the hardest question would be finding the correct answer. The year had taught her differently. The correct answer sat in the book on her table, in the friar's careful hand, in the twelve-year-old's name, and there was nothing in all her education that could make the thing itself bearable. Rule was not finding the answer. It was reading the names and then going on with the work.

Foulques found her at the gun lines, standing under the awning of the biggest bombard with her hand on the cold bronze, and he came to stand beside her without speaking for a while. The chancellor had aged in the winter; she saw it now, the stoop that had not been there, the tremor in the hand that had never trembled over a document.

"Madame," he said at last. "I must ask to speak with you. Privately. Soon. There is a thing I have carried too long, and I find I cannot carry it into whatever the summer will be."

She turned to look at him fully. "The green case."

It struck him like a blow. His face went grey, and then, with an effort she could see, he gathered himself. "You have known."

"I have known there was a locked room. I did not know its contents. I know now that whatever is in it concerns the duke and me, and a girl who did nothing, and that it has cost you your sleep and your wine. Foulques. I am not asking for it here, in the rain, in the middle of a war. But when this campaign allows, I will have the whole of it, and the duke will be present, and you will bring every paper. Do you understand?"

"I have been waiting for you to say that since the day you were fifteen." His voice was steady now that the thing was named. "I will bring everything. I have kept it all, madame. Perhaps that is my one merit in the matter. I did not destroy the evidence of what we did."

"We. You said _we_."

"Because it was. A chancellor does not act alone, and a chancellor does not refuse his prince. But I held the pen. I will not pretend otherwise." He bowed, an old man's bow, and walked away into the rain toward his own tents, and she watched him go with the friar's book still under her arm and the cold bronze under her hand, and understood that the last locked door of her girlhood was about to open, and that she had no idea what she would do on the other side of it.

* * *

The ripples reached her before the week was out, which was the point of the thing. Othon d'Erquelines wrote in his own hand, a long, anxious document that said nothing and said it at length: his distress at the troubles of the house, his unshaken devotion to the duchess's person, his hope that no false friend would ever poison her mind against her true lords, and, buried in the fourth paragraph, a request to see the chancellery's evidence concerning "certain leases on the southern marches," since he himself held similar arrangements and wished to be certain of his own standing.

"He's checking whether his name is in the net," Thiébaut said, reading it over her shoulder.

"He's checking whether the net exists. Answer him warmly. Send him the lease regulations as a public document, nothing private, and add a line about how consoling it is that the loyal lords are diligent in their own paperwork. Let him spend the summer being diligent."

The lesser lords needed no such hints. Three of the seven winter visitors to Gui's hunting lodge sent in their muster quotas early and without the usual complaints, and one, a nervous young baron named Villers, rode to the camp in person to make a full and tearful declaration: he had attended a hunt, yes, and heard talk, yes, but had known nothing of treachery, and here were his receipts and his conscience and his company of forty lances if the duchess would have them. She received him with kindness, kept the declaration in writing, and confirmed him in his lands, and the story of the weeping baron did more for the discipline of the marches than a hundred hangings.

"Power that forgives the small fish and grinds the big one," Foulques observed, making the note. "It is a fearsome combination, madame. The textbooks don't teach it."

"The textbooks were written by big fish."

* * *

The column rode at dawn, eight hundred lances and mounted archers under Brive and Jean, with the Dominican in his wet black habit riding beside the standard, and Jeanne watched them go from the camp's rise and then returned to the pavilion and the work that did not stop for dread: the supply lists, the watch rosters, the letters to the cities, the careful building of the army that would meet Graçay when he came for Sainterre.

That night, when the camp had banked its fires and the guards called the watch through the dark, she went to the field chapel alone, a small canvas tent with a single candle before the altar, and knelt on the bare ground a long time. She did not pray for victory; she had long since stopped asking God to do the work of her treasuries. She prayed for the steadiness to read whatever was coming, and for the forty-two women and the twelve-year-old and the thirty-one hanged militiamen and the boy Philippe and all the names in all the books, and she prayed, with less certainty, for the courage to know the truth about herself and the man she had married without flinching from either.

Then she rose, and snuffed the candle, and went back through the dark camp to the pavilion, where Thiébaut sat awake over the maps with a cup of cold wine, and she did not tell him what Foulques had said, because the telling would be soon and whole or not at all, and she sat down beside him and put her hand over his on the map, and together they went on with the war.
