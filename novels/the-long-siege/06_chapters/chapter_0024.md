# Chapter Twenty-Four — The King's Peace

The Prince-Bishop of Heurtois received them in a palace built for the precise purpose of looking neutral, and it was in that high, draughty, magnificently impartial building that the war was finally reduced to paper.

The conference opened on the second Monday of February, in the great hall of the bishop's residence, with the French delegation at one long table and the Vauréal-Lorraine delegation at the other, and the bishop's own clerics between them like referees at a duel. Graçay himself led for Paris, which told Jeanne everything about the Regent's mood: a royal lieutenant did not sit at peace tables while his master's cause was thought to be winning. Across the polished oak he looked older than the man of the Roches-Noires, a professional soldier who had spent two years discovering that professional soldiers cannot win against ledgers, and the two of them exchanged the bows of opponents who had come to respect each other's arithmetic.

Von Zollern was there as the Emperor's observer, perfumed and watchful, and the Burgundian Netherland envoys had come uninvited on the theory that other people's peace treaties were their business, and Dolfin sat at the far end with his clerk, because every war in Europe ended somewhere within walking distance of a Venetian banker.

Thiébaut sat beside her with his left arm in a silk sling, the shoulder healing stiff and slow, and the sight of the sling across the table did the alliance's work before a word was spoken. Foulques, thin and careful since the seizure, carried the documents, and the Abbé de Viray carried the theology, and Brive, who hated conferences, had been brought for the single purpose of looking like a man who would happily resume the war.

Graçay opened with the crown's formal position, delivered in a voice made for parlements: the forfeit fiefs were ancient crown holdings, the war had been a regrettable policing action, and the Regent's natural tenderness toward her sex wished to settle all differences on terms that preserved the honor of France. The terms, as read, were the old terms: the fiefs under French protection, a garrison, a marriage portion, the usual language of conquest dressed in the vocabulary of reconciliation.

Jeanne let him finish, and then she put her own first document on the table, the casualty and expenditure returns of both sides, carefully compiled, including the figures Dolfin's friends had supplied from Paris.

"Sire de Graçay. We will begin with the truth, since the bishop's hall is consecrated and the figures are sacred to me. The crown's army has taken one fortress by purchase and lost it by assault. It has fought one successful battle in two years, and failed to hold a single one of the cities it was sent to reduce. Its pay is nine months in arrears. Its last loan was undersubscribed in three cities. My customs revenues exceed their pre-war level, my cities are armed under sealed charters, and my credit is offered at eleven percent while the Regent's is refused at eighteen. You have come here because Paris cannot afford another campaign. I have come here because I prefer not to pay for one. Let us write the peace that follows from those facts, and leave honor to the heralds."

The room was quiet. Graçay looked at her for a long moment, and then, with the faintest inclination of his head, he set the crown's first paper aside and nodded to his clerks, and the real conference began.

* * *

It ran nine days, and the work was the old work of every peace: turning positions into numbers and numbers into clauses.

The southern fiefs were the hardest. Paris could not withdraw entirely without confessing the forfeit void; Jeanne could not accept a French garrison on her roads without making the whole war pointless. The compromise, when it emerged from three nights of Foulques's drafting, was the homage. The three fiefs were acknowledged as ancient holdings of the Vauréal house, but their duke or duchess would do homage for them to the French crown in the old form, once, at the king's court, at a time of peace. No garrison, no governor, no French officer within their borders; the homage was personal, feudal, and largely ceremonial, but it gave Paris the sentence it could take home: the crown's suzerainty recognized. In exchange, the forfeit declaration was annulled and struck from the rolls, and every French claim on the northern towns and the chartered cities was withdrawn in perpetuity.

"The homage will cost you with the imperial party," Viray warned her, in the private room they used for the alliance's own quarrels. "Von Zollern will write to Vienna that you've bent the knee."

"Von Zollern can write whatever he likes; the Emperor has never sent a single gun. An homage performed in peacetime at the king's court costs me a journey and a speech. A garrison costs me a road. I know the price of both." She turned the draft. "Also note the next clause. The crown recognizes the city charters as the settled and ancient customs of the dominion. Paris signs that, and no future French agent can walk into Sainterre as the champion of the city's liberties against its duchess. I'm buying the cities with a bow."

The marriage portion became a cash payment, euphemized as a dowry of reconciliation: twenty-five thousand écus, paid in three installments, against the return of all prisoners of rank without further ransom and the crown's formal release of its claims on the captured ordnance. It was the part she hated, the gold that would go to the treasury that had burned Sainte-Florence, and she said so, and Dolfin, who had drifted into the room like a well-dressed shadow, said, "Madame, every peace includes an indemnity paid by the winner. It is the price of making the loser able to sign. Think of it as the final installment of the loan. It is certainly cheaper than another siege."

The prisoner exchange took a day, the trade clauses two, and the eternal question of the river tolls an entire evening, and on the ninth day the Treaty of Heurtois was engrossed in fair copies, sealed with the arms of France, Vauréal, Lorraine, and the bishopric, and signed at a table that had been worn smooth by centuries of other people's wars. Graçay signed with a steady hand, and when the clerks had sanded the ink he said, low enough that only she and Thiébaut heard, "Madame, I served the late duke your father in the field once, and I thought him the most difficult man in Christendom. He would be proud of you, and Paris would not have believed that was meant as a compliment."

"I will take it as one, and put it in the book of the unusual."

Von Zollern, in his final audience, made one last attempt at the imperial marriage, vaguely, in the language of future understandings, and she answered him in the same language, with the same smiles, and sent him back to Vienna with a gift of Sainterre broadcloth and no commitments at all. The Empire, she had learned, was best managed by being treated as a letter that never required a decisive answer.

* * *

The other trial waited for her at home, and it was the harder of the two.

Hautperthuis was brought to the Haut-Tertre in the third week of February, under a strong guard, in a closed wagon with his name on the warrant, and the great hall was made ready for the court of peers that the law of the dominion required for a lord of his blood. Every vassal of standing was summoned; the bishops and the aldermen and the officers of the cities sat behind the lords, because Jeanne had ordained that the trial would be open and its record printed, and the whole dominion would be able to read the evidence against one of its own great names.

He played his part well, in his furred gown and his grey beard, the warm smile held in place like armor. He denied nothing that the documents proved and admitted nothing that they did not. The court heard the lease records of the planted tenants, the fair-day meetings with the French factor, the steward's accounts with the Paris paymaster, the correspondence from the captured fortress, and the chain of the poison plot, from the groom Colas to the steward in his service. Lisette's map, enlarged and fair-copied, hung on the wall of the hall, the spider's web with his name at its center. His counsel argued necessity, the duchess's marriage to a foreigner, the ancient right of lords to choose their side in a disputed succession; and the court heard it, because she had insisted every argument be heard at length, so that no man could later say the great lord had been rushed.

She spoke last, from the high chair, in the voice of the council chamber, and the hall was so full that the silence had a weight.

"Gui de Hautperthuis. You were of my blood and my council, and your lands were the shield of my southern road. The court has found the facts, and the facts are not in dispute: you took a foreign pension, you planted foreign soldiers on my marches, you delivered my fortress by the murder of its captain, and your steward's hand reached into my husband's cup. The law calls this high treason, and the law's sentence is the extinguishing of the bloodline and the forfeiture of the name. In mercy, and because I will not have the children of your house judged for the father, I am striking the attainder's clause against the blood. Your children keep their lives and such property as was your wife's. The name of Hautperthuis as a lordship ends with you. The lands return to the crown, and the tenants keep their holdings and their rights, because they were never the traitors."

She paused, and made herself say the rest, the part she had rehearsed alone in the chamber until the words stopped shaking.

"For your person, the sentence is death, by the axe, as befits your rank, in the courtyard of this palace, at dawn on the day after tomorrow. I will not pretend that this is not a grief. You dandled me as a child. You ate my father's bread. That is why the law must be seen to reach even the people who have memories. Take him away."

The court rose in silence, and he went out between the guards with the smile finally gone and his real face visible at last, frightened and old, and Jeanne sat a moment longer in the high chair with her hands folded over the child heavy inside her, and then she rose and walked out through the watching hall with the duchess's pace, and did not let herself stop until she was in the privacy of the solar, where she was sick into a basin for the first and only time of her pregnancy.

* * *

She went down to the garderobe tower the night before the execution, because she had learned in the Rostaing business that a ruler who cannot look at the people she condemns will eventually condemn people she cannot look at. Foulques tried to dissuade her; Thiébaut did not, and came with her as far as the cell door, where he posted himself with his back to the wall.

The cell was dry and clean, which was itself a statement, and Hautperthuis sat at its table with a lamp and a cup and the calm of a man who has finished negotiating. He rose when she came in, and bowed, and waited for her to sit before he sat again, the old habits of the court surviving everything else.

"You shouldn't have come, cousin. It will cost you sleep."

"I have a budget for sleeplessness. I came because the trial was for the record and this is for us. Tell me one true thing, without the smile. Why? You had the richest lordship in the dominion. The road repairs, the leases, the office I refused you — none of it explains the gates of Mont d'Or."

He was quiet a while, turning the cup in his hands. "Because I could not be ruled by a girl and a foreign duke, and I was too old to learn. My whole life, the world had a shape: the great houses, the blood, the council of men who owned the land. In one year you took it apart with charters and bankers and printed sheets and women's guilds, and every time I looked, there was less room in it for men like me. Paris offered me the old world back. I believed the French would win because I wanted the French to win, and wanting is the poor man's intelligence." He looked up. "There. The true thing. Does it help?"

"It helps me understand the sentence isn't only about you."

"It never is. That's why they make a show of it." A pause. "The children, Jeanne. My boys."

"Are mine now, by the court's order and my own. They'll be educated with the orphans' school, under my eye, and they'll enter adult life with their mother's property and no father's name to carry. I will not make the son pay the father's invoice. It's the one part of my father's practice I've deliberately unlearned."

Something cracked in the polished face, and he looked away at the wall. "Then you're a greater ruler than he was."

"I'm a different one. Whether greater is for the ledgers to decide." She rose. "I won't come again. Make your confession properly; I've asked the bishop to send a man worth talking to. Goodbye, Gui."

"Goodbye, little duchess." He said it without mockery, almost gently. "You were always the one in that family who could count."

Outside the cell, Thiébaut fell into step beside her in the torchlit corridor, and said nothing, and after a while she said, to the wall ahead, "He wanted the old world back. That's the treason. Not the gold. The nostalgia."

"Most treasons are. Gold is just the costume." He offered his good arm, and she took it, and they climbed the tower stairs together into the cold upper air.

* * *

She had decided to be present in the same way she decided every hard thing: by writing the decision down the night before, so that the morning could not argue with it. The warrant lay on the table with the rest of the state papers, and underneath it, in the private hand, the single line: _A ruler who cannot watch the sentence cannot sign it. I will watch._

The courtyard was black with people before dawn, the lower town and the court and the soldiers of the guard in their ordered ranks, and the scaffold stood against the wall of the chapel where her father lay, black-draped and plain, with the block and the sawdust and the great two-handed sword of the headsman, because she had refused the axe's clumsiness and paid for the executioner of Nancy, a man famous for one stroke. Thiébaut wanted her to watch from the hall window; she refused, and came down into the courtyard in a dark gown and a thick veil, and stood at the front of the dais with Brive on one side and Foulques on the other, and the child moving inside her like a second heartbeat.

Gui came out at the first bell, in a dark velvet gown, his hands bound before him with a silken cord, the privilege of his blood. He had made his confession and heard Mass, and he walked the scaffold with the careful dignity of a man performing the last scene of a long career. At the top he turned to the court and the crowd, and his eyes found the veiled figure on the dais, and he said, in a voice that carried, "Madame, I was wrong, and I have written it. The children are not to blame."

It was the best thing he had ever done, and she inclined her head once, under the veil, so that he could see he had been heard. Then he knelt, and put his head on the block, and the Nancy executioner did his work in the single stroke for which he was paid, and the sound went through the courtyard like a door closing, and the crowd, which had expected a longer noise, let out its breath all at once.

She did not look away. She had promised herself she would not, and she kept the promise the way she kept all promises, as a matter of bookkeeping. The body was taken for burial in the church of a distant convent, without the lord's tomb, and the head was placed over the southern gate for the statutory month and then, at her private order, taken down and buried with it, because she had no use for a skull and the children did not deserve one.

In the chamber afterwards, with the doors shut and the guard outside, the composure held for exactly as long as it took her to reach the chair, and then she wept, hard and without dignity, for the man who had dandled her, for the boy Philippe and the girl at Sainte-Florence and all the names in the books, for the child who would be born into a country that required its mother to sign such warrants, and for the girl she had been, who had once believed that being a duchess was mostly a matter of Latin and ledgers.

Thiébaut came in without knocking, because the rule about knocking had died somewhere in the siege, and he said nothing at all, which was correct, and sat in the chair beside her and put his good arm around her and let the crying run its course. When it was over and she had blown her nose on the state handkerchief, he said, "The people will say you were merciful."

"The people will be wrong. I was precise. Mercy would have been a man who didn't make the choice." She dried her cheeks. "His children, Thiébaut. I want them brought up at court, under my eye and my charge, the boys educated with the orphans' school and the girls with the others. The blood survives. Only the treason is buried."

"An expensive mercy."

"Everything I do is expensive. Ask Dolfin." She looked at the sealed window, beyond which the courtyard was being scrubbed. "He said he had written it. Find the letter. I want to read what a traitor writes when the block is in the room."

The letter came that evening, in the bishop's keeping, and it was short and not what she expected. It did not beg. It gave the names of the last French contacts in the dominion, three of them unknown even to Lisette's map, and it ended with a paragraph in a hand less steady than the rest: that he had been wrong about her from the first, that he had believed a woman and a foreign marriage meant a duchy for the taking, and that the country she was making was one he could no longer read, which was perhaps why he had chosen the one he knew. She read it twice, and put it in the private archive with Foulques's sealed confession and the green case's documents, the growing library of the locked rooms she had chosen to open.

* * *

The ratifications came in through the spring. Paris sealed first, because Paris needed the peace more than it needed the grievance; the Emperor's court sent a chilly note that recognized nothing and ignored everything; the cities held bonfires when the charter clause of the treaty was read aloud at the market crosses, and Sainterre rang its bells for a day. The disbandment began, the careful paying off of the contracted regiments, the Landsknechts marching east with their pockets full and their reputations enhanced, the feudal lords going home to their manors with new stories and old grievances.

Foulques, in the chancellery, began the long work of codifying the emergency customs into ordinary law: the charter forms, the city courts, the women's guild, the book of the dead, the printed news sheet that was now simply a part of how the dominion governed itself. The law, he said to her once, was the memory of the state, and the state had learned a great many things in two years that it must not now forget.

Bride took the army's reductions with the grumbling of an old soldier and the private relief of a man who had survived his third war, and Rostaing, reinstated and quieted, was given the southern command at Mont d'Or, the fortress that had made and unmade him, and went to it with a garrison and a clerk and no expression at all when the warrant was read.

The peace was proclaimed at Aubecourt on a bright March morning, from the steps of the cathedral, and Jeanne made sure the ceremony answered the trials and the executions with something better than silence. The treaty was read aloud in the vernacular, all of it, the homage clause and the charter clauses and the names of the restored towns, while the town and the court and the companies stood in the square, and when the heralds cried the final words the bells began and did not stop for an hour.

She had the proclamation printed as well, in Verhoeven's shop and its two competitors, and a copy went to every parish and guildhall in the dominion, with the charter guarantees marked in red ink at the edge. The same sheet carried the news of Mont d'Or's new garrison and the reopening of the southern road, so that the peace arrived in every village already attached to the practical blessings of bread, tolls, and safety.

"Paris will call it propaganda," Foulques said, watching the bundles go out with the couriers.

"Paris has been calling things propaganda since the first stone was carved. We'll call it literacy, and let the difference show in the tax receipts." She watched the square below, where the townspeople were drifting toward the taverns in the holiday mood she had declared at the treasury's expense. "Also note the homage. I'll perform it myself, in Paris, when the child is born and the roads are safe. A duchess who bows once in public and keeps every gate in private has the better end of the bargain."

Thiébaut, beside her with the sling gone and the arm still stiff, said, "I'll come with you. Someone has to keep the king's courtiers from quoting the wrong precedents at you."

"Then it's a progress. Put it in the calendar for the autumn. First the lying-in, then the homage, then the next emergency, in the usual order."

And the child, oblivious to treaties and attainders, grew and waited in its own time, while the snows retreated up the hills and the river opened and the first barges of the peacetime trade came up the Ortelle with their holds full of everything that was not war.
