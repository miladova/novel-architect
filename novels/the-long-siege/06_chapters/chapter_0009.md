# Chapter Nine — The Charter and the Cannon

Sainterre announced itself from a league away by the smell: wet wool, fuller's earth, coal smoke, and the particular sour reek of the dye vats, a stink so famous in the north that travelers claimed you could navigate to the city blindfolded. Jeanne's army came over the last ridge in the afternoon light and saw the whole cloth metropolis spread in the valley of the upper Ortelle, its walls long and black with the smoke of forty thousand hearths, its three great gatehouses wearing the red and white of the guilds instead of the red and silver of the duchess.

"Every gate," Brive said, beside her. "They've taken the ducal arms off every gate. That's not revolt in a hurry. That's revolt with a carpenter's bill."

Thiébaut reined up on her other side, studying the walls through the same professional calm with which he studied everything. "New palisades outside the eastern gate. See there, where the suburb starts? They're extending the covered way. And the ditch has been cleared. Someone in that city has read the same books I have."

"De Key has a journeyman carpenter's training," Foulques said, from his mule. "His father built guildhall benches. The dangerous revolutionaries are always the ones who can read a measurement."

The host made camp on the rising ground west of the city, between the river and the Ghent road, the position her father's maps called the White Field, where the ducal armies had traditionally assembled to reason with Sainterre. Tents went up in the ordered rows Thiébaut's sergeants insisted on, the horse lines upwind, the latrines down, the guns parked with their muzzles toward the walls. There was no assault to prepare and everyone knew it; three thousand men could not surround a city of forty thousand, and the war in the south needed every company whole. The army's work here was to be seen: a fact in armor, an argument in steel.

Jeanne sat her horse a while at the camp's edge, watching the walls. The walls watched back. She could see tiny figures moving along the parapet, the glint of a crossbow's steel, the red-and-white banners lifting in the wind. Somewhere down there Coppens was a virtual prisoner in his own house, and de Key held the cloth hall and the gates, and the printing press she had tried to license was turning out the broadsheets that the camp's pickets had already found nailed to roadside trees: _No Foreign Husband. No Old Taxes. The City Is Its Own._

"Will they fight?" Jean asked, ambling over with an apple. "I've money on two days of shouting and a nice fat tax bill."

"They'll fight if we make them," Thiébaut said. "The question is whether the city wants what de Key is selling. Cities rarely do, all of it. They want the tolls. They don't want the French."

"Then we give them the tolls and make the French frightening," Jeanne said. "See to the battery, my lord. Put _Marguerite_ where the Ghent road can see her, and let the gunners take all day about it. I want the walls to count every ox."

* * *

Coppens came out at dusk through the river postern, in a fisherman's boat, wrapped in a sailcloth that smelled of herring, and he arrived at the duchess's pavilion with the sailcloth still around his shoulders and the face of a man who had not slept since his last audience at the Haut-Tertre. He knelt with difficulty, his knees not being what they had been, and rose only when she told him twice.

"Madame. He has three gates and the cloth hall and the weavers' guild, which is four thousand men with crossbows and the arms to use them. The fullers follow him because he promises them the dye houses. The dyers follow me because they know who pays their accounts. The merchants want this over before the London convoy is due, which is in six weeks. The common people want bread, and de Key has been telling them your tax is why it costs double."

"And the broadsheets?"

"Printed at night in the cellar of the cloth hall, passed out free. A man called Verhoeven, a printer from Bruges. He sets type in the vernacular so the apprentices can read it, and the apprentices read it aloud in the taverns. Madame, the broadsheets are doing the work of an army. There are men in my own guild who voted for me last year who now call me the duchess's dog." Coppens's heavy face worked. "I am not a brave man. I came out because the alternative is watching the city I built burn for an idea that won't pay a single weaver."

"Sit. Drink." She poured the wine herself, which made the old guildsman blink. "The charter framework my chancellor sent. Can the moderates carry it against de Key?"

"Not as it stands. He'll say the six-year toll term is a trick to take the tolls back. He'll say the licensed militia is your garrison in our clothes. He'll say the war contribution is a dowry for the Lorraine mercenary." Coppens drank, wiped his mouth, and grew steadier, the way a trader steadies once the real bargaining starts. "But if the charter named the city's own officers for the tolls, and the militia's powder stayed in the city's arsenal under two keys, one alderman's and one ducal, and the war contribution were fixed at the figure we offer rather than the figure you demand, then I could put it to the guilds and carry it. The fullers would still shout. The fullers always shout. The rest would vote."

Thiébaut, who had said nothing, leaned forward in his camp chair. "Maître Coppens. If the charter is granted and de Key refuses to accept the guilds' vote, what does he do?"

The old man looked at him with reluctant respect. "He calls the vote a fraud. He arms the weavers. He shuts the gates and dares you to storm them, because he believes the French will reach Sainterre before you can. The Sire de Graçay has sent him letters, my lord duke. I have seen one. Paris promises the city its ancient freedoms, meaning whatever keeps it out of the duchess's hands."

"Has he answered them?"

"I don't know. I think he's keeping the French as his second bidder." Coppens set the cup down. "Madame, if you grant the charter in full, publicly, tomorrow, in the city, with the guilds assembled, you make de Key choose between a vote he might lose and a riot he can't disguise. I ask you to come inside the walls. Under safe conduct sealed by every guild, including his, because he won't dare refuse the seal and look afraid. Come with a small guard. Let the city see you're not the woman in his broadsheets."

The pavilion went quiet. Foulques's face said everything about chancellors and armed cities. Brive, who had come to stand at the flap, said, "No," before the word was fully out of Coppens's mouth.

Jeanne looked at Thiébaut. He was turning a small knife over and over in his hands, the way he did when he was thinking, his eyes elsewhere. "It's a risk," he said at last. "But it's the right one. A ruler who won't enter her own city hands the story to the man who will. I'll come inside with fifty lances and the household guard. If the gates close, the battery answers, and the first answer goes into the cloth hall."

"You'll come inside with twenty lances," Jeanne said. "Fifty says I'm afraid of my own city. Twenty says I'm not. And we bring the charter, fair-copied and sealed, so there is nothing to negotiate away in the room. Maître Coppens, send your boat back tonight. Tell the guilds the duchess will speak at the cloth hall at noon tomorrow. Tell de Key his safe conduct is in the same document as everyone else's. He can be there or explain to his weavers why he wasn't."

* * *

They entered Sainterre at ten in the morning, through the Ghent gate, between ranks of the city militia in red-and-white jacks with their partisans held rigid as fence posts. Twenty Lorraine lances in unpolished steel, no banners but the single joined arms standard; the household guard around the duchess's horse; Foulques with the charter in its leather tube; and at the very front, alone on her grey mare with no guard between herself and the street, Jeanne in the steel-grew gown and the ducal mantle, riding through her own angry city at a walking pace.

The streets were packed. The silence of a watching crowd is a physical thing, a pressure on the skin, broken by small sounds: a child's voice shushed, the clop of hooves on the cobbles, the slop of dye water in a gutter. Faces in every window. Apprentices perched on signboards. Women with folded arms on the doorsteps, the same flat assessment she had got from the fishwife in Aubecourt, multiplied by ten thousand. Once an egg splattered against a wall ten feet away, thrown from an alley, and the guard's hands went to swords, and she said, without turning her head, "No one moves. An egg is a vote. Let it vote."

Thiébaut rode half a length behind her left shoulder, where he could see both the street and the rooftops, and she could feel the controlled readiness of him like a drawn bow, and took from it a steadiness she would not have admitted to needing.

The cloth hall stood at the city's heart, a vast Gothic barn of brick and glass, its square full of people, its steps crowded with the armed weavers of de Key's faction in leather jacks and red armbands, crossbows on their shoulders. The broadsheets had been pasted across the hall's doors. A man was reading one aloud in a carrying voice as the procession arrived: _"...and the foreign duke will garrison your houses and take your daughters, while the duchess counts his gold..."_ He stopped when he saw her. The square took a breath.

She dismounted at the steps, and the crowd saw the guard stay at the bottom, and she climbed the steps alone except for Foulques with the charter and Thiébaut at her back, and stood where all of Sainterre could see her, in the shadow of the hall's great carved doorway.

"People of Sainterre." She did not shout; she had learned in the Aubecourt square that a lowered voice travels. The front ranks hushed to hear, and the hush spread. "I have not come with the army at my back, though it is on my field and paid by my hand. I have come with a charter. Before I speak of it, I will say one thing plainly, because I will not have my city governed by lies printed in cellars. My husband holds no office, garrison, or patent in Vauréal. His soldiers camp beyond your walls at my pay and my order, and they will not enter this city except at the city's own request. Read the treaty. It is a public document, and I have had copies printed, by a licensed printer, for anyone who can read and anyone who can listen. The broadsheet that says otherwise is the work of a man who fears your literacy more than he loves your freedom."

A sound went through the square, half murmur, half laugh, the sound of a crowd recognizing an argument it could use at the tavern.

Then de Key came out of the hall.

He was younger than she expected, Willem de Key, thirty-five and broad, with the long arms of a weaver and the face of an apostle in a poor church: lean, burning, sincere. He wore the weavers' red armband and no weapon that showed, and he came down the steps toward her with the ease of a man on his own ground, followed by a dozen hard young men in the same red.

"Madame la duchesse." His voice was made for halls, deep and clear, and it carried to the back of the square without effort. "The city is honored. We note that you come with a charter in a tube and an army on the hill, and we are curious which one you expect us to read first."

A roar, half approval, half unease. Letting him speak was a calculated risk; she had discussed it with Thiébaut at midnight and concluded that a demagogue silenced in public was a martyr, while a demagogue answered in public was a man with a problem.

"Read the charter first, de Key, since the army costs you nothing and the charter costs me a great deal." She turned so that the square, not the man, was her audience. "It confirms your aldermen, elected by the guild masters. It gives your city court its trade and labor jurisdiction. It fixes the tolls for six years. It licenses your militia under officers who hold the duchess's commission, with the powder under two keys, one of them your aldermen's. And in exchange, the city votes the war contribution it has itself proposed, keeps the peace, and admits the ducal judges for crown cases. That is the whole of it. No garrison. No new tax. No husband's officers. You may inspect every word, and so may every man here, because I have had it printed."

Foulques unslung the tube, and her clerk raised the fair copy high, and from the crowd a schoolmaster was pushed forward, willing or otherwise, and began to read it aloud in the vernacular, his voice cracking at first and then finding its stride. The square listened. It was the thing de Key could not allow, Jeanne knew: a listening crowd. A crowd listening was a crowd thinking, and his power lived in the gap where thought might have been.

He struck at the end of the first paragraph.

"Six years!" he shouted over the schoolmaster's voice. "The tolls come back in six years! A charter with an ending is a lease, and a lease is what a landlord gives a tenant. Are we tenants in our own city?" And then, turning it, finding the deeper wound: "And the militia powder under her key! When the French come, will we ask the duchess's keeper for leave to defend our wives? This is not a charter. This is a bridle, and she has brought a soldier to hold the reins."

The red-armband men took it up, a chant of _bridle, bridle_, and the square began to move, the front ranks pressing, the armed weavers on the steps spreading out with the practiced motion of men who had planned the geometry. Thiébaut's hand went to Jeanne's elbow, not pulling, just there, and over her shoulder he said one word, low: "Steel."

"Not yet," she said.

De Key was still speaking, arms wide, the apostle's face lifted, and the crowd was his again in the way crowds are, swinging like a weather vane, and she saw the young men around him produce what they had been carrying under their jackets: not crossbows yet, but clubs, iron bolts, the cobblestones of the square pried loose in advance. This was not a riot. It was a performance with a script, and she had a brief, cold certainty that the script's final scene was her retreating from Sainterre in disorder, or not retreating.

The first stone came from the alley beside the hall, a good throw, and it took the clerk on the side of the head. The fair copy went red. He went down, and the crowd's sound changed, became the noise a mob makes when it crosses its own threshold.

"Now," said Thiébaut.

The twenty lances came up the steps not as cavalry but as a wedge of armored men, shoulder to shoulder, and the household guard closed around her with the precision of long drill, and somewhere below, the city militia, Coppens's moderates, the dyers and merchants with their two-key argument to win, hesitated and then, at a barked order from an old guildsman with a gold chain, formed across the square's far side, separating the press of apprentices from the hall. Two militias, one mob, and the armed weavers in between.

What followed lasted perhaps ten minutes and felt like an hour. The red-armband men charged the steps and were met by the Lorraine wedge, which did not draw steel but used the flat of arms and the weight of plate, and the crossbows that appeared were answered by the guard's crossbows coming up in a single trained motion, and de Key's men, whatever their courage, discovered the difference between enthusiasm and discipline. A weaver got within three feet of Jeanne with a cobblestone raised, and Thiébaut took him down without looking at him, his left arm moving in a short, awful, efficient arc, and the man folded onto the steps with his teeth broken.

The killing was an accident, and everyone there would claim it was a massacre. A boy, sixteen or so, one of the fullers, panicked in the crush and loosed a crossbow into the air, and the bolt came down into the throat of a Lorraine man-at-arms, who made a surprised sound and sat down and died on the cloth hall steps with his hand at his neck. After that the guard's bolts went out in a volley, and four weavers fell, two dead in the square and two more who would die in the night, and the mob broke because mobs always break once the dying starts, pouring into the side streets and the alleys and the dye houses, leaving the square to the militia and the blood and the broadsheets fluttering in the wind.

"Hold the steps," Thiébaut said to his sergeant, and then to Jeanne, quietly, "Are you hurt?"

"No." Her heart was hammering so hard she could hear it. "The clerk?"

"Alive. Head wound. The surgeon's with him." He looked at the dead man-at-arms, then at the two weavers in the square, and his face was the face she had seen in the map room, the one that did not waste anything. "This was coming whatever we did. Now it has a price, and de Key has to explain why he started a fight he lost."

* * *

De Key had fled the square before the volley; she learned that within the hour, through the aldermen's watch, out the river postern with three companions and a sealed letter in his coat, and the letter's destination was the French camp. Coppens, emerging from his house with the careful timing of a man who had spent the riot being unreachable, called the guild council that evening under the guard of both militias, and the vote, when it came, was not close. The weavers' guild abstained in a body and lost the argument by it; the other eight guilds accepted the charter, and the war contribution with it, and the old cloth merchant signed his name as the city's first elected lead alderman with a hand that shook only slightly.

She visited the press before the sealing, because a duchess who intended to license printing ought at least to know what the thing looked like. Verhoeven's cellar workshop was low and black and smelled of ink and lamp oil, and the printer himself, a thin Bruxellois with ink in his cuticles, received her as a man receives the weather: with resignation and an eye for shelter.

The press stood in the center of the room like a small cathedral of oak and iron, and on the composing table lay the loose type of de Key's last broadside, its broken sentences still legible in reverse. Jeanne picked up a line and turned it, feeling the metal's weight. "How many of these can you print in a night?"

"Four hundred sheets, madame, with two men and candles. The guildsmen distributed them free at the gates." Verhoeven's eyes were watchful. "I am a craftsman. I print what I am paid to print, as a smith shoes whatever horse is brought."

"A smith who shoes a thief's horse is hanged with the thief. But I am not here to hang you. I am here to hire you." She set the type down. "The charter's full text, five hundred copies, in the vernacular, posted at every guildhall and read at every market cross in the three cities. Then the marriage treaty, the same. Then, each week, a printed sheet of the news of the war and the council, under the city court's mark, sold for a denier. Let de Key's next broadsheet arrive in a town that has already read the truth in a licensed sheet. Rumor is cheap, Verhoeven. I intend to make it compete with a professional."

The printer's face did the slow arithmetic of his kind. "A weekly sheet, madame, has never been done north of the Rhine."

"Then we shall be the first, which costs nothing in ink and a great deal in courage, of which you appear to have a reasonable supply, having printed rebellion in a cellar for a month." She turned to Foulques. "Pay him in advance, from the city's contribution. A press that owes the duchess money is a press that thinks twice."

Up the cellar stairs, in the grey light, the chancellor said quietly, "You are arming him."

"I am arming everyone, Maître Foulques. The only question is whether I hold a share of the weapons. My grandfather kept the sword monopoly. This century is issuing new kinds."

Jeanne sealed the charter in the cloth hall the next morning, under the windows where the broadsheets had been scraped off, with the square full again but quieter, and the four dead weavers' bodies laid decently in the church of Saint-Géry at her expense, and the Lorraine man-at-arms buried with his arms and a mass paid from the privy purse. She attended all the funerals, including the weavers', and walked through the streets on foot afterward with no guard but Coppens and Thiébaut, and let the city look at her.

"He'll come back," Coppens said, low, as they passed the shuttered weavers' quarter. "De Key. With the French behind him."

"Then keep your powder dry and your aldermen honest." She stopped at the corner where the first stone had come from; the alley was empty now. "Maître Coppens, I granted this charter under duress and we both know it. Write that down in your city's book, because I want it remembered. I want your successors to know the duchess paid for this city's peace with a piece of her own sovereignty, and that the city paid for it in taxes and in blood on these steps. A charter that costs nothing binds no one. This one cost us both. That's why it will hold."

The old guildsman looked at her for a long moment, and then did a thing he had not done at the Haut-Tertre, for all his bows there. He bowed as the city bows to itself, fist to chest, slow and full. "It will hold, madame. On my guild's name."

* * *

In the camp that night, the tents struck and the army preparing to turn south, Thiébaut found her alone by the horse lines, watching the gunners grease _Marguerite_'s bronze throat.

"One man-at-arms, four townsmen, a clerk with a bandaged head, and a charter that will be quoted against every duchess of Vauréal for the next century." He stood beside her, not touching. "Do you count it a victory?"

"I count it a marriage." She kept her eyes on the gun. "Something expensive, partly against my will, that I now have to make work. The city voted the taxes, the gates are open, the river trade starts again at dawn, and de Key is a guest of the French, which tells every waverer in my dominion what his fine words were worth. Yes. I count it a victory. I also count that boy's face, and I expect I'll keep counting it."

"He was sixteen. The fullers confirmed it."

"I know. Lisette got the name. His mother's a widow in the weavers' quarter. I'm settling a pension on her, from the privy purse, not the treasury. A treasury payment makes him a riot casualty. A privy payment makes him my debt." She turned to face him at last. "You moved very fast on those steps."

"I had a great deal to protect." He said it flatly, without romance, a statement of fact. "Also twenty years of practice. Jeanne. The next time a man raises a stone within ten feet of you, I will not wait for permission. I thought I should state the rule, since you're so fond of rules."

"Stated and accepted." She looked back at the dark bulk of the city, its thousands of lamps reflected in the river. "Now take me back to the pavilion. We have a fortress to save, and I have a letter to write to a commandant with sixty-one men, and I intend to tell him help is coming before the French tell him anything at all."

They walked together through the ordered camp, past the sentries who presented arms and the campfires where the soldiers were already turning the day's blood into the day's stories, and behind them Sainterre burned its broadsheets in a bonfire on the guildhall square, the smoke going up straight and black into the spring night.
