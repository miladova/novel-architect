# Chapter Fourteen — Letters from Paris

The French courier was taken at the lower crossing of the Nied, in the grey first week of February, by a Lorraine patrol that had learned to watch for single riders on good horses with plain saddlebags. He swallowed nothing and fought not at all, being a clerk of the postal service rather than a soldier, and the dispatches reached the Haut-Tertre still in their sealed tubes, with the courier himself under a polite guard in the gatehouse, because the Vauréal chancellery had discovered that captured clerks were worth more intact than hanged.

Foulques broke the seals in the round chamber with the reverence of a priest at the altar, and the council spent the afternoon inside the enemy's mind.

There were four letters. The first, from the Regent's war secretary to Graçay, laid out the spring plan in the calm, bored prose of the Paris bureaucracy: a two-pronged advance once the roads dried, the main force against Mont d'Or to pin the field army, a second column through the eastern marches with the cooperation of "the party known to us," which the margin identified nowhere but which every person at the table read as Hautperthuis. The second was a paymaster's list, and it told them the French army was three months in arrears and borrowing against the next year's taxes, which Jeanne underlined twice. The third was an agent's report on the Sainterre charter, shrewd and hostile, noting that the chartered cities were now the duchess's strongest anchor rather than her weakest wound, and recommending that Paris find its own city to champion.

The fourth was Anne de France's own hand, short and silky, addressed to "our well-beloved cousin the Duchess of Vauréal," and it was this letter that the council read last, in silence, because it was not intended for them at all.

It offered peace. The Regent wrote with apparent warmth of the ancient friendship between the crown and the house of Vauréal, regretted the misunderstandings of the forfeit, and proposed terms: the southern fiefs to remain under French protection as a dowry of reconciliation, a marriage portion in gold to the crown's war treasury, a French garrison, politely called an escort, at Saint-Vaast, and in exchange the immediate withdrawal of all French claims on the northern towns and the Regent's personal guarantee of the duchess's sovereignty under the ancient treaties. The letter ended with an invitation to a conference at Heurtois, under the bishop's roof, at the duchess's convenience, and a postscript in the same elegant hand: _It is the part of wise princes to buy cheaply what wars sell dear. I write as one woman of business to another._

"She's read my ledgers," Jeanne said.

"She's read everyone's ledgers." Foulques turned the page. "Note the structure. Every concession she offers concerns lands she does not hold. Every guarantee she asks concerns the door into your country. This is not a peace offer. It is an inventory."

"It is also," Thiébaut said, from the map, "the first sign her treasury is nervous. You don't write letters like this in a winning year. The paymaster's list is the truth of it. Paris wants the war paused so it can afford to restart it."

"Then the answer is not no. The answer is a courtship." Jeanne set the letter down. "We accept the conference in principle, at a date after the roads dry, and we send negotiators with instructions to be endlessly courteous and endlessly incapable of agreeing. Every week Paris spends talking is a week she isn't paying an army to march. Foulques, draft it yourself. Be so reasonable that she cannot find the insult."

"While we use the same weeks to finish our own preparations." Brive's beard moved in what might have been a smile. "Madame, you would have made a fine Frenchwoman."

"I am a fine Frenchwoman. That's why I know exactly what she's doing."

* * *

The Rostaing business came to them three days later, carried not by dispatch but by a priest, which was how the worst things traveled in winter.

He was the parish curate of a village called Argnon, on the southeastern marches, and he had ridden two days in snow to reach the court, and he asked for the duchess by name and would speak to no one else. She received him in the justice room, with Foulques and Thiébaut and Brive, and the old man knelt on the cold flags and wept before he could speak, and when he did speak the room went very still.

Captain Rostaing, the hard young cavalryman whose courage at the fords had made him the army's favorite, had been scouring the eastern marches for the French raiding parties that winter. At Argnon, the curate said, the village had given shelter for one night to a dozen armed strangers who claimed to be disbanded soldiers and who had paid for their bread in good silver. They had been French scouts. Rostaing's company had found their trail, reached the village at dawn, and asked the headman why he had fed the king's enemies. The headman had answered that he had not known, which was probably true, and that the strangers had had swords, which was certainly true.

Rostaing had held a summary inquiry at the well, in the snow, with no witnesses called and no clerk. He had concluded that the village was a nest of traitors, and he had burned it. Not the houses alone. The curate's list, read out in his shaking voice, named twenty-seven dead: four men, nine women, fourteen children and the aged, trapped in the church where they had been put for safety when the thatch went up. The scouts themselves were long gone. Rostaing had reported a successful action against a hostile village and commended his men, and the report had gone into the war files and would have stayed there if the priest had not walked through the snow.

When he finished, the justice room held the particular silence of men who have just had their own side made unendurable.

Brive spoke first, heavily. "The captain's authority in hostile country does extend to example. There are customs—"

"There are customs for soldiers and laws for villagers." Jeanne's voice was level, which frightened the curate more than shouting would have. "Argnon is in my dominion. Its people are my people, sworn to my peace. There is no hostile country here. There is my country, in which a twenty-eight-year-old captain has made himself judge, jury, and fire over women and children on the word of a trail. Father, leave us the list and your parish register. You have my protection for the journey home, and the village will be rebuilt from the privy purse. No one will ask your name again."

When the priest had gone, she looked at the four men in turn. "Bring him in. Not under arrest yet. I want him to walk into this room believing he's been summoned for a commendation."

* * *

Rostaing came in bright with it, booted and spurred and still carrying the frost of the road, a handsome, reckless man who wore his courage like a decoration. He kissed her ring with the easy confidence of the army's darling, and then read the room, and the confidence did not leave him but became watchful.

She let him tell his own version first, in full, the trail, the strangers, the well-side inquiry, the judgment, the fire. He told it well, without bluster, and she saw the shape of his truth: he believed every word. In his mind he had fought the war the only way it could be fought, and the dead of Argnon were the price of keeping a hundred other villages honest, and a woman at a cushioned court two hundred miles away could not be expected to understand the arithmetic of the snow.

"Captain." She pushed the curate's list across the table. "Twenty-seven. Fourteen of them under twelve years old. Read the names."

He read them. His jaw tightened, but his eyes held. "My lady, war is not a court of pleas. If every hamlet that feeds a French scout is treated as innocent, every hamlet will feed one. I made an example. The eastern marches have had no more sheltered scouts since Argnon. Ask the patrols."

"I am asking you. By what law did you hold court?"

"By the law of necessity, and by my commission as the duchess's officer in the field."

"Your commission binds you to my law, and my law requires a clerk, witnesses, and an appeal to the marshal's court for any judgment of death against a civilian. You held no clerk, called no witnesses, allowed no appeal, and your victims could not flee a burning church. Necessity is the word men use when they have forgotten the law they are paid to defend." She leaned forward. "If your arithmetic is right, captain, why did the French scouts go free while the villagers burned? Why did the armed enemy walk out of your story and the unarmed die in it?"

That got through. For the first time something cracked in the confident face, not guilt exactly but the recognition of a question he had not asked himself. "They were gone. The trail—"

"The trail led to peasants because peasants don't ride away. You killed the people you could catch and called it the people who were guilty. That is not war. That is hunting."

The word hung in the room. Brive, who loved the boy's courage, stared at his boots. Thiébaut had not spoken once; he sat with his arms folded and his face still, watching her, and she felt rather than saw the support held in reserve, the knowledge that this had to be her judgment and not a husband's.

* * *

Brive came to her chamber that evening, which he had never done, and stood by the fire with the look of a man who had rather be in a siege.

"Madame. I'll not waste your time. The captains are talking. The whole camp is talking. They don't defend the women and children; even Rostaing's friends won't defend the church. They say two other things, and you need to hear them from me and not from a barrack wall. First, that the French scouts would have slit those villagers' throats themselves if the village had refused them, so the village was damned either way and Rostaing merely made the damned useful. Second, that a duchess behind a table can afford a conscience the men in the snow cannot, and that if every hard judgment in the field is to be hanged at leisure, no officer will make a hard judgment again, and the next French raid will walk through a duchy of careful men."

He stopped, and added, lower: "His father carried my banner for nineteen years. I held him at his christening. I'm not here for him. I'm here because the army believes what it believes, and you cannot command a belief you don't know."

Jeanne let the fire crackle between them for a moment. "Thank you for telling me instead of resigning. Now answer me two things, marshal, as a soldier. If Rostaing's example works, if the marches grow safer because of Argnon, who is it exactly that is safer? The villagers, or the army? Because the villagers are dead. The example does not protect them. It protects future patrols at the cost of present peasants, and a state that prices its peasants below its patrols is a state whose peasants will open their doors to the first Frenchman who knocks. Second: you say careful officers won't make hard judgments. What hard judgment did Rostaing make? He did not fight. He did not scout. He did not catch an enemy. He burned the one thing in the country that cannot run away. That is not the hard judgment. That is the easy one, and I will not have my army learn that the easy thing wears the name of the hard one."

The old man's face worked. "And the army's belief?"

"The army will believe what it sees. It will see a brave man stripped and sent to the ranks, not hanged, and the law said hanging. It will see the village rebuilt at my expense and the dead named in every church. It will see that courage is honored even when its bearer is condemned, and that peasants have a standing in this duchy that captains cannot burn. Give that five years, Brive, and you'll command an army that fights for the country instead of over it."

"You ask a great deal of five years."

"I ask the same five years from everyone, including myself." She rose and put her hand briefly on his armored forearm. "I am not going to hang your godson. But I am going to make him answer. If he is half the man you think, he will answer at Mont d'Or with a pike in his hand, and the story will end better than this chapter."

Brive stood a while longer, looking into the fire. Then he bowed, one of his old formal bows, and said, "Your father could never have done this. He'd have made the brave man a lord and paid the village in silence. I don't know yet whether you're wiser than him. But you're different, and different is the only thing the French won't have planned for."

* * *

The trial, such as it could be, took two days in the great hall, open to the army's officers and the town, because she would not have a whispered sentence. Foulques presented the law, the curate gave his evidence with the parish register in his hands, and Rostaing, advised now and silent, heard the court find him guilty on three counts: unlawful judgment, unlawful killing, and the destruction of the village without lawful order. The statutory sentence was death, and the hall knew it, and the army's officers sat in their rows with closed faces, because the state was about to execute the bravest captain in the host, and every man there was measuring the thing in his own heart.

She pronounced it herself, from the high chair, in the voice of the council chamber. Then she set the document down and did the harder thing.

"The sentence stands as the law's sentence. I will now exercise the duchess's mercy, and I will give my reasons in public, because mercy without reasons is favor, and favor is how armies become bands." She looked at the kneeling man. "Rostaing, you did not kill for gain or cruelty, and you did not kill in fear. You killed out of the belief that your judgment stood above the law. That belief is the true enemy, and it cannot be pardoned, because it would return with your next command. Therefore: the death sentence is commuted. Your commission, your rank, your company, and your arms are forfeit. You are stripped of the name of captain in this hall, before these witnesses. You will leave the duchy's service and take service with the frontier garrison at Mont d'Or, as a common man-at-arms, under Valvert, at common pay, for the duration of the war. If you earn a name again, it will be a name built from the ranks, and no one will ever say the duchess's law made exceptions for bravery. If you refuse this, the sentence stands. Choose now."

He chose, hoarsely, on his knees, and the hall let out its breath. The officers filed out past him where he knelt, and not one looked away, and she saw in their faces the complicated thing she had wanted: the knowledge that the law reached everyone, and that even the army's darling had been made to answer to a clerk's words and a priest's register.

Afterwards, in the emptying hall, Foulques lingered, and she saw that the chancellor's face had gone grey under its usual composure. "You said something in the justice room," he said, low. "About the law being the only shield the poor have. It moved me, madame. More than I can well say."

"Why? It's true."

"It is true. I once knew a man who forgot it. Who used the law as a weapon against a girl who had done nothing, because a greater man's convenience required it, and who told himself for seven years that necessity was the word." He put a hand to his chest, briefly, as though it pained him. "You will think me strange. I am only an old clerk with a cold conscience. Forgive me."

He went out quickly, leaving her with the echo of it: _a girl who had done nothing. Seven years._ Lisette, materializing as ever from a curtain, said, "He's been keeping too many late nights. All the same—"

"All the same, write down what he said, word for word, in the private book. And Lisette. Find out where he keeps the green case, and whether it ever leaves his room. I've stopped being patient."

Thiébaut found her there among the empty benches, and did not ask her what Foulques had said, and she did not tell him; but when she stood, his hand closed briefly on her shoulder, and the pressure said that whatever the locked room held, she would not open it alone.

* * *

Rostaing left for Mont d'Or two days later, in a common trooper's cloak on a common trooper's horse, with the stripped rank and the frontier orders in his saddlebags, and she watched him go from the gatehouse because a ruler who pronounced such sentences ought to be seen watching their execution.

He stopped at the gate, though his escort moved on, and looked up at her window, and she came down to the courtyard because there was no useful purpose in making a man shout at stone.

"You could have hanged me," he said. The easy confidence was gone; the face that remained was younger and more honest.

"The law could have hanged you. I chose otherwise, and my reasons don't include kindness. Argnon needs to know its dead were answered, and the army needs to know its bravest man is not above the clerks. A hanging would have bought the first and lost the second. Mont d'Or buys both."

"Then I'm an invoice."

"You're a soldier. It's much the same thing." She held his eyes. "Rostaing, the courage was real. The judgment was not. Learn the difference, and the ranks will give the captain back to you, and I'll confirm it. Don't learn it, and the frontier is a long cold place to spend a war. Serve Valvert. He knows the difference better than any man in the dominion."

He saluted, the common soldier's salute rather than the officer's, and rode out under the portcullis. Brive, who had watched from the stair, came down and stood beside her.

"He'll either be the best captain in the army by next winter or drink himself into a ditch."

"Then we make sure the frontier has no wine worth drinking and every reason to make a man of him. Write to Valvert tonight. Hard, fair, and never once my name in the matter. If there's a redemption, it belongs to the ranks."

* * *

The war council that followed the trial was smaller and colder, held in the map room with only Foulques, Brive, Thiébaut, and the Bastard Jean, and the map on the table was marked in two colors, the known French movements in black and the guessed ones in red.

"The party known to us." Thiébaut put his finger on the secretary's phrase. "It has to be Hautperthuis. The second column's route runs through his marches, and no column of that size moves in winter without prepared supply. Someone has contracted the grain and the guides."

"Proof, not rumor." Jeanne studied the red marks. "Lisette has the steward's meeting at the Heurtois fair and the planted tenants, the ex-French sergeants. None of it hangs him. If I move on a vassal of his rank on suspicion, every other wavering lord runs to Paris before the ink dries."

"Then we leave him in place and make his position useless." Thiébaut's finger moved west. "The second column can't start until the Harchies roads are passable. But Graçay is already tightening on the Lorraine forts. Our reports say the fort at Sarrebourg, the one that gave me this shoulder, is being invested by a brigade of gascons and light guns. If it falls before the spring, the French winter inside Lorraine and the war comes to Nancy."

"Can it be relieved in February?" Brive asked, the skepticism plain.

"No sane army marches through the Harchies in February." Thiébaut's mouth did the not-smile. "Which is why the gascons won't post a proper screen. I take six hundred lances and the best of the mounted archers, light, no guns, eight days' biscuit, through the forest tracks. We come out behind their investment, break their supply, and the garrison sallies. Jean knows the forest; he smuggled brandy through it at sixteen."

"I smuggled excellent brandy," Jean said. "The forest remembers me fondly."

Jeanne looked at the map, at the long green smear of the forest between the two rivers, and thought about the arithmetic of risk that was becoming her daily trade. "Six hundred is half our best cavalry. If you're caught in the snow, we lose the mobile army and you, in one week."

"If Sarrebourg falls, we lose Lorraine's southeastern wall and spend the spring rebuilding it. One is a risk. The other is an invoice."

"Then we take the risk, and we make it smaller." She traced a track on the map, one of the charcoal-burners' roads her own foresters used. "The Grisay gunners have been making winter powder and testing short-barreled guns on light sledges. I'll send a convoy with fifty mounted gunners and four of the sledge guns, by the western track, the one no soldier would choose. If you're in the forest eight days, the convoy needs ten, and it comes in behind you. My seal on the orders, my treasury for the cost. If this works, Thiébaut, the army sees a Vauréal column save a Lorraine fort. The alliance stops being a wedding and becomes a fact."

The two brothers exchanged a look. "She's better at this than the war college," Jean said.

"She's better at this than most generals," Thiébaut said. "Do it. And Jeanne — one thing. I'll be ten days out of reach of any courier. Whatever else happens, whatever the council brings, don't move on Hautperthuis while I'm in the forest. I want to be in the room when that door opens."

"You will be. I keep my best quarrels for witnesses I trust."

That night she wrote three documents in her own hand. The first was the rebuilding order for Argnon, with the grants of timber and the remission of five years' dues, and the pensions for every bereaved household, and the curate's name nowhere upon it. The second was the order stripping Rostaing's name from the army lists and entering him as a man-at-arms at Mont d'Or, with a private letter to Valvert: _He is to be treated as any man, neither broken nor favored. I have made him answer to the law. See that the law answers him with fairness._

The third was a short note to the Regent's conference, accepting Heurtois in the politest terms and naming the duchess's envoys, with instructions to discuss everything and settle nothing until the spring had revealed what Paris intended to do.

She sanded the third letter and sat back, and through the connecting door she heard Thiébaut dictating to his clerk the orders for the winter march he was already planning, the desperate mid-February march through the Harchies to relieve the Lorraine fort the French were moving to encircle. She would learn the details in the morning, and argue about them, and probably lose the argument, and finance it anyway.

She closed the ledgers. Outside, the snow fell steadily over the sleeping duchy, white and even, covering the rebuilt village and the stripped captain and the sealed letters from Paris as though the world intended, for one night, to keep no accounts at all.
