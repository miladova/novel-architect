# Chapter One — The Ledger of the Dead

The bell at the lower gate was rung by someone who had forgotten its manners. It went on and on, clapper hammering at bronze without the measured cadence of a herald or a toll collector, and Jeanne came up out of sleep with the sound already in her bones, her hand finding the little knife under the pillow before her eyes were open. The fire in the bedchamber had burned to red caves of ash. Rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere in the Haut-Tertre a door opened and shut, opened and shut, and feet went past at the run.

She did not light a taper. She had slept in her linen shift, and she drew on the dark wool gown hanging at the bedpost, the one with no jewels at the collar, the gown for accounts and audiences of complaint. Her fingers did the laces without asking leave of her wits. Her wits were elsewhere, counting: the gate bell meant news from the south; news from the south in the third week of March could only be one kind of news; her father's army had been in the field nineteen days, and the last dispatch, four days old, had said nothing worth saying.

Lisette was already at the door, breathless, a cloak over her arm. "Riders," the maid said. "A dozen, maybe fewer. The marshal's been called, and the chancellor. My lady, the men on the wall say they're riding wounded horses."

Jeanne took the cloak. In the corridor, rushlights trembled in iron sconces and the stone sweated cold. Two guards fell in behind her with torches, their mail chinking. The grand stair turned down past the row of dead dukes carved in the wall, her grandfather among them with his lion's muzzle and his prayerful hands, and she made herself walk at the pace she had been taught: unhurried, visible, the pace of a person to whom haste is a privilege she cannot afford. She had practiced it behind her father's shoulder for ten years. She had never needed it at night, alone, with a bell screaming.

The lower courtyard was a mouth of rain and torchlight. The riders had come through the gate in a clatter that had woken the kitchens and the kennels and the mews, and now they sat their horses like sacks of wet gravel, or slid from them into the arms of grooms. There were eleven where there should have been thirty in a dispatch party. Their surcoats, the Vauréal red and silver, were black with mud and worse than mud. One man's arm was bound in a strip of someone's shirt, and the bandage had stopped being white. Another held his jaw as though he were afraid it would come off in his hand. The horses hung their heads and steamed, and one had an arrow shaft snapped short in its neck.

Marshal Brive reached the courtyard ahead of her, his white hair sticking up from a nightcap, a cloak thrown over his nightshirt and a sword buckled over the cloak, the sword the one thing he had not forgotten. He had reached the foremost rider and taken him by the shoulder. At the sight of Jeanne on the steps, the old man's face did something it had never done in her memory. It crumpled, and then it rebuilt itself, and both were worse than the crumple.

"My lady," Brive said, and stopped.

The foremost rider got down. He was young, a household knight named Audemer, whom she remembered from the hunting field, clean and laughing and careful always to salute her before he leapt a ditch. He moved like an old man. His left hand was gone at the wrist, the stump dressed and dark, and he crossed the wet flagstones with his right fist pressed to his chest in a salute that cost him his balance.

"Duchess," he said.

No one had ever called her that. The word went through the courtyard like the bell's note through the wall.

She said, "Speak, Audemer."

"At the Roches-Noires, my lady. The nineteenth day of the month, before noon. The French had the pike and the guns where our scouts said there was pasture. My lord charged twice. The second charge went in. The guns were waiting in the carts. I saw him unhorsed. I saw the standard fall. By evening the field was theirs, and what was left of us was running for the fords." His voice had the flatness of a man reading an inventory. "I did not see him die. Three men who did are dead themselves, two on the road home. The Sire de Graçay had the body by sundown. His herald came to the ford under parley. They will give him up for the customary ransom, and the bishop's man has gone with the gold. My lady, the army is gone. The gendarmes are gone, the ordinance company is in pieces, and the French hold the south road."

Rain hissed in the torch flames. A groom was sick against the wall, neatly and quietly, as though politeness survived everything. Jeanne looked at the eleven men and the eleven horses and counted the living the way she counted everything, because counting was the door her mind shut behind itself when the cold came in. Eleven. Nineteen days. A duchy of six walled towns, three restive cities, a hundred and forty parishes, and no army between them and Paris.

"See to the men," she said to Brive. "The surgeons now, the kitchens after, and every horse in my own stables. Audemar, you will give your account once more, in full, to the chancellor, and then you will sleep if you can. No one else is to speak of this outside the council chamber until I have spoken. On your homage."

Brive looked at her. "My lady, the household will know by dawn."

"The household will know by dawn what I choose to tell it," she said, "which is that my father is dead and his daughter holds the Haut-Tertre. Marshal. The watch is doubled, the gates sealed until first mass, and any man who leaves this courtyard with a rumor loses his tongue and his place, in that order."

It was the first sovereign sentence of her life, and it sounded passably like someone else's, which was all that could be asked. She went back up the stairs at the same pace, past the carved dead, and did not feel her feet on them.

* * *

The corpse came three hours before dawn, in a cart drawn by two oxen and escorted by a French herald under a safe-conduct sealed at the ford. By then the rain had stopped, and the sky over the Ortelle was the color of pewter. Jeanne received the cart in the upper courtyard, with Foulques on one side of her and Dame Oriabel on the other, and the household drawn up in two lines, everyone in the darkest wool they owned, which in a court of mourning was not dark enough.

The herald was a Gascon with a careful face. He delivered himself of a speech about the honorable treatment of the fallen, which he had clearly made before, and a sealed letter from the Sire de Graçay, which he gave to Foulques and not to her, a small insolence she noted in the ledger she kept behind her eyes. Then the cart's canvas was drawn back.

They had washed him. That was the strangeness. The French had washed the body and closed the eyes and dressed it in a clean white shirt, perhaps because a ransomed corpse fetched its price better when it did not smell, perhaps because some clerk remembered chivalry. The face was not much damaged. A dead man's face, grey and waxen, the strong nose thinner than she remembered, the mouth set in the line it wore over bad accounts. There was a wound at the throat, dark and puckered, and the hands were broken in several places, which the shirt and the winding sheet concealed as far as the wrists.

Jeanne had not touched her father in affection since she was small enough to be lifted onto his saddle. She had touched his ring in homage twice a year, at New Year and at Michaelmas. She touched his hand now. It was as cold and firm as a stone hand in a church, and she kept hers over it for the length of a Pater Noster, because the household was watching and the herald was watching and the dead man had taught her that everything done before witnesses was government.

"Take him to the chapel," she said. "The embalmers before sunrise. He will lie in state three days, and the requiem will be sung by the bishop himself, and I will pay for every Mass out of the privy purse rather than wait on the treasury. Herald, you will tell the Sire de Graçay that the Duchess of Vauréal acknowledges the return of her father's body and the manner of it. You may tell him also that I keep accounts of every kindness, and every other thing."

The Gascon bowed, less carelessly than he had arrived. The cart creaked toward the chapel door, where the canons were already mustering in their singing clothes, and the first plainchant rose into the raw morning, old and indifferent and steady, the one sound in the world that did not need to be told what to do.

In the stairwell Oriabel's hand found hers and squeezed it hard, and for one breath the whole machinery of Jeanne's composure groaned on its hinges. Then she said, "The council chamber in one hour. Treasurer, chancellor, marshal, master of the ordnance. And bring the southern dispatches, all of them, including the ones my father thought too dull to answer."

* * *

The council chamber of the Haut-Tertre was round, which her great-grandfather had considered modern, and walled with maps and the horn-bound ledgers of three generations. A fire of oak logs had been lit against the damp and was losing. At the long table, under the painted arms of Vauréal, five people sat with the faces of people at a deathbed who have been asked to divide the sheets.

Foulques arranged his papers with the slow precision of a man for whom order was a kind of prayer. Brive sat in steel and silence, having not undressed. The treasurer, a dry little cleric named Maître Colard, had brought four ledgers and a boy to carry them. The master of ordnance, a one-eyed Fleming called Van Brock, smelled of the forge he had been dragged from. Jeanne took her father's chair, the high one with the worn arms, and the room adjusted itself around her like water around a stone.

"First the money," she said, "because every other word we say today is paid for."

Colard did not need to be told twice. He opened the first ledger, and the story came out in his thin, precise voice, the story no battlefield dispatch ever carried. The Roches-Noires campaign had eaten the year's war taxes in advance. The ransom for the duke's body, paid in gold at the ford, had emptied the privy chest. The ordinance companies that survived were owed two months' pay and would dissolve within the week without it. The garrisons at Mont d'Or and Pont-d'Ortelle were owed longer. Customs receipts at Malemort were down because the river trade had stopped the moment the south road did.

"The ready money in the vaults," Colard said, turning a page as though it might bite him, "is eleven thousand écus, of which three are earmarked for the Dolfin interest due at Easter. Against that, my lady, the monthly cost of putting even a small field army back on its feet is—"

"Twelve thousand," she said. "I have read my father's margins. Go on."

He went on, miserably. The feudal levies could be called but not held past forty days, and the vassals would come, if they came, to measure the new duchess rather than to fight. The Swiss pike company contracted for the spring had not been paid its first installment and was already hiring itself elsewhere. The crown's declaration, the sealed letter Graçay's herald had delivered, invoked an old failure of homage over the three southern fiefs and declared them forfeit to France. It was legally clever, historically dubious, and militarily sincere, which was the only kind of sincerity that mattered.

"Now the towns," Jeanne said.

Foulques took up the thread. The dispatches from the north, left to ripen while the south burned, were a garden of threats. At Sainterre the guilds had held an armed assembly in the cloth hall and drawn up articles: a charter of aldermen, a guild court, fixed tolls, the right to maintain their own militia. The articles came wrapped in professions of grief for the dead duke and loyalty to his daughter, the wrapping so thin it was an insult. At Malemort the port boss Van Maerlant had closed the customs house "pending the security of the river," which meant pending whoever won. At Grisay the forges were still working, because forges always worked, but the preacher Anselm had told a crowd of three thousand that princes were a scourge permitted for the sins of the rich, which was being interpreted in several useful ways.

"And my vassals?" Jeanne said. "The ones with swords."

Brive spoke for the first time. "Hautperthuis has written. Warmly. He is on his way to the Haut-Tertre even now, with a suitable train, to put himself at your service." The old marshal's mouth said the word _suitable_ the way a man says _vermin_. "Erquelines has written to no one, which means he has written to the Emperor. The rest are waiting to see whether the French stop at the fords."

"Will they stop?"

The question went around the table and came back to Van Brock, who shrugged his massive shoulders. "Graçay is no plunderer. He will consolidate the south, repair the forts, and wait for the spring roads to dry. He has the Regent's caution and the Regent's books. He will be at Mont d'Or by May with guns if we give him May. If we had an army, my lady, I would say the line of the Ortelle holds. We have eleven men."

Nobody laughed. The fire popped.

Jeanne looked at the maps on the wall, the painted roads and rivers and little ink castles, the whole inheritance her father had spent twenty years soldering together out of marriages and purchases and the careful bankruptcy of his neighbors. She thought of the eleven riders in the wet courtyard. She thought of the waxen hand. She thought of the things that happen to rich duchies with dead dukes and girl heirs, the things her father's own ledgers recorded without sentiment: wards sold, widows cloistered, towns invited to pay for their own conquest.

Then she opened the fresh ledger she had brought with her, the one with the blank vellum pages and the new leather cover, and she wrote the date at the top of the first page in her best hand, the hand her father's clerics had beaten into her: the nineteenth of March, in the year of Our Lord fourteen hundred and eighty-four, the first day of my reign.

"Write these orders," she said. "The feudal host is summoned to Aubecourt by the twentieth of April, with the quotas as attached, and every lord's response recorded in writing and kept. A fast courier to the Dolfin bank at Sainterre: I will receive the banker himself, not a factor, before the week is out, and he is to bring terms, not condolences. A letter to the guild dean Coppens, in my own hand, granting him safe conduct to the Haut-Tertre to lay his articles before me. He is to be answered politely. Politeness costs nothing and buys time, and time is the only soldier we can currently afford."

She turned the page.

"The marshal will make the defense of the northern roads his study and give me a paper by Friday. The master of ordnance will inventory every gun, barrel, and powder cask in the duchy, including the town walls, and I will know why the Sainterre wall pieces are better than my own. Maître Foulques will prepare the funeral and the homage ceremonies as one occasion, so that every man who comes to weep stays to swear. And the watch will admit no more heralds, French or imperial, before I have read their letters and decided which of them I wish to be seen receiving."

The five men looked at her. Colard's pen hovered. It was Brive who said the thing the room was holding its breath around.

"My lady. All of this holds for a season, perhaps. A duchy without an army is a corpse that has not yet fallen down. There is one garrison within reach that has not been beaten, one lord with a full company and a name that the French respect. The council before Roches-Noires had already opened the question. Your father set it aside. It cannot be set aside now."

She knew what was coming. She had known since the gate bell. She had known, in some room of herself that never slept, for seven years.

"The Duke of Lorraine," Foulques said, very gently, as though gentleness had ever been what she wanted from him. "The treaty of marriage was drafted before your father marched. A defensive league between Vauréal and Lorraine, sealed by your hand and his. His army, my lady. Our money. There is no second answer in this room, and you will find no second answer in Christendom."

The painted arms on the wall seemed to lean inward. Lorraine. The word tasted of iron and wet stone and a closed door in a corridor she had walked past a thousand times. She kept her face the way she had kept her hand on the dead hand, steady and public, and wrote in the new ledger, beneath her orders, one line.

Then she said, "I will consider the Lorraine question, and I will answer it after my father is buried. Is there anything else that will not keep until tomorrow?"

There was not. The council rose, scraping, bowing, gathering its ledgers and its dread. Brive paused at the door and gave her a look she could not read, something between pity and the beginning of respect. Foulques left last, as chancellors do, and his eyes, too, rested on her a moment too long, the eyes of a man keeping a door shut somewhere.

* * *

She dismissed Lisette at midnight and sat alone in her father's chamber, where the servants had not yet found the courage to change anything. His reading spectacles lay on a folded map of the southern fiefs. A cup of wine stood at his elbow, gone sour, and the smell of him still lingered in the hangings: parchment, woodsmoke, the dried herbs he strewed against fleas. She had intended to weep, now that there were no witnesses, and was dismayed to find the tears would not come. Instead she opened the casket at the table's foot and found, beneath the estate rolls, the letters she had written and never sent, tied with a ribbon whose color had gone out of fashion years ago.

She did not untie them. She had not untied them in seven years, and she was not the girl who had tied them. The newest one, written at fifteen in a hand that still believed in the goodness of explanations, she could have recited from memory, every foolish line. She thought of the word _Duchess_, spoken in the wet courtyard by a man with one hand. She thought of the line she had written in the new ledger, the line beneath the orders, which no one else had seen.

It said: _What cannot be refused must be made to serve me._

She retied the casket, locked it, and put the key on its chain around her neck, where it lay cold between her breasts beside her mother's crucifix. Outside, the cathedral bell began at last, and then the bells of the lower town answered it, tower after tower, rolling south across the river toward the army that no longer existed and the border that no longer had an army on it. She blew out the taper and sat in the dark for a while, listening to her inheritance ring.

Then she lit another, went back to the table, and began, in the margin of her father's map, to plan a funeral and a marriage and a war, in the order in which they would be made to pay for one another.

* * *

They buried Hugues de Vauréal on the twenty-second of March, in the vault beneath the cathedral choir, in the company of his father and his father's father and the wife he had not been able to save. The bishop sang the requiem in a voice like a damp viol, and the church was so full that the aldermen of the lower town stood against the pillars with the scullions, and the smell of wet wool rose to the vaults and fought the incense.

Jeanne knelt closest to the bier, in black from throat to ankle, a veil of the finest and most public kind. Behind her the great houses arranged themselves in order of ambition, which in a reign twelve days old had not yet hardened into order of rank. She could feel them sorting themselves the way she felt draughts, without turning: Hautperthuis had arrived the previous evening with forty horsemen and a face of borrowed grief, and knelt now where she could see his gloved hands folded on the stone. Erquelines had sent a nephew and an excuse. The lesser lords filled the nave like a jury. In the side chapel, plain among the jeweled mourners, stood the envoys who had ridden hard through the rain: the imperial, the French, the Venetian, three kinds of vulture in three kinds of velvet, come to measure the corpse's daughter.

The chants washed over her. _Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine._ She had known the words since she was seven and had sung them, badly, for a favorite falcon. She watched the candles bend in the breath of the church and thought, with a clerk's shame, about candle wax, which was up a farthing the pound since the river trade failed, and about the twelve altar candles burning here, and the cathedral's right to bill the privy purse for them. She thought about her father under his pall of red and gold, smaller than the duke inside it, and about the broken hands.

At the moment of the elevation, when every knee went down and every head bowed, she looked up. It was a small, private sacrilege, the kind a child commits in the certainty that God has larger concerns. The painted window above the choir showed the old dukes of Vauréal processing toward Christ with their charter rolls in their hands, all of them clean and certain and unacquainted with gunfire. Her father was not up there yet. His glass had not been commissioned. There was no one to commission it now but her.

Outside, after the interment, the crowd pressed close in the square, the lower town come out to see what a twenty-two-year-old duchess looked like. She had expected silence or, worse, sympathy. What she got was the flat, measuring stare of people deciding whether to pay their taxes. A fishwife in the front rank, arms folded under her apron, looked Jeanne over as she might look over a stall, and found the goods uncertain. Jeanne met her eyes and did not look away, and after a moment the woman's face did something complicated, and she bobbed her knees, the smallest possible curtsy, the kind that could be denied later.

Jeanne mounted the horse they had brought, a quiet grey mare rather than the great war destrier her father would have used, and spoke the words Foulques had written and she had rewritten three times. She did not shout. She had been taught that a voice lowered into silence carries farther than a voice raised over noise, and the square went quiet to hear her.

"My father is with his fathers. His lands are not orphaned, his towns are not widowed, and his people are not forfeit. I will hold what he held, and I will keep his peace, and I will keep his justice. Go home, and say the duchess said so."

It was short. It was perhaps inadequate to the moment. It was also, as Brive admitted to her afterwards over bread and hot spiced wine in the guardhouse, the first thing said in the duchy since the Roches-Noires that did not smell of panic.

* * *

The private grief came at last in the cathedral vault, when the public day was done and the canons had banked their candles and only Oriabel waited at the stair foot. Jeanne had asked to be left, and against every rule of her household she had been obeyed.

The vault smelled of old stone and old death, the family dead stacked in their lead and marble, her mother's sarcophagus small against her father's new one. She knelt on the cold step between them, and for a while nothing happened, and then everything happened at once, in the ugly, choking, unladylike way real grief has, the kind no court can use. She wept for the father who had taught her Latin by making her translate treatises on siegeworks, who had never once praised her to her face, who had ridden away with an army and come home in a cart. She wept for the girl who had once believed that being loved and being ruled were different things. She wept until there was nothing left in her but the stone and the dark and the sound of her own breathing.

Then she blew her nose on a linen handkerchief of the finest kind, folded it, put it away, and said aloud to the two sarcophagi, "I have no more time for this."

It was not true. Grief was not a creditor who went away unpaid. But it could be made to wait, like the Dolfin bank, like the French, like every other claimant at the gate. She climbed the stairs into the last grey light of the evening, where Oriabel waited without questions and Lisette waited with a cloak, and the three women crossed the courtyard through a fine rain that was becoming, by some small mercy, snow.

At the door of the council chamber she stopped. Through the oak she could hear the low murmur of her council assembling early, Brive's soldier's rumble, Foulques's dry whisper, the rustle of Colard's ledgers. Somewhere beyond the walls a horn sounded from the river road, long and official, the horn of a great lord arriving with a train.

Hautperthuis, probably. Or the imperial envoy's second audience. Or the answer from Sainterre, or the next demand from Paris, or the next bill. It did not matter which. They were all the same visitor, wearing different cloaks, and they would all be shown in to the same round room, and they would all find the same woman in the high chair.

Jeanne settled her veil, put her hand to the latch, and went in to receive them.
