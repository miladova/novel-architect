# Chapter Four — Nancy's Bargain

The grain stores of Nancy were supposed to hold nine hundred quarters of wheat. The chief storeman, a man named Petitjean who had survived three dukes and two sacks, swore on his mother's grave that the figure was accurate, and Thiébaut, who had learned to count grain by counting it, drove his own arm into the bin to the elbow and came up with a handful of chaff and blackened kernels at the level where the wheat ought to have been.

"Your mother," he said, "was fortunate in her son's opinion of her. The bin is false-bottomed. Fetch the steward, the sergeant of the granary guard, and the man who built this, and be quick about it."

Petitjean went the color of old tallow. The duke of Lorraine stood in the dust of his own storehouse, in a plain leather jerkin with his steward's tally board in one hand, and did not raise his voice. He had learned very young that a man who never raises his voice is always overheard. By the time the three culprits arrived, he had probed four more bins and found two honest ones, and he had the numbers written in his head: six weeks' bread for the garrison, not four months. Six weeks, with the French raiding parties already over the southern march and the spring campaign unborn.

He hanged the builder, because the false bottom was construction and therefore the man's own craft; he broke the steward before the garrison and stripped him of everything but his life; he put Petitjean, who had sworn the false oath, into the builder's old place on the theory that a caught thief makes the best watchman, which his father had taught him and which had never yet failed. Then he paid the garrison's arrears out of his own plate, which the goldsmiths weighed in the courtyard under the eyes of three hundred men, and rode back up to the palace of the dukes of Lorraine as the evening bell went, with two silver dishes the poorer and a garrison that would, for another month, hold the walls.

In the courtyard his half-brother Jean was waiting with a hawk on his fist and the particular grin that meant bad news being enjoyed.

"You've been hanging men again," said the Bastard of Lorraine. "The town says you're a just prince. It says so very loudly, in the taverns, where justice is admired in proportion to its distance."

"The town will admire me more when it isn't eating its boots. The bins were false, Jean. Six weeks."

The grin went. "Six weeks? Then we had better hope your bride's gold travels faster than her letters. The Vauréal courier came in an hour ago. The council is assembled, Viray has sent twice for you, and there is a letter for your own hand, delivered under a seal I did not recognize, which the courier says was to be given to no one else."

Thiébaut took the letter. He knew the seal before he turned it. The Vauréal arms, the red and silver, and below them, in place of the chancellor's office, a single hand-drawn letter: J. He had not seen that hand since he was twenty years old, and his body recognized it before his mind did, the old cold march of the heart, the old silence under the ribs.

"Feed the hawk," he said, "and tell the council I'll be along when I've washed the granary off."

* * *

He read the letter alone, in the high chamber that served him for study, armory, and bedchamber combined, because Lorraine could not afford a duke with separate rooms for his misfortunes. The window looked west over the roofs of Nancy toward the road that led, three days' hard riding and a world away, to Aubecourt.

It was a short letter. It was also, he thought, the most carefully unkind document he had ever held, and he had held peace treaties. It named him the Duke of Lorraine throughout, never his name. It set out her terms for the gate, the noon, the public reception. It told him the girl he had known was not at home. It was signed, at the bottom, in that same hand, with the single word _Jeanne_.

He sat for a long time with the page on his knee. A log shifted in the hearth. The bad shoulder ached, as it did whenever the wind came west off the Vosges, a deep old weather in the bone, and he pressed it with his right hand the way one presses a tooth.

Seven years. He had made himself into a different instrument in seven years, the way a smith makes a plowshare out of whatever iron the scrap pile offers: campaigns in the snow, debts paid with plate, garrisons held by willpower, the long grinding defense of a duchy that every map in Paris had already divided among French lords. He had taught himself to sleep on the ground, to read a battlefield by the birds, to judge men by their excuses, and to want nothing that could be taken from him. There were nights when he believed the lesson had taken. Then some small thing, a storm, a certain grey light, a woman's handwriting on a dispatch, would show him the forge was not yet cold.

He had not been back to Aubecourt. He had not permitted himself even the road. Once, two years before, a truce conference had been called at Pont-d'Ortelle, a half-day's ride from the Haut-Tertre, and he had sat through three days of clerks' quarrels and ridden home without once turning north, and Jean, who noticed everything, had said nothing, which was the Bastard's particular form of mercy.

Now the road was the only road. He folded the letter into his jerkin, over the heart, where he kept the things he did not intend to lose, and went down to the council.

* * *

They were twelve around the table, the great lords of Lorraine and the clerks who governed them, under a painted ceiling that flaked in the damp because there was no money to repair it. The Abbé de Viray sat at the duke's right hand, fifty years old and priestly in everything but shrewdness, with the face of a fasting saint and the mind of a Lombard. Jean lounged at his left, the hawk mercifully absent. The captains stood along the wall, smelling of horses.

The news from Vauréal had arrived in pieces: Roches-Noires, the dead duke, the destroyed army, the French at the fords, the girl in the high chair. Viray had assembled every dispatch and now summarized them in a voice made for sermons, so that even the dullest lord in the room could follow the road they were on.

"France intends the dismemberment," the abbé concluded. "The southern fiefs first, by this forfeit nonsense. Then the cities, which are already in revolt against their own duchess. Then, when Vauréal is a collection of obedient French towns, the turn of Lorraine, which has no money, no allies, and a frontier drawn by a madman. There is one wall between us and that future. It is not our army, excellent though the army is. It is a marriage."

A murmur. Old Comte de Salm, who had been against every innovation since the invention of gunpowder, cleared his throat. "The Vauréal girl. We know the history. She will not have him. The courtship of their childhood ended in the most public refusal this side of Rome. Why should she sign now?"

"Because she has no more choice than we have." Thiébaut spoke from the head of the table, and the room turned. "Her council has done its arithmetic. We have what she lacks: a field force that has not been broken. She has what we lack: everything else. This is not a courtship, Salm. It is a siege alliance, and the bride is a fortress."

"Then the terms," Viray said, and spread the draft treaty.

He had fought the terms, Thiébaut knew; the abbé fought every concession on principle, because concessions not fought for were concessions unvalued. But the core of the document was the duke's own instruction, and it stood: separate sovereignty, separate seals, no Lorrainer office in Vauréal without her patent, the reversion clause that gave his house no grip on her inheritance if the marriage was fruitless. The lords who wanted him to demand the regency, the constableship, the right to garrison her towns, had all been overruled one by one.

"You give away every lever," Salm complained. "A husband's rights, a man's rights—"

"A man's rights do not hold Sainterre." Thiébaut's voice was quiet, and the old man subsided. "I have seen her father's court from the inside. Any lever I take, she will spend her whole reign working to pry loose, and her vassals will help her, and the French will walk through the crack. A light hand is not weakness. It is the only hand that can be kept there."

Jean grinned at him across the table. "Also, my brother is a romantic. He intends to be invited back."

"Your brother intends to be paid," Thiébaut said. "Speak to the gold convoy. It leaves Malemort the moment she receives our seal. Six months' arrears, Jean, in actual coin. The men will weep. Some of them may even salute."

The council laughed, even Salm. The vote, when it came, was unanimous, because unanimity is easy for men standing on a foundering ship. Viray sealed the treaty that night with the Lorraine arms, and the duplicate rode north at dawn with a fast escort, while Thiébaut sat late over maps of the Vauréal roads and calculated what he could spare to ride with him and what he dared not strip from his own walls.

* * *

Jean found him there after midnight, with two cups and a flask of the rough red wine the country produced in quantity and distinction avoided.

"Fifty lances," the Bastard said, sitting down uninvited. "That's the rumor. You're taking fifty lances into a country that's half in revolt with French columns on its roads. Have you considered a hundred?"

"A hundred strips Sarrebourg. Fifty says I come as a bridegroom. A hundred says I come as an occupying power, and every wall between here and Aubecourt shuts. I'll take fifty, and I'll take you, and I'll take the best scouts in Lorraine, and we'll ride fast and light and trust the countryside to be more afraid of the French than of us." He drank. "Also I want a word with you before I go. If I don't come back—"

"Spare me."

"If I don't come back, Lorraine passes to my uncle's line, and you know it. Viray has the succession papers. What I want is your word you'll hold the Vosges passes through the summer regardless. Don't chase my body with the army. Don't give battle in grief. Hold the roads, pay the garrisons with the Vauréal gold, and let the mountains do the work."

Jean was staring at him. "You actually expect trouble on the road."

"I expect the French have scouts too, and that a dead duke of Lorraine on his way to his wedding saves Paris a great deal of money. I expect bandits in the Harchies, because disorder always breeds them. I expect to be attacked. What I don't expect is to fail to arrive, because that would be rude, and she has already accused me in writing of being unable to receive the girl she used to be." He produced the letter, briefly, and tapped it. "I mean to answer that in person."

The Bastard looked at him for a long moment. The easy grin was gone. "You've never told me what happened there. At Aubecourt. The whole truth. I've stopped asking, mostly, because a man's silences are his own. But you're riding back into that house, and I'll say this once: whatever it was, you were twenty, and twenty-year-old men are not permitted to be wise, and most of them are not required to be brave in front of an entire court. You did what you did. Don't let it eat the only useful alliance we've had in ten years."

Thiébaut turned the wine cup in his hands. The firelight moved on the painted walls. He thought of the letter in his jerkin, and of another letter, seven years old, that he had never answered because answering had been made the price of things he could not pay with words. He thought of a dawn in a dusty solar and the sound of a key turning, and of the only sentence he had been allowed to carry away from it: _This is my doing. She is innocent of everything but kindness._

"Jean," he said. "If I told you the whole truth, you would do the one thing I cannot afford. You would forgive me. And then I should have to explain why forgiveness is not among the things on offer." He set down the cup. "Hold the passes. That's all I'll take from you tonight."

Jean said nothing more. They finished the wine in the old silence of men who had shared tents since boyhood, and at first light the duke of Lorraine heard Mass, kissed the abbey's relic, and rode out of Nancy under the alérion standard with fifty lances behind him and his brother at his side, west and north, toward the country that held his army's pay, his duchy's survival, and the one person in Christendom to whom he could not explain himself.

The road climbed into the March hills. Behind him the palace grew small, and the spires of Nancy, and the lead roofs of the cathedral; ahead, the long green country rolled away toward the river he had not crossed in seven years. He did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for men whose decisions were behind them.

* * *

They made the first night at a monastic grange in the hills, where the prior, a man with the eye of a mule trader, tried to charge the duke of Lorraine for the use of his own hayloft. Thiébaut paid without argument, in silver, and made a note of the grange's name, because wars were won and lost on such lists and he had a long memory for priors.

The fifty lances made camp with the practiced quiet of men who had done it in worse places. No fires beyond what cooking required, pickets on the high ground, the horses watered and unsaddled in strict rotation, the standard furled. Thiébaut walked the lines himself, as he always did, tasting the ration bread, testing a saddle girth, speaking to each man by name. It was not kindness, though it passed for it; it was the simplest discipline there was, the knowledge that an army which knows its leader's face is an army that will stand one volley longer than an army that does not.

Jean found him at the picket line, checking a hoof for a thrown shoe.

"Scouts in. The Harchies is busy. Two villages burned between here and the border, no one's colors, which means free companions wearing whichever colors suit the hour. The French pay them to chew the roads; our own merchants pay them to be left alone. They'll have heard a rich party is moving. Fifty lances is a tempting number."

"Not tempting enough for real soldiers. Tempting enough for bandits with a grievance." Thiébaut straightened. "We'll alter the march. Send four good men ahead in merchants' clothes, slow, with two pack mules and a visible purse. Let them be seen. The bandits will take the bait and show their position. We'll be a quarter-league behind in the woods, and we'll come at them from both sides of the road."

"That's a cold way to treat four good men."

"That's why I chose the four meanest men in Lorraine. Ask for volunteers; you'll have ten before you finish the sentence. Tell them the purse is theirs if it comes to a fight."

Jean laughed and went off to arrange it, and Thiébaut stood a while longer in the horse-smell and the dark, looking north. The grange stood on high ground, and beyond the black mass of the nearer woods he could see the faint glow on the sky that was the watch-fires of the border marches, his country ending and hers beginning.

The shoulder woke him whenever he slept on hard ground. He had learned to lie on his right side and not move, and to think of other things, and tonight the other things were a solar with dust in the air and a girl's face lit by lightning. He allowed himself, once, in the dark of the hayloft with fifty men breathing around him, the memory he permitted only on the march: not the great hall, not the words, but the night before it, the two of them under one cloak, the solemn fifteen-year-old voice promising to give up the world for a cottage. He had believed her. That was the wound that never quite closed: not the loss, but the knowledge that she had meant it, every word, and that he had been made to answer meaning with theater, and that she would go to her grave believing the theater was the man.

He had thought, in the first years, that he would find some way to tell her. A letter through a trusted hand. A meeting at a pilgrimage shrine. Some crack in the wall of silence that the old duke had built around the girl, the maid, and the clerk. But the watch had been thorough, and the threats had been precise, and Thiébaut of Lorraine had come home to a duchy on the edge of dissolution with no money and no friends, and the years had done what years do: the possible routes had closed one by one, and the silence had become a habit, and the habit had become a kind of stone.

Then Hugues was dead, and the stone was suddenly a road, and the road led here, to a hayloft, to a wedding, to a woman who had written him a letter as precise and merciless as a tax assessment and signed it with his own heart's first word.

He slept at last, badly, and was woken before dawn by the scouts' return, and by first light the column was moving again, down out of the hills, toward the forest and the border and whatever the road had in its throat.

* * *

The border was a river called the Nied, no wider than a banquet table, with a stone bridge that had been repaired so often it looked like an old man's teeth. Lorraine ended at the nearer bank. Vauréal began at the farther, and the difference was visible at once: the Lorraine side kept its watchtower manned, its hedge trimmed, its customs officer present and alert. The Vauréal side had a tower with a mossy roof and no flag, a customs house with its door hanging open, and an officer who arrived at a trot ten minutes after the column, buttoning his tunic.

He was an elderly sergeant with a Vauréal badge gone green at the edges, and he took one look at the alérion standard and another at fifty quiet men in steel and chose his words with the care of a man negotiating with weather.

"My lord duke. We were told to expect a great train, baggage wagons, the whole court. The duchess's people are preparing at Aubecourt—"

"The duchess's people will be surprised, which is good for them and bad for anyone waiting on the road. Sergeant, your tower has no watch. Your customs house has no books. Who holds this bridge at night?"

The man swallowed. "We do, my lord. Four men. Though since the Roches-Noires, the levy's been drawn down for the southern forts, and four is—"

"Two, and one of them old. I can count the chickens from here." Thiébaut sat his horse and looked at the empty tower, and the sergeant watched him look, and the years of the man's life passed visibly across his face. "I am not your officer, and I will not be until your duchess makes me so, if she does. But write this down tonight in whatever book you have left: a bridge with no watch is a road with an invitation. Light the tower beacon at dusk from now on, every dusk, and send a rider to Aubecourt with the word that the Duke of Lorraine crossed the Nied with fifty lances on the third of April and found the gate of the marches asleep. You'll be thanked or hanged, and either way the watch improves."

He left the sergeant stammering and crossed the bridge into Vauréal, and felt the country change under his horse's hooves the way a man feels a floor change in a strange house. It was richer country, that was the first thing: wider fields, bigger barns, stone villages with church spires, the commerce of a duchy that had not spent two generations being fought over. And it was frightened country, the second thing: farmsteads with the shutters nailed, a mill standing idle with its gate padlocked, families on the road with carts heaped high, going north, away from the French and the fords and the smoke on the southern horizon.

At midday they passed a burned hamlet. It was still warm; the raiders had been there within the day. Three cottages, a smithy, a byre, the usual wreckage of a quick visit, and in the churchyard two fresh mounds with a wooden cross apiece, dug by whoever had survived. Thiébaut dismounted and walked the ground the way his trade taught him: the hoofprints, the cart ruts, the ash pattern, the place where the raiders had watered their horses. Jean joined him, grim.

"Twelve, fifteen horses. No pikes, no order. Free companions, like the scouts said. They came from the southeast and went northwest, back toward the Harchies. They have oxen with them, look, the wide wheels. They're carrying everything that wasn't nailed down."

"And two graves. Who?"

A woman's voice answered from the church door: "My man and the priest's boy. They stood at the bridge with axes. The rest of us ran for the woods. We came back when they'd gone."

She was thirty perhaps, hard and thin, with a child clamped to her hip and ash on her face and the particular calm of people who have used up their fear. Behind her, other villagers were drifting out of the trees, the way villagers do after the soldiers leave, to see what remains.

Thiébaut took off his glove. "Woman, the men who did this. How far ahead?"

"Half a day, lord, if you're asking me to guess. They had our cattle. Cattle are slow." She looked at the standard, and then at him, with a peasant's unblinking assessment. "You're the duchess's Frenchman, then. The one she's marrying."

"I am the duke of Lorraine. Whether I am the duchess's anything remains under discussion. Can your village feed fifty men for an hour? Bread, cheese, water; I pay, in silver, on the nail."

It was the silver that decided her, as silver decides most things in burned villages. An hour later the column ate in the churchyard, under the wooden crosses, and the villagers watched the strange soldiers who paid for bread and cut their own firewood, and the woman with the child sat on the church step and told Thiébaut the news of the marches: the raiding bands, the French patrols, the lords who had begun looking the other way, the name everyone whispered, though she would not say whose tenants they had once been. Hautperthuis. The name hung in the air unsaid, the way smoke hangs in a thatch.

He gave her a purse for the two graves and a note under his hand to the nearest Vauréal garrison, and rode on with the ash of the hamlet in his nostrils.

* * *

That evening, in a beech wood above the road that entered the Harchies, the scouts came back with the confirmation. The bandits were camped in a ruined charcoal burner's clearing two leagues ahead, perhaps forty strong, drunk on the hamlet's cider, with the stolen oxen and a dozen other miserable prizes. The four decoy merchants had been seen on the road; the bandits meant to take them at dawn where the forest narrowed.

"Forty," Jean said, weighing it. "Fifty. I've seen worse odds at dice."

"We won't use the odds. We'll use the ground." Thiébaut had the map on his knees, a sketch the scouts had made on bark. "They expect four merchants. They'll come down off this slope onto the road at the narrows. We put the lances in the trees on both sides, dismounted, horses held back. The merchants ride through at dawn, slow, fat, careless. When the bandits commit, we close the road behind them and take them from the flanks. No pursuit into the deep wood; the Harchies is their house and ours only up to its door. Any man who throws down a weapon is bound, not killed; I want tongues more than corpses."

"Tongues for what?"

"For whoever's paying them. A free company doesn't burn Vauréal villages within a week of a French invasion for its health. Somebody with a seal has hired the road to be unsafe. I want the name on the seal."

The captains nodded and moved off to their posts. In the last light, Thiébaut walked the picket line himself, checking the lances, the girths, the quiet of the horses. He stopped at the edge of the wood where the road ran north, and between the black trunks, far off across the rolling dark of the country, he saw it: a point of light, then another, then a scattered constellation, the lamps and watch-fires of a great town in a river valley.

Aubecourt. The Haut-Tertre would be up there on its bluff above the lamps, and someone in it would be sitting up late over ledgers, because she always had, even as a child, even when the candles were rationed; the girl who had learned Latin from siege manuals and skated badly and promised away the world in a storm. Two more days, if the dawn went well. One fight, fifty bandits, and then the gate at noon and the letter's conditions and a marriage made of paper and necessity and a seven-year-old silence he still did not know how to break.

Jean came up beside him and followed his eyes. "Lights," the Bastard said. "Civilization. Wine. Beds. A man could get himself killed tomorrow and never know what the duchess keeps in her cellars."

"Then don't get killed." Thiébaut turned back into the wood, where his men were bedding down among the beeches. "And Jean. At dawn, when it starts, keep close. I have a wedding to arrive at, and I mean to arrive on time."
