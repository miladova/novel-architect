# Chapter Seven — The Wedding at Haut-Tertre

She was woken before dawn by the sound of the lower town hammering itself festive, and for a moment, in the dark, she could not remember why a city would be hammering at four in the morning, and then she could, and lay still with her hands flat on the sheet while the knowledge came over her like cold water: today.

Lisette came with the washing water and the women came behind her, four of them, Oriabel at their head with the dress over her arms like a priest carrying a vestment. The steel-grey gown had been remade in three nights of sleepless labor, the silver thread worked into a pattern of walls and rivers along the hem, and over it went the ducal mantle of red velvet lined with miniver, heavy as a responsibility, which her mother had worn and her grandmother before her. They dressed her as they would have armed a knight: layers, lacings, the ceremonial girdle low on the hips, the great ruby brooch at the throat, the veil held by a band of gold instead of a maiden's circlet. No white. No flowers in the hair. A sovereign's wedding.

"You are white enough without the cloth," Oriabel said, studying her. "Eat something. There will be nothing until the feast, and a fainting bride is a story the vultures will carry to Paris."

Jeanne forced down bread and hot milk in the little antechamber while the palace woke around her. Through the window she could see the processional way being strewn with fresh rushes, the guild banners going up between the houses, the crowd already thick in the square, a holiday crowd with the particular edge of a crowd that has been told it is witnessing history and intends to judge it. Children perched on the fountain. Sellers of pies and commemorative badges did a trade. She watched a man sell a hundred tin badges stamped with the two arms joined, and made a note to find out who had licensed him.

"Letters during the night," Lisette said, producing them from her apron like a conjuror. "Foulques said you would ask."

There were three. From Coppens, smuggled out of Sainterre in a bale of undyed cloth: the de Key faction now held three gates, and the moderates were being made to swear a weavers' oath on pain of losing their workshops. From the commandant at Mont d'Or: the southern fort Saint-Vaast was being strengthened by French engineers, and Graçay's guns had been seen on the kingdom road. And a short line from the forty-seven-man post whose commandant she had promised a ballad: _Reinforcements received. We hold. The duchess's name is said at every watch._

She read them all and put them all away, and when she looked up the women were watching her with the particular gentleness people reserve for brides, and she understood that they had no idea she had just spent her wedding morning on three separate emergencies, and that this was the condition of her life from now on: to be always, in every room, the only person who knew the whole bill.

"One more thing," Lisette said, and brought out a small package. It was a handkerchief of the finest linen, edged with old lace, and Jeanne recognized it at once: her mother's, kept these thirteen years in the same casket as the unsent letters. "Oriabel thought. For the church. Something of hers."

Jeanne held it. Her mother had married at seventeen, a girl with a duchy in her dowry, and had died at thirty on a blood-soaked bed trying to give the duchy a spare heir. The handkerchief smelled faintly of dried lavender, kept with herbs all its long widowhood in the dark. Jeanne folded it into her sleeve and said, "Thank you. Both of you. Now find me the marshal. I want the guard rotation one more time before I put on the veil."

* * *

The procession left the Haut-Tertre at nine, between double ranks of household troops in the red and silver, and the crowd's noise hit her like weather as she came through the gate: bells, cheers, the deeper roar of thousands of people all exhaling at once. She walked under a canopy borne by four aldermen of Aubecourt, a canopy she had insisted on sharing with no one; the bridegroom walked fifty paces behind with his Lorraine lords, which was the order she had set and the heralds had argued about for two days. A duchess in her own right entered her own cathedral first, or did not enter at all.

The cathedral of Saint-Cyr was dressed as though for Easter, every chapel alight, every relic out, the nave hung with the joined arms in red and blue and gold. The bishop waited at the choir step in full pontificals, a fat, clever man who had sung her father's requiem three weeks ago and now stood ready to marry his daughter, and gave no sign that he found the rapidity of the transition remarkable. Bishops are trained early in the useful art of not being struck by history.

Thiébaut met her at the step. In black velvet with the Lorraine chain of office, he looked less like a bridegroom than like a man attending a council of war, which she suspected was the highest compliment available to him. He gave her the bow owed to a sovereign, and his eyes, passing over her in one quick clerk's inventory, missed nothing: the veil, the brooch, the girdle, the hand she did not offer until the rubric required it. His own hand, when the moment came, was warm and hard and steady, a swordsman's hand with calluses along the fingers, and it closed on hers without urgency, the way one closes a hand on a helm before a charge.

The Mass was long, and she had been trained since childhood to endure long services with a face of attentive piety, and she endured this one as a performance inside a performance, her lips moving in the Latin while her mind went where it always went, to the accounts. The vows: to have and to hold, for richer for poorer, in sickness and in health. She made them in a clear voice, the voice of the council chamber, and heard his voice beside her, lower, answering the same ancient words in the same ancient language, two people promising things no contract could enforce. The ring went on, gold and plain, the bishop's hand over theirs, the smoke of the incense rising between their faces.

Then the kiss, the brief liturgical kiss of peace at the chancel rail, his mouth at the corner of hers in front of four hundred witnesses, the first time he had ever touched her with his lips, and the crowd in the nave broke into applause and cries of _Noël_ and _Vauréal_, and she discovered that her heart was beating so hard she was afraid the ruby brooch would move with it.

"Madame la duchesse," he said, so low only she heard it, as they turned together toward the nave.

"My lord husband," she said, equally low, equally correct, and walked down the aisle on his arm at the sovereign's pace, while the bells of Saint-Cyr opened above them and the bells of the lower town answered and the noise rolled across the river to the camps of the army that was, after today, paid for by her gold and bound to her by this man's honor.

* * *

The feast ran to nine courses in the great hall, because twelve would have been vanity and seven an insult, and it lasted four hours, which was enough time for every faction in the duchy to present itself in the shape of a gift and a smile. Hautperthuis gave a gold cup engraved with a scene of the hunt, and a speech so perfectly pitched between grief and joy that she applauded with the rest while marking the cup's weight, which was less than its size. Erquelines's nephew gave the imperial letter he had been carrying for a week, choosing the wedding day to deliver a marriage offer from an Austrian cousin, a piece of insolence Jeanne received with thanks and set beside her plate where everyone could watch it be ignored. Dolfin's gift was a small painted map of the river trade, exquisitely done, which she valued above all the rest and which the court took for a mere pretty thing.

The Lorraine men were the novelty, and the court inspected them as the court inspects everything, for weakness. The Bastard Jean sat below the salt and charmed the wife of the Aubecourt alderman within an hour, told a story about the Harchies bandits that lost nothing in the telling, and somehow ended the evening with the alderman himself proposing a family trade concession. The Abbé de Viray discussed plainsong with the bishop and grain prices with Colard, in the same gentle voice, and Jeanne saw Foulques watching the priest with the wary respect of one professional recognizing another.

Her husband sat at her right hand under the canopy of estate and ate whatever was put before him and spoke when spoken to and watched the room. Once, when the trumpets came in with the subtleties, the sugar castles and heraldic beasts that the cooks had spent two days on, she felt his glance and turned, and he said, under the noise, "In Nancy we cannot afford sugar. My cooks believe this is a fairy tale."

"It is paid for with the Venetian's money. You are eating credit."

"The best thing on the table, then." He lifted his cup to her, a small private gesture, and drank, and she did not know whether to be irritated or amused, and chose irritation, which was safer.

By the time the light turned amber in the high windows and the hall had reached the wine's third stage, the musicians were playing the old dances and the younger lords were leaping and stamping in the French fashion, and the master of protocol appeared at her elbow with the face of a man about to undergo surgery. It was time for the bedding.

* * *

They had kept the ceremony as short as custom allowed, but custom was a jealous god and could not be denied its essentials. The procession formed: the married women of the court for her, the married lords for him, the bishop with his sprinkler, the musicians with a song so full of cheerful filth that Lisette, behind her, made a sound like a strangled cat. They went up the great stair in a wave of candlelight and laughter and old ritual, and Jeanne, walking in the middle of it with her veil on her arm, thought of the girl in the solar and what she would have made of this public delivery of the body she had once promised away in secret.

The bridal chamber was enormous and hot and full of people. The great bed stood under a red canopy, the sheets turned back, the pillows beaten, the fire lit despite the mild night because the bedding ritual demanded a fire. The women undressed her behind the screen with practiced speed: the mantle, the gown, the girdle, the linen, until she stood in her shift with her hair down and her mother's handkerchief clutched in one hand, and Oriabel poured a cup of spiced wine and made her drink half of it.

On the other side of the screen, by the sounds of masculine hilarity, the lords were doing the same for Thiébaut, with less ceremony and more advice, most of it audibly supplied by Jean. The bishop sprinkled the bed, said the prayers over the linen, blessed the union and its hoped-for fruits, and then the whole roomful of witnesses watched, in the names of law and custom and the absence of any annulment, as the two of them were put into the bed together, she in her shift and he in his shirt, and the coverlet drawn to their wavers, and the curfew cup passed, and then, at last, with bows and grins and one final volley of the filthy song, the company withdrew, and the doors closed, and the key turned, and they were alone.

The silence was enormous. Beyond the doors the court's noise receded down the stair, laughing and music-bent, and in the chamber the fire made its small domestic sounds and the candles guttered in a draught from the window hangings. They lay side by side on their backs like two effigies on a tomb, staring at the red canopy.

"Well," Thiébaut said eventually. "That was remarkably thorough."

She had expected fear, or anger, or some return of the old tight hate. What she felt instead, to her deep annoyance, was the ridiculous, bubbling pressure of laughter, and she suppressed it with effort, because laughing in bed with a man on one's wedding night was the kind of thing that led to other things. "The bishop's Latin has improved since my father's funeral. I noticed."

"I noticed the Erquelines letter beside your plate. A marriage proposal, at your own wedding feast."

"The imperial party has no sense of occasion. It is one of their more reliable qualities." She kept her eyes on the canopy. "My lord. We may as well have the conversation. The law and the court and the Church have all had their parts. There remains the question between us. I will not be claimed like a fortress, and I will not have a duty performed at me in the dark as though I were a toll to be paid. If the marriage is to be a formality for a season, I will say so plainly, and no one outside this room will ever know, and the treaty will hold either way."

He was quiet long enough that she turned her head. He was looking at her, and in the candlelight his face was not the council face at all.

"Jeanne. The treaty holds whether I touch you or not. I signed it for roads and revenues, and those do not depend on this bed. Nothing in this room is a toll. Nothing in this room is owed. But I will tell you what I would choose, if the choosing were mine alone." He paused, and she watched him choose the words, this man who chose words the way he chose ground. "I would choose that we not spend our wedding night lying to each other in the dark. I want you. I have wanted you, in one way or another, since you were eleven and ordering me about the garden wall. I am not going to take anything, because there is nothing here that is mine by right. But if you can bear the thought, I would like to make the marriage real. Slowly. With your leave at every step. And if you cannot bear it, I will sleep at the edge of this bed like a well-paid guard, and we will both be perfectly comfortable and perfectly miserable, and I will never mention it again."

She had prepared for every man except this one. She lay holding her mother's handkerchief and listened to the fire and thought, with a kind of despair, that the most dangerous thing about him had always been this: he said the true thing, simply, and left her no wall to throw herself against.

"Slowly," she said. "And if I tell you to stop, you stop."

"If you tell me to stop, I stop."

"Then don't ask again. Just— know it."

He turned onto his side. His hand came to rest on the sheet beside hers, not touching, an offered geography. "Jeanne. Look at me."

She turned her head. In the candlelight his eyes were more green than hazel, and serious, and older than the boy's she remembered, and she saw him take stock of her the way he took stock of a room: not to possess, but to know. His hand moved the last inch and closed over hers, and when she did not pull away, he lifted it to his mouth and kissed the palm, once, gravely, as though swearing on a relic.

"I have no practice at this," she said, and hated herself for the tremor in it.

"Neither do I, at this. At the rest, a man learns what he needs and forgets most of it. You will tell me what to do. All of it. That is the rule of this bed."

He kissed her then, properly, not the liturgical brush at the chancel rail but a real kiss, careful and questioning, his mouth warm and unhurried, one hand at her jaw while the other stayed where it was, laced with her fingers on the sheet. It was nothing like the kisses she remembered from the solar, which had been all lightning and astonishment; this was a man's kiss, made of knowledge and restraint, and it did something worse than astonish her. It made her answer. Her eyes closed. Somewhere beneath the covers her body, that traitor, began to unclench muscle by muscle, as though it had been keeping its own long siege and had decided, without consulting her, to discuss terms.

When his hand went to the lacing of her shift, he stopped. She nodded, once, not opening her eyes, and he undid it with a soldier's efficiency and a great deal less speed, and the linen came away from her shoulders in the warm dark. She felt the air of the chamber on her skin, and then his hand, flat and warm, at her waist, staying there while he kissed the place beneath her ear and said, low, things she would not afterward remember word for word, only the tone: that he was here, that she was not alone, that nothing was being taken.

She had imagined this moment over the years despite herself, in the way of courts where every marriage was a transaction and every bride had theories. She had imagined duty, embarrassment, perhaps pain, the endurance her mother's generation spoke of in proverbs. She had not imagined being asked. The asking undid her more thoroughly than any force could have. When at last he moved over her, his weight braced on one arm, his face above hers, she put her hands on his shoulders and felt the old bad one, the left, rigid with the effort of holding him up, and said, "Your shoulder—" and he said, "The shoulder has been waiting longer than you," and despite everything she laughed, and he kissed the laughter, and after that there was no more room for proverbs.

It hurt, the first time, a sharp, tearing, unromantic hurt, and she made a small sound against his mouth, and he stopped entirely, breathing hard, his forehead against hers, until she moved her hand to the back of his neck and said, "Don't stop, I didn't say stop," and he said, "The rule is yours, use it," and she said, "Then go on," and he did, slowly, while the candles burned and the fire cracked and the red canopy of the ducal bed became the whole world, a small hot country with its own laws.

It was not beautiful in the way the songs promised. It was specific and physical and strange, the smell of his skin and the salt of him, the ache, the awkward collision of knees, the way his hand found hers again and held it as though it were the only point of navigation in a dark sea. And then, without her expecting it, against all the proverbs, there was a turning in it, a tightening and a strange gathering, and her breath caught at something building behind the pain, and he must have felt it because he changed his rhythm, watching her face with an intensity that made her want to hide, and she could not hide, and the thing gathered and broke in a long wave that went through her whole body and left her gripping his hand with both of hers, and after it he let himself go at last, with one sound wrenched out of him that he tried and failed to swallow, his face buried in her hair.

Afterwards they lay in the wreck of the sheets, both breathing as though they had run a race. The hurt settled into a dull throb. She stared at the canopy, and her mind, which never stopped, came back online with its usual industry, and began at once to catalog, to assess, to file the experience under the proper heading, and discovered with some alarm that no heading existed.

He moved away from her with care, onto his back, and found the cup of watered wine on the stand and gave it to her without being asked, and drank after her from the same side, which the nurses would have scolded them for. The fire had burned low. Somewhere in the palace a clock struck, one bell, two.

"Well," she said, because someone had to say something and he appeared to be waiting for her. "That is done."

"That is begun." He did not look at her when he said it, which she appreciated. "Are you hurt?"

"Some. Not beyond bearing. I will have the linen changed in the morning. The court will want its proofs; let them have them." She sat up, pulling the sheet with her, and was absurdly glad of the dark. "My lord. I have a question, and you will answer it plainly, because there has been enough plainness tonight to last the reign. Did they teach you that, in the camps? The asking. The stopping."

He was quiet a moment. "No one teaches it. Most men never learn it. I learned—" He stopped, and she felt him choose. "I learned young what it costs a person to have no door that can be shut. I decided, if I was ever given a door of my own, I would not behave like the men who taught me."

It was the second time in as many days that he had come close to the walled country of seven years ago without entering it. She let the silence stand, and then she did the thing she had not intended to do: she lay down again beside him, on her side, facing him, close enough that their breath mixed.

"The treaty says separate chambers are permitted after a season if either party wishes it."

"I know. I read it."

"I don't wish it. Not yet." She said it to the dark, to his collarbone rather than his eyes, which made it possible. "For one thing, the connecting door is absurd. For another, the court will read separate beds as a quarrel, and I will not give them the satisfaction. And for a third," she stopped, and made herself finish, "I find I do not dislike the arrangement as much as I intended to."

His laugh was low and close, almost soundless, and she felt rather than saw him smile. "A commendation of the highest order. Madame, I will endeavor never to rise above dislike."

"See that you don't." She closed her eyes. "Wake me at dawn. There is a letter from Sainterre I have to answer, and the war council, and I believe the guildsmen want another audience, and—"

"And tomorrow is tomorrow. Sleep."

He did not touch her again, except that his hand found hers under the coverlet and held it, loosely, the way one holds something one has been permitted to hold. Jeanne lay in the dark of the great bed, in the arms of the state she had married, and listened to his breathing slow toward sleep, and discovered that the ledger in her mind had gone quiet, an event so rare that she stayed awake a while simply to notice it. Then the warmth and the wine and the long day took her, and she slept, and did not dream, for the first time since the gate bell.
