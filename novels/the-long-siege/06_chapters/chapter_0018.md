# Chapter Eighteen — What the Chancellor Carried

They were back at the Haut-Tertre for the summer war council, the court returned to its stone walls and its bells, and Foulques collapsed in the middle of a sentence about grain contracts.

One moment he was standing at the round table with a dispatch in his hand, speaking in the dry clerk's voice they had all heard for twenty years, and the next the dispatch fluttered to the floor and he had gone down beside it, his knee striking the table leg, his face the grey of river clay. Brive reached him first, being a soldier and therefore used to men falling, and lifted him with the help of a guard, and the room became a confusion of shouted orders and running feet. Jeanne was on her knees beside him before she had decided to move, her hand at his wrist, finding the pulse there, thin and rapid and frightened.

"The physicians. Now." Her own voice surprised her, steady as a charter. "Clear the room. Marshal, the council adjourns; the contracts wait. Lisette, my own physician, and send to the cathedral for the bishop's man as well. I want two opinions before anyone bleeds him."

They carried the chancellor to his own chamber, the narrow, ordered room above the chancellery where he slept among his archives, and put him to bed, and the physicians came and bled him and examined him and used the grave, evasive language of their trade. The heart, they said. A seizure of the heart. He might live the week; he might live the year; he might be taken in the night. In the meantime, complete rest, no business, no agitation, no papers.

"No papers will kill him faster than any seizure," Jeanne said, but she said it outside the door.

He was awake by evening, weak and chalk-white, propped on pillows in the room that smelled of parchment and the dried herbs he strewed against fleas, and when she came in alone with the door closed and the guard outside, he looked at her with the eyes of a man who has been shown the exit and decided what to do with the interval.

"Madame. I was going to wait for the summer. You gave me until the spring, and the spring became the campaign, and I told myself there would be time. The heart has corrected my scheduling." His voice was thin but clear. "I will not die with the green case unopened. It is in the coffer at the foot of the bed. The key is on the chain at my neck. I would ask you to take it yourself."

She did not move at once. Seven years of her life were in that coffer, the shape of every wound she had built herself around, and there was a part of her that wanted to walk out of the room and leave the case shut forever. Then she thought of the girl in the solar, who had deserved better than silence, and she rose and took the key from the chain, her fingers steady in a way her heart was not, and unlocked the coffer.

The green case lay on top. Beneath it were folded documents in several hands, tied with the same ribbon, and a small portrait in an old style, and she lifted the whole weight of it and brought it to the table beside his bed, and sat, and said, "Start at the beginning. I want every word, Foulques. Whatever it costs either of us."

* * *

He told it like a man making a final deposition, in order, with dates, the way he had been trained.

Her father, he said, had known nothing of the feeling between his daughter and his ward until the sewing woman came to him with her report, but once he knew, every decision had been made in the space of a single night. The duke's position was precise: a fifteen-year-old heiress compromised, even by rumor, in a court full of envoys, could not marry the landless young Duke of Lorraine, whose duchy was bankrupt and whose wardship was the seal of a peace that was already failing. A love match with Thiébaut would give every vassal a pretext and France a weapon. The girl, moreover, had to be protected from the consequences of her own tongue, which had been heard saying she would give up the inheritance.

"The duke's first thought," Foulques said, "was to put the young lord on trial for the ravishment of the heir. I argued him from it. A trial would have made the story public and permanent, and the law would have required the worst. His second thought was convent walls for you and a writ of treason for him. I could not move him from the convent. I could move him on the method."

The method was the agreement in the green case. The young duke would sign a written renunciation of every claim, suit, and understanding with the heiress, and perform it before the full court in terms that made the attachment look like a boy's indulgence of a child's infatuation. He would leave Vauréal at once and never communicate with her again, on pain of the original charges being revived. In exchange: no trial, no convent, and the maid Lisette, who had carried the notes and stood to be flogged and branded and turned out without a character, would be spared. The document in the case was the renunciation in Thiébaut's own hand, and below it, in her father's marginal writing, the list of the threatened penalties and the names of those who held their safety by the signing.

"He had no choice that did not cost someone else," Foulques said. "I want that understood before you read the words, madame. He did not choose the great hall. He chose it over your convent and your maid's face. He was twenty years old. He signed at dawn, and he wept while he signed, and I have seen only two men weep in my life, and I remember both."

The words in the great hall, then, had been written for her father's ear, not hers. Every cruelty she had carried for seven years had been the price of her own safety and Lisette's, paid in a coin she had never been allowed to see. The boy who had not looked at her had not looked because looking would have broken him, and the man who had ridden away without turning had done it because turning was forbidden on pain of her ruin.

Jeanne sat with the document on her knees and the room very quiet, and she did not weep, because she had trained herself too well for weeping, but something behind her ribs shifted like a stone in a wall, and she put one hand flat against her chest as though to hold the structure in place.

"The night in the solar." Her voice came out level, which astonished her. "Nothing happened. You know that."

"I knew it then. The guard knew it. Your father did not care, because the question was never what happened. The question was what could be made to have happened. I wrote that sentence down in my notes, madame, the day after: _the question is not the truth but the story._ I have spent seven years trying to decide whether it is the definition of statecraft or the definition of hell."

"Why didn't he tell me? Afterwards. Years afterwards. When I was of age."

"Because the agreement forbade him communication on pain of reviving the charges, and because the duke, your father, watched the ports and the couriers for years; I know, because the watching came through my office. And because, I believe, he could not be certain the threats were dead. The convent order was actually drawn up. I have kept it; it is in the case. It was never canceled. It was merely filed. A man who knew that would not write the letter, even years later, that might send the woman he loved into a strict observance and her maid to the branding iron."

Jeanne closed her eyes. Behind them she saw not the great hall but the solar, the lightning, the cloak over both of them, the face of a boy who had meant every word. She had carried the wrong story for seven years, polished and complete and false, and the true story was worse and better at once, and she did not yet know what she was going to become on the other side of knowing it.

"Leave me with the papers," she said. "I will read them all. And Foulques — do not die. I forbid it. I have not decided your sentence, and I will not have you escaping it."

The ghost of his old smile crossed the grey face. "No, madame."

* * *

She took the green case to her own chamber and dismissed everyone but Lisette, and the maid, who had lived inside the edges of this story since she was a girl, sat by the window with her sewing and said nothing at all, which was the highest service in her possession.

The documents were careful and complete, the work of a chancellor who had spent seven years preparing to be judged. The renunciation in Thiébaut's hand, young and fierce, the signature driven into the parchment. Her father's marginal threats, in his own clerk's hand, the convent order with its blank date and its strict observance, the order concerning the maid with the words _flogging and branding_ spelled out in the administrative language of a kitchen inventory. Foulques's own contemporary notes, dated and ordered, including the line about truth and story. And at the bottom, the small portrait, painted in the Aubecourt years, of a fifteen-year-old girl and a twenty-year-old boy in the gallery, standing too close and too unaware of it, the artist having caught what the court had refused to see.

She read it all twice. Then she sat with the portrait in her lap for a long time, while the afternoon light moved across the floor and the palace went on with its indifferent business of bells and footsteps and the distant shouting of the drill yard.

The anger, when it came, was not the thing she had expected. She had imagined, if the truth ever proved him innocent, a great unlocking, tears and reconciliation and the easy closing of a wound. What arrived instead was a cold and complicated rage, and it was directed at three men in turn. Her father, who had built the machine. Foulques, who had oiled it. And Thiébaut, who had let her carry the false story for seven years and become the man he had become in the silence, without once giving her the chance to choose what to do with the truth. That last was unfair, and she knew it, and the knowledge did not make it smaller.

"Lisette." Her own voice sounded strange to her. "The branding. Did you know?"

"I knew they meant me harm if anyone talked. I didn't know the paper existed." The maid's sewing had stopped. "I always said he looked like a man on the wheel that morning, didn't I? I said it. You wouldn't hear it."

"I wouldn't."

"Because hearing it would have meant the story wasn't simple, and simple was all you had." Lisette came and sat on the stool beside her, the way she had when they were children. "Jeanne. What now?"

"Now I speak to him. And I do it tonight, because if I sleep on it I will construct a version that lets me avoid it, and I have spent seven years inside a constructed version. Send word. The solar, the old one, after vespers. He will remember it."

* * *

She walked the east wing before vespers, through the schoolroom and the long gallery, with the case under her arm and Lisette following at a respectful distance. The place had not changed; storage still, the cradle still in its corner, the broken falcon's perch. She stopped at the window where she had watched him ride away, the bend in the road by the willows where a boy had not turned back, and she saw the memory now with new eyes: not the coldness she had carried, but a guard's report waiting to be written, a convent order with a blank date, the discipline of a man who had been told that a single glance would be used against the people he was trying to save.

"The wall," she said, going to the window that overlooked the lower garden. "He caught me here. And my father caned him for it, in front of the guard. I thought then that he was being punished for me. I understand now that he was being trained. The lesson was never about the wall. The lesson was that his body and his honor were the coin, and he had better learn to spend them."

Lisette said, "He learned it well, didn't he. The great hall was the same lesson, bigger."

"The whole marriage is the same lesson. He keeps giving away what he wants so that other people can be safe. The light clauses, the patents, the shoulder, you, me." She pressed her palm to the cold glass. "And I have spent a year reading every concession as strategy. Some of it was strategy. He would say it was mostly strategy. But some of it was a man who was never once permitted to choose himself."

"Can you forgive that, my lady? Not him. The knowing. Can you bear finding out you were wrong?"

Jeanne was quiet a while. A groom crossed the garden below, whistling, with two dogs at his heels, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary. "I don't know yet. I know I've been angry at the wrong man for seven years, and anger is easier than this. This is a country without maps. But my father used to say that a ruler who cannot revise her map when the ground changes is a ruler who loses. The ground has changed. I will revise."

She turned away from the window. "Also, I am furious at being made to revise. I expect I will be for some time. He will have to tolerate it, since tolerating things appears to be his particular gift."

Lisette's mouth twitched. "Shall I tell him that when I take the message?"

"Tell him the solar, after vespers, and to bring his memory. He'll need all of it."

* * *

The old solar had been opened and cleaned, at her order, the week before, though she had not known why she gave the order. The tapestries were gone, but the chest beneath the window remained, and the view of the orchard, and the wall they had climbed. A fire had been lit, and two chairs placed before it, and she sat in one of them with the green case on her knee and waited, and at the appointed hour the door opened and Thiébaut came in alone, in plain clothes, with the face of a man who has known this summons was coming and has prepared for it like a soldier.

He saw the case. He stopped. Then he crossed to the fire and knelt, not to her, exactly, but before both of them, the old gesture of the tourney field, and said, "Ask me anything. There are no locks left."

"Then start with the night. Tell it to me as though I had never been there."

He told it. The storm, the candles, the conversation, the kiss, the sleeping under one cloak, the door at dawn, Foulques's face, the guards. Then the day that followed, the part she had never seen: her father's chamber, the charges, the convent order, the order against Lisette, the choice presented to a twenty-year-old with no army and no friends. The renunciation written and signed at dawn, the tears Foulques had mentioned. The words in the great hall, chosen, he said, to be so cruel that she would not follow him or try to write, because a girl who followed a disgraced ward walked into the same trap. The ride to the border with the guard who had orders to report every glance backward. The seven years of watching the couriers and never sending.

"Why the clause about never communicating? Why accept that? You could have told me once. One letter."

"Because the convent order was never canceled. I saw it, Jeanne. I held it. Your father's hand, his seal, the blank date they had only to fill. If I wrote and the letter was taken, you went into strict observance and Lisette lost her face. I was not prepared to buy my own peace at that price. And then," he stopped, and for the first time his control cracked, and she saw the cost of the seven years in the open, "then the years went by, and you were the Duchess of Vauréal, and I was the duke of a bankrupt rock, and there was never a moment when telling you did not risk the only thing I could still do for you, which was to stay out of your story. I thought if you ever needed the army, you would send for it. You did."

"For the army."

"For whatever you needed. I would have come for the army and I would have come for a clerk's post and I would have come to sweep your stables. The treaty was the only door your father's ghost had left open."

The room was very quiet. The fire made its small explosions. She looked at the man kneeling by her chair, the weathered face and the grey at the temple and the left arm he still favored, and behind him she saw the boy of twenty signing the parchment, and behind the boy the girl of fifteen whose whole understanding of love had been rebuilt into a fortress by a morning's work.

"I am not going to forgive you tonight," she said. "I want you to understand that. I may not get to forgiveness at all. You made a choice for me, Thiébaut. You and my father and Foulques, all the men who knew better, decided what I could bear and what I could know, and I have spent seven years being the person that decision made. I do not know how to unbecome her in an evening."

"I am not asking for forgiveness." He had not risen. "I am asking for the chance to be known. Whatever you decide, the treaty holds, the army holds, the war holds. I made you a promise that the marriage was not on trial, and I will keep my promises, all of them, even the ones you didn't ask for."

"Get up. Kneeling doesn't suit you, and I refuse to have this conversation at two different heights." He rose, and she stood with him, and the firelight was between them. "I have also decided this: we do not become the kind of marriage that buries the past and calls it wisdom. You will tell me the rest, over time, every year of it, and I will tell you mine, including the years I spent hating you. And if I ever catch you choosing what I can bear for my own good again, I will make the attainder of Hautperthuis look like a kindness. Is that understood?"

"Perfectly." The ghost of the not-smile, careful and hopeful. "May I ask what happens tonight?"

"Tonight you sleep in your own chamber, because I cannot think with you on the other side of a wall. And tomorrow we go to war, because Graçay is moving on Sainterre and I will not have my domestic arrangements losing me a city. The rest we will build, badly and slowly, in the intervals of the fighting. Go on."

He went to the door, and there he stopped with his hand on the latch. "Jeanne. One thing, from the night in the solar, since all the doors are open. I meant every word. The cottage, all of it. I still do."

Then he was gone, and she was alone with the fire and the green case and the strange, enormous work of revising her own life.

* * *

She did not sleep, and she did not want to. She worked through the night, which was the only medicine she trusted: the Sainterre dispatches, the supply contracts, the letters to the cities, the old machinery of rule. At dawn Foulques's physician came with the bulletin that his master had passed a quiet night and was asking, against all orders, for the duchess.

She found him propped on his pillows, thinner than ever, the case gone from its coffer and a look on his face that she recognized after a moment as peace, the peace of a man who has been caught and sentenced and found both preferable to the waiting.

"Your resignation of the seal," she said, sitting beside him, "is refused. I have it here." She produced the document he had sent at midnight and tore it once, twice, and put the pieces in his hand. "You will not die, and you will not escape. A man who can carry a secret for seven years can carry his penance for the rest of his life, which I intend to be long and extremely busy."

"Madame—"

"Be quiet. Here is the penance. First, you will keep the chancellery, because no one else knows where the bodies are buried, and I require that knowledge in my service. Second, you will write, in your own hand, the full account of the matter, with every name and every threat, and it will be sealed in the private archive until my death, after which it belongs to history. Third," she paused, choosing it, "you will personally see to the education, at the duchy's charge, of every orphan girl of the court and the lower town whose fathers die in this war. Latin, accounts, law. I want a generation of women whom no man can decide things for, because they will know more law than he does. Name the school for my mother."

The old man's eyes were wet, which she had never seen. "And the duke, madame? What is his sentence?"

"His sentence is the same as mine. We have to make a marriage work without the lie it began in. It is a harder one than yours." She rose. "Live, Foulques. That's an order."

At the door she looked back once, and he was already reaching for his pen, because work was the only confession he had ever been capable of, and she closed the door softly and went down into the courtyard where the army was mustering for the north and the new war and the long, slow, unfinishable business of learning to trust the one person who knew every weakness in her walls.
