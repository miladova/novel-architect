# Chapter Twelve — The Banker's Ledger

The campaign season ended as campaigns do, not with a decision but with mud. Through June and July the army took the war to the French in the old fashion of marches: raiding parties across the fords, the convoys harried, the two captured bridge-forts repaired and regarrisoned, Graçay's expensive army made to pay for every league of road it held. By August the Regent's treasury was feeling the war in the only place Paris felt anything, and the French pulled back to the forfeit fiefs to digest them, and by the first snows the field armies were in winter quarters and the war moved indoors, where Jeanne had always been strongest and where it was about to become more expensive.

The Haut-Tertre in December was a different palace from the one she had inherited in March. The corridors smelled of woodsmoke and drying wool; the chancellery had doubled its clerks and tripled its hours; the great hall did double duty as a storehouse, with bales of Sainterre cloth stacked against the walls as collateral Dolfin's factors had already inspected twice. A duchy at war, she discovered, was a household that never slept: dispatch riders at all hours, the treasury cart trundling between Aubecourt and Malemort under guard, the wounded from the frontier billeted in the lower town with their pensions and their missing limbs and their claims.

She and Thiébaut had settled into the strange, workable architecture of a marriage made in council. Days were separate sovereignties: she in the round chamber with Foulques and Colard, he in the map room with Brive and the Lorraine captains. Evenings were shared, the supper table a second council, the nights the negotiated country of the great bed. The court, denied quarrels, had begun to manufacture stories about the arrangement instead, and Lisette brought her the harvest of them with relish: they were said to be rapturously happy, coldly estranged, secretly pious, and in one imaginative version the parents already of a hidden son being nursed in a village.

"People will believe anything that saves them from thinking," Jeanne said, and went back to the tax rolls.

The tax rolls were the winter's true battlefield. The campaigning had cost, by Colard's final reckoning, seventy-eight thousand écus, of which Dolfin's loan had covered forty and the rest had been scraped from the customs, the war contributions, the sale of two ducal manors, and the melting of the late duke's ceremonial plate, which the goldsmiths had received with the faces of men at a funeral. Now the vassals had to be made to pay for the next season, and the vassals had other ideas.

* * *

The assembly of estates in the great hall was three days of practiced grievance. The lords wanted the war financed by the towns, the towns wanted it financed by the lords, the clergy wanted it financed by nobody and offered prayers instead, and Hautperthuis, smooth and mourning-clad and freshly arrived with his train, wanted a committee.

"A committee," Jeanne repeated, in the silence after his speech. "For the collection of the subsidy."

"To ensure, madame, that the burdens fall justly, and that the ancient customs of the lordships are respected." He smiled his warm, unreadable smile. "No man here refuses the duchess's needs. We seek only the means to meet them without crushing the tenantry, whose love is the true wealth of the state."

There was a murmur, the sound of men buying time. She let it run, then set down her own copy of the subsidy schedule.

"The tenantry's love is a fine thing, cousin. The tenantry is also currently being defended by soldiers whose pay is in arrears. Let me assist the committee before it is born. The crown's own tax practice, which I have had copied from the Paris records, sets the noble subsidy at a tenth of assessed rents in wartime. I ask a fifteenth. The towns, under their new charters, vote their own contributions at a twelfth, which is their affair. The clergy, who hold a fifth of the best land in the duchy, will pay a twentieth and like it, since I have the bishop's own admission that the alternative is a forced loan at interest, which he considers a sin. There will be no committee, because a committee is a machine for turning an emergency into an argument. The schedule stands. Payments in two installments, by Candlemas and by Easter. Colard has the books open at the door."

It was not quite the end of it; nothing ever was. Erquelines's nephew wanted exemptions for his uncle's marches and got instead a lecture about the imperial correspondence Lisette's people had intercepted, delivered in a voice sweet enough to curdle milk. Two old lords tried the gambit of weeping for the late duke and were given copies of his last campaign budget to weep over. Hautperthuis himself, when the hall had emptied, lingered to pay his compliments, and she watched the charm go to work one more time at close range.

"You have grown into your father's chair, madame. It is remarkable to see."

"I have grown into my own chair, cousin. My father's is in the chapel vault." She folded the schedule. "Your own installment is a large one. I trust the road repairs did not overburden the estate."

"Not at all. A labor of love." The smile did not waver. "Though I confess I look to the spring with some anxiety. The French are very strong. There are those who wonder whether even the Lorraine alliance can—"

"Those persons may bring their anxieties to the war council, where the Sire de Graçay's strength is discussed with maps rather than murmurs. Good day, cousin."

When he had gone, Foulques emerged from the window embrasure where he had been ostentatiously reading. "He'll delay the first installment."

"He'll delay it, and then pay it in clipped coin, and then ask for a receipt in full. Lisette?"

The maid materialized from behind a tapestry, which never failed to unsettle the clerks. "His steward met a French factor at the Heurtois fair, my lady. Two days' ride, in a priest's clothes. We have the inn and the witness. Also his leases: three border manors changed hands last month through a lawyer in Pont-d'Ortelle, and the new tenants are all men who used to be French garrison sergeants. My lady, he's planting soldiers on the marches as rent-payers."

"Keep watching. Nothing that would warn him. I want the whole pattern before I move." She turned to Foulques. "And Dolfin. He's asked for an audience; give him tomorrow, and give him the treasury's true books, not the pretty ones. A banker who catches you lying once prices every truth you tell him afterward."

* * *

Dolfin came in out of the December sleet with snow on his velvet and the smell of the counting house about him, and they did business over spiced wine in the treasury office with one chair and no witnesses but Colard.

"The war will cost you ninety thousand next season," he said, opening his own books. "I have the figures. Your subsidy will bring perhaps thirty-five, the customs twenty-eight if the river stays open, the Sainterre contribution twelve. That leaves fifteen, plus the interest on the existing forty, plus whatever the spring's emergency invents, and the spring always invents something. My house is prepared to lend a further thirty thousand."

"At what rate?"

"Twelve percent, given the collateral's performance. But my partners want new security. The Malemort customs are now lent against twice. They want the iron tithe of Grisay, and a seat on the Grisay board."

Jeanne set down her cup. "The iron tithe is the duchy's oldest revenue. If I give your house a hand in the forges, the forges answer to Venice within a decade."

"Then refuse, and finance the spring from your vassals' patriotism, which I have watched for three days and would not lend a florin against." Dolfin was not unkind; he was merely accurate. "Madame, hear the professional. You are winning the war. I mean that literally; my factors travel, and Graçay's suppliers are going unpaid while yours are paid in full. But wars of attrition are decided by the side whose borrowing holds out one season longer. The Regent borrows at eighteen in Paris and hates every minute of it. You borrow at twelve. Time is your asset, and time is paid for in my coin. Give me the iron tithe as a six-year security, reverting fully on repayment, with no board seat but an inspector's right of audit. I will talk my partners down from the board."

She looked at Colard, who gave the small nod of a man who had already arrived at the same arithmetic.

"Six years, the audit right, and a clause allowing early redemption at par." She wrote it herself on the spot. "And one thing more, Messer Dolfin. Your house knows every money movement in this country. I want the names of every lord in Vauréal who holds a balance outside the duchy, at Paris or with the imperial banks. Not the amounts. The names."

The banker's eyes flickered, the only sign of surprise he permitted himself. "That is not information my house sells."

"It is information your house gives to a borrower who is asking, in confidence, as a condition of the next thirty thousand. I will not use it in court. I will use it to know which of my vassals can afford a long war and which cannot. You may keep the amounts."

He considered her for a long moment, and then did a thing she had not seen before: he laughed, once, without bitterness. "The late duke kept ledgers of soldiers. You keep ledgers of souls. Very well. The names, in a sealed list, with the loan papers. Madame, I will say to you what I say to no client: do not lose. A default would oblige me to admire your successor, and I have grown used to admiring you."

* * *

Claire de Sombernon arrived three days before Christmas, with a cart of salvaged furniture, two women, and a story that had half the palace in tears before vespers.

Her late husband's manor in the Lorraine marches had been among the first places overrun in the spring's fighting; the French had put the steward to death and turned the house into a supply post, and she had spent six months in a kinswoman's damp castle before Thiébaut's letters won her safe conduct through the lines to the Vauréal court, where he had promised her the protection of the household until her lands could be recovered. She was twenty-seven, auburn-haired, quietly beautiful in the manner of women who have been told so often that they no longer need to think about it, and she knelt to Jeanne with a grace that made every other woman in the room look as though she were kneeling for the first time.

Jeanne raised her with the correct words and the correct smile and the cold, unwelcome sensation, somewhere under her ribs, of a cat entering a room full of birds.

The court noticed everything. It noticed that the new lady was given apartments near the duchess's own, that Thiébaut received her in the antechamber with a brief handclasp and a low conversation about her late husband's tenants, that Jean the Bastard greeted her like an old friend and the Abbé de Viray kissed her forehead like a niece. It noticed above all that she was perfectly, irritatingly admirable: she organized the palace's sewing circle into a bandage workshop, she played the lute without ostentation, she was polite to Foulques and kind to Lisette and ate without complaint the salt cod of fast days.

"She's good," Lisette admitted, darkly, on the second evening. "That's the problem. If she were a schemer I'd know what to do with her. She's just a widow with good manners and a manor the French are sleeping in."

"Then we shall have to be excellent to her, and die of it quietly." Jeanne examined her own face in the silver-backed glass with more care than usual. "Find out one thing. Not gossip. Fact. What there was, between her and the duke, before the marriage."

Lisette found it by the unromantic method of asking the Abbé de Viray's housekeeper over bread and ale, and brought it back entire and unadorned: after his return to Lorraine, in the bleak years, there had been a connection, discreet, brief, ending by mutual consent before the Vauréal treaty was ever signed. Claire de Sombernon had wanted a convent or a second marriage; the duke had wanted neither to be misunderstood; they had parted friends, and she had married within the year, a marriage he had attended as a witness.

"Over," Lisette said. "Decidedly, boringly over. He hasn't written her a personal letter since the wedding, only the safe-conducts, and I've seen them all because her woman talks."

"Thank you. That will be all."

The knowledge did not help as much as it should have, because the trouble was not the facts. The trouble was the sight of the woman across the hall, at home in a way Jeanne could not yet be, in the easy possession of years of Thiébaut's life that Jeanne had not shared: the poor duchy, the cold winters, the farrier and the shoulder and the long road between. She had the treaty and the bed and the name. Claire de Sombernon had the missing seven years, and there was no clause for that.

* * *

The Christmas feast was smaller than the wedding's, nine courses again but plainer, with the officers of the army and the household rather than the vassals, and the hall dressed in green boughs and the red and silver rather than cloth of gold. Jeanne had planned it as a statement of disciplined wartime economy, and the statement was proceeding well until the musicians struck up a dance and Thiébaut, with the unreadable politeness of a man walking onto a frozen moat, led Claire de Sombernon out for the first measure.

It was correct. It was, in fact, unavoidable: the highest-ranking widow of the court took the first dance with the host, and the hostess danced with the highest-ranking gentleman, which was the Bastard Jean, who promptly stepped on her hem and apologized with such comic sincerity that she laughed despite herself. The court watched all of it with the attention courts give to anything that might become a story. Jeanne danced the measure correctly, talked to Jean correctly, smiled across the hall at the correct intervals, and kept her face on so tight by the end of it that her jaw ached.

Thiébaut handed Claire back to her chair with a word that made her smile, and came down the hall toward the high table, and the smile he gave Jeanne was entirely without guilt, which was somehow the most provoking thing about it.

"You're angry," he said, low, taking his seat beside her.

"I am observing the feast."

"You observe the feast the way a cat observes a mouse hole. Jeanne. Whatever is being built in your head, stop building it. I danced with her because not dancing with her would have been the larger story, and I have known her since she was a girl in my wife's—" He stopped. "Since she was a girl at my court. There is nothing in that room that belongs to us except us."

"Don't." She kept her smile on for the hall. "Don't say the thing that makes me the unreasonable wife. I know the protocol. I wrote the protocol. I am perfectly capable of being reasonable in public. It is the private practice that is currently costing me."

"Then we'll practice in private. After the feast."

* * *

The fire in the bedchamber had been built high against the December cold, and the tapestries were drawn against the draughts, and for once she dismissed the women early and did not take out the dispatches. He came in smelling of snow and horses, shut the door, and stood against it with his arms folded, and they regarded each other across the firelit room like two negotiators who had finally run out of clerks.

"Ask me," he said.

"I don't need to ask. I know the facts. The connection after your return from Vauréal, brief, ended before the treaty, and her marriage which you witnessed. My information is good."

"Your information is excellent, and also beside the point, because the facts are not the thing." He crossed to the hearth and stood looking down into the flames. "Jeanne. I will tell you the whole of it, not because you asked but because I have decided there will be no locked rooms between us that I hold the key to. You may keep yours as long as you like. Mine are open."

He was quiet a moment, choosing it.

"I came home from your father's court at twenty with a duchy falling apart and a hole in my life where my future had been. I did not intend to touch anyone for years. Claire's husband was alive then, and there was nothing, only friendship. He died in the French raid of 'eighty-one, and she was left with a burned manor and a dower the lawyers were eating, and we were both people who had been refused something, and for six months, carefully, with all the proprieties, we were together. It was kind. It was never going to be anything but kind, and we both knew it. She wanted a life. I had no life to give anyone; I had a siege. We ended it cleanly, she married Sombernon the next year, and I stood as witness because I was glad for her. Since my wedding day I have not written her a word that the Abbé de Viray could not read aloud in church. That is the whole account. Do with it what you like."

Jeanne sat in the chair by the fire with her hands in her lap and made herself examine the jealousy honestly, the way she examined a ledger column. It was not distrust of him; the facts were the facts, and she believed them. It was the old wound, the thing that had happened in the great hall, the certainty lived at fifteen and never quite killed: that she could be given up, that another arrangement could always be made, that she was at bottom a clause in someone else's treaty.

"The problem is not Claire," she said at last. "The problem is that she had seven years of you that I don't get to inspect. No one does. I have the man at the end of them, and I have to take the finished article on trust, and I am not built for trust. I was built for accounts, and accounts are only good when you've seen every entry."

"I know." He came and knelt by her chair, the armorless, undefended gesture, and took her hand. "But consider what you're asking. You want evidence that I won't leave. The only evidence is the staying, and the staying takes years. There's no document. If there were, you'd be the first person I'd show it to. You'll have to watch me keep the door, day after day, until the watching becomes a habit. That's the whole security on offer. I'm sorry it isn't better."

It was so exactly the answer she would have given someone else that she had nowhere to put her anger. She looked at the top of his head, at the grey beginning at one temple that the campaigns had put there, and thought of the boy in the solar and the man kneeling by her fire and the long, uninspectable road between.

"One condition," she said. "No mercies I have to discover from the servants. Whatever there is, whatever comes, you tell me yourself, first, even when it's awkward. Especially when it's awkward. I can bear anything except being the last person in my own palace to know."

"Agreed. And your side of it?"

"My side of what?"

"Reciprocity. The locked rooms. You don't owe me the past; I've told you I won't ask. But the present, Jeanne. The things you decide alone in the dark because you've never had anyone to say them to. I want those. Not because I have a right. Because a government of two that only one of them talks to is just solitude with a witness."

She looked at him for a long time. The fire shifted. Somewhere in the palace a watchman called the hour.

"I'll try," she said. "That's the only promise I know how to make honestly."

"It's the only one worth having." He rose, and lifted her hand, and kissed the palm the way he had on the wedding night, gravely. "Now. The feast was excellent and the dance is over and I believe there's a bed that has heard more state secrets than the council chamber. Will the duchess consent to be escorted to it?"

"The duchess will. And Thiébaut," she stopped him at the bedcurtain, "thank you. For the open door. I know what it costs you."

"It costs less than the other thing." He drew the curtain. "I've tried both."

* * *

Claire asked for the audience herself, in a short, correctly worded note delivered by her own woman rather than through the palace channels, and Jeanne, who had been expecting either the note or its absence as a test of character, granted it at once.

They met in the solar of the queen's tower, a small room of women's things, tapestry frames and a virginal and a view over the frozen river, and Jeanne took Lisette but no other guard, and the other woman brought only her sewing.

"Madame." Claire did not kneel; she curtsied, the courtesy of one great lady to another, and sat only when invited. "I will be brief, because I have spent my life among courtiers and distrust the long speech. My presence here is an injury to you whether it should be or not, and I have been asking myself whether to go. I have decided not to, unless you send me, because a woman who flees in the middle of a war confirms every story it would be kinder to kill. But I owe you the direct statement, and I make it once: the duke and I were something, years ago, in another country, and it is over as completely as anything between two living people can be. He has never given me one word since his marriage that the whole court might not hear. I am here for my manor and my tenants, in the only court strong enough to get them back. That is the whole of my politics."

Jeanne had prepared several versions of this conversation and used none of them. "You're plain, madame."

"I'm twenty-seven and a widow. Plainness is one of the few luxuries left to me." Claire's hazel eyes, lighter than Thiébaut's, were direct. "I will also say, since I am being plain, that you need not like me. But you may use me. I know every lord within fifty miles of Nancy, their wives, their debts, and their mistresses, because that is the work a woman of my station does whether anyone asks her to or not. If I am to live in your household, let me earn my bread."

It was impossible to dislike her. Jeanne noted the fact with some irritation. "Then earn it now. What does the Lorraine camp truly think of the marriage? Not the official version."

A small, genuine smile. "That you are the most alarming piece of good fortune to befall Lorraine in a century. That the duke sleeps, which he did not before. That the army's pay arrives on time, which it never did. And that you and I would one day have exactly this conversation, on which there were wagers in my escort, which I have now won."

Despite herself, Jeanne laughed. "Tell your escort the duchess admires its imagination. Madame de Sombernon, you will remain, and you will have a place in the household, and we will be courteous in public and honest in private, because I have no use for anything else. One rule: no closed doors with my husband that I have to hear about from anyone but him."

"Agreed with all my heart. Closed doors are a young woman's mistake." Claire rose, curtsied again, and went out with her sewing, and Jeanne stayed by the window watching the ice on the river, and found that the cat in the room of birds had turned out to be another bird, and that she was not entirely sure what to do with the absence of an enemy.

"She's good," Lisette repeated, gloomily.

"She's useful. There's a difference, though I'm damned if I know which is more dangerous."

* * *

The Grisay dispatch came with the first hard frost, carried by a courier whose horse died at the lower gate. Brother Anselm's preaching had moved from the churches to the market squares and now to the guildhalls: the rich were damned, the clergy were wolves, the duke's wars were God's judgment on a wicked city, and the forges ought to be making plowshares rather than guns. Two of the ducal tax collectors had been driven out of the iron hills with their account books burned, and the city's aldermen, chosen under the new charter, were writing in some panic that they could not answer for the peace past Candlemas.

Jeanne read it aloud at the supper table, in the flat voice she used for bad news, and watched the table's faces. Brive wanted soldiers. Foulques wanted an ecclesiastical process. Dolfin, dining with them that evening, wanted the forges protected above all considerations, because the iron tithe was now his security.

Thiébaut said nothing until the others had spent themselves, then: "It isn't a revolt yet. It's a conversion. You don't put down a conversion with the provost. You go and argue."

"Argue with a man who preaches damnation?" Brive said.

"Damnation is an argument. It has premises and conclusions, and its premises can be shown to be expensive." He looked at Jeanne. "Take the charter, take the law, take a small guard, and take Lisette, because a city in a religious mood will trust a woman's household sooner than soldiers. I hold the army a day's march away. If he turns it into a fight, I'll come. But I think he'll talk. Preachers who want to fight don't write sermons; they write letters."

Jeanne folded the dispatch. "After Candlemas, then. Let him burn hot through the winter. The colder the weather, the thinner the crowds in the squares. And send word to the aldermen: the duchess is coming, with the charter in one hand and the law in the other, and she expects to be heard in her own city."

She wrote it into the ledger that night, beneath the loan and the subsidy and the names of the vassals Dolfin's sealed list had given her, and closed the book on the year fourteen hundred and eighty-four, which had begun with a corpse in a cart and ended with a marriage, an army, and the first fragile outline of a reign.
