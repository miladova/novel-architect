# Chapter Twenty-One — The Breaking of the Siege

The letter came through the lines in a wax tube at the third hour, carried by a shivering swimmer who had floated two miles down the Ortelle among the reeds, and Thiébaut read it in the pavilion with the whole war council pretending to study the maps. He read the military parts first, as she had known he would, and the last part second, and then he read the last part again, and his face went through a sequence of changes that Jean, who had been watching him for fifteen years, later described as the expression of a man who has simultaneously been given a duchy and told to defend it with his bare hands.

He set the page down and said, to the council, "The powder holds ten days. The breach is at the river wall. The city can open the water gate at a signal. And I am to be a father, which is not strictly a matter for the council, but you will all know by Sunday anyway."

The room's relief at the first three pieces of news came out as laughter at the fourth, and even Brive permitted himself a smile, and then they got down to the work, because ten days of powder was an hourglass, and the sand was already moving.

The plan took three nights to build and every favor the alliance had banked in a year. Dolfin's gold, advanced against the next quarter's customs, hired a regiment of German Landsknechts, twelve hundred pikes under a captain named von Eppeln, who marched in from the east with the careful neutrality of men paid well enough not to ask whose war it was. The feudal host, thinned and hardened by a season of raids, supplied the men-at-arms and the mounted archers. The Sainterre militia's brothers from Malemort brought a company of arquebus men, new and uncertain and proud. In all, perhaps five and a half thousand effectives against Graçay's eight, but eight thousand strung around a city in a long, thin ring, with their convoys burned and their pay in arrears and their commander's best officer, Marcenay, disgraced twice and hungry for a reputation.

"Graçay expects a relief attempt at the southern camp, where his guns are." Thiébaut put his finger on the map. "So the feint goes south. Brive, you take the lances and the banners, every standard we own, and you make a demonstration at dawn with trumpets and dust and the kind of noise an army makes when it intends to be noticed. He'll draw his reserves to you. The real column comes west, through the river woods the French think are impassable for guns, and we bring the sledge cannon from the Harchies, because the ground is the same and the gunners know the tracks. At the signal, the city opens the water gate, and Coppens's militia takes the French works from the inside while we hit the ring from the outside. The key is the timing. We have to be at the gate at the same hour they are."

"Von Eppeln's terms say his pikes don't assault works," Jean said.

"His pikes hold the field against the French cavalry, which is what pikes are for. The assault is ours and the city's. Pay him his double rate and tell him the double rate buys silence as well as steel." Thiébaut's finger moved to the river. "The signal. One rocket from the west at the first hour after midnight. Two if it's delayed. The swimmer goes back tonight with the date and the hour. After that, no more letters. We run silent until the morning."

Bride looked at the map for a long moment. "It works or it ends the war in a morning, with the field army dead in a marsh and the city without hope."

"Everything works or ends the war in a morning. That's the definition of a battle." Thiébaut rolled the map. "I've spent a year making this the battle they don't want. The French are tired, unpaid, and spread thin. We are paid, angry, and coming through a wood. I'll take those odds."

* * *

The swimmer went back with the tube and the date, and the army moved in four columns over two nights, leaving the campfires burning and the tents standing and a skeleton of drummers and trumpeters to keep the French scouts honest. Brive's feint went south in a cloud of dust and every banner in the train, and the reports that came back through the scouts were exactly what the plan required: Graçay had drawn his cavalry reserve and two thousand foot to the southern lines, and the western ring was held by the gascon infantry and the free companies, with Marcenay's men around the river works.

The night march through the river woods was the kind of thing soldiers tell stories about and never want to repeat. No trumpets, no fires, the guns dragged in silence with rope and oxhide over the soft ground, the men hand-passing the powder casks along the chain where the wheels would have betrayed them. Thiébaut walked at the head of the column with the scouts, the bad shoulder wrapped and aching in the damp, and Jean beside him with the pike wound healed into a permanent stiffness, and behind them five thousand men moved through the black trees with the muffled, patient sound of a tide.

They reached the edge of the woods at the third hour, and the siege ring lay below them in the dark: the French watch-fires in their long curve, the black bulk of the city beyond, the river bending like a question mark between them. The water gate was a darker patch on the wall where the reeds grew thick, and Thiébaut looked at it for a long moment and thought of a woman in a steel cap in a burning ward, and put the thought away, because a commander who takes his feelings into a battle is a man who gets his people killed.

"Rocket at the first hour," he said. "Pass the word. No one fires until the gate moves."

* * *

Inside the city, Jeanne had spent two days making the inside half of the plan work. The militia was sorted into the storming companies and the wall-holders; the women's crews were assigned to the water gate with the beams and rams; the remaining powder was husbanded for one hour's hard firing; the wounded from the garrison were given weapons they could hold from fixed positions, because every body would be needed. Coppens, who had not slept since the swimmer returned, moved through the cellars and the halls with his lists, and Verhoeven's press ran off the order of the morning in hundreds of copies, so that every captain held the same paper and there could be no confusion in the dark.

She saw to the one thing no one else would have thought of: the canal boats. Every boat in the city was gathered at the water gate, and the wounded of the coming fight were to go back through them the moment the lines broke, and the flour stores were loaded on the first boats to feed the outer army, which she had correctly guessed would arrive short of bread.

At the first hour after midnight the rocket went up from the western woods, a single red thread in the dark, and the city answered with one bell from the cloth hall tower, the lowest and deepest of the bells, struck once. The beams of the water gate came up, the rams went to work on the inner barrier, and the first companies of the Sainterre militia, two hundred strong, went out through the reed beds at the French siege works with their pikes lowered and their boots muffled in rags.

Thiébaut's first line went forward at the same moment, and the French woke to the battle on both sides of their own wall.

* * *

The fight for the siege lines lasted until the sun was up, and it was never one battle but a dozen small ones, each with its own ground and its own name that would be told in the taverns afterwards. The gascons, professional to the last, formed in the trenches and held the Landsknechts for an hour of push of pike, the two blocks grinding together in the dawn mud with the long weapons bristling like two hedgehogs in combat, until the arquebuses of the Malemort company began to tell on the gascon flank and the great block edged backward, step by step, the way a door gives under steady pressure.

At the river works, the free companies broke first. Marcenay's men, the survivors of Sainte-Florence and the church, had no stake in a siege line and no pay for one, and when the Sainterre militia came over their own parapet behind them and the Lorraine lances came through the reeds in front, they made the calculation of their kind and ran, many of them throwing their arms into the river. Marcenay himself did not run. Whatever else he was, he was no coward, and he rallied a knot of his sergeants around the French battery and kept the guns firing case shot into the press until the press reached him.

Rostaing reached him first.

The disgraced captain, who had spent six months as a common man-at-arms in the ranks at Mont d'Or and then in the field, carrying his own kit and answering to sergeants he had once outranked, came through the battery ditch at the head of the file with no armor above the waist but a dented breastplate and a face that had forgotten how to smile. The fight at the guns was close and wordless. Marcenay killed the first man and the second, and wounded Rostaing's sword arm, and Rostaing took the blade through his own forearm to close the distance, and killed the French captain against the wheel of his own bombard with a dagger through the gorget, the way a butcher kills a pig. He held the body there a moment, in front of both armies' survivors, and then he sat down in the mud beside it and bled, and did not rise until the stretcher-bearers came, because the leg wound he had not noticed would not carry him.

They told Jeanne later, in the cloth hall, that he asked only whether the law would consider his account paid, and she sent back word by the same messenger: _Tell him the duchess keeps the books, and the entry is marked in full._

* * *

De Key died by his own city, which was the only end the story allowed.

He had been in the French camp through the siege, the prophet in foreign clothes, and when the ring broke he ran for the eastern road with a small party of the men who had followed him from the first riot, and the road was held by the weavers' company of Sainterre, the guild whose hall he had once filled with his voice. They knew him in the dawn light as well as they knew their own hands, and they took him without a fight and hanged him from the framework of the French siege gallows before any officer could stop them, and the company's sergeant pinned a copy of his first broadsheet to his chest, so that he went into the next world carrying his own words. When the report reached Coppens, the old guildsman said only, "The city did that. Not the duchess. Remember it in the record."

Graçay did the professional thing. With his western ring gone, his cavalry pinned by Brive's feint turned real, and his guns taken in their emplacements, he did not spend the army of the Regent in a prideful last stand. He drew the southern brigades off in good order, burned what stores he could not carry, spiked the guns he had to leave, and was marching south by midmorning with his wounded and his pay chests and his honor, leaving the field, the siege works, and a great deal of his reputation in the mud outside Sainterre. The Lorraine horse harried his rear for two miles and then, per the standing order, came back rather than be drawn into a running fight on French terms.

The southern feint became a real battle halfway through the morning, which was the risk Brive had carried. Graçay, discovering at last that the dust and trumpets were a screen, sent his heavy cavalry around the camp to catch the allied flank, and the gendarmes came across the open ground in the old magnificent fashion, two hundred armored horsemen at the full trot with their lances down, the charge that had won a hundred fields. Von Eppeln's Landsknechts received it in the new fashion: a square of pikes, the front ranks kneeling with the butts braced in the mud, the steel hedge glittering in the sun, and the charge that had been the queen of battle spent itself on a formation that could not be made to run. Three times the gendarmes came, and three times the square held, and the mounted archers worked the flanks with their arrows, and by the third charge the French horses would no longer face the pikes, and the cavalry drifted off toward the marching column like water around a stone.

Bride, watching from the ridge with the reserve, said aloud to no one, "There goes the world my father knew," and then sent in the lances at the disorganized retreat, and did the new war the courtesy of taking its prisoners properly.

It was in that last phase that the old marshal found Rostaing's name coming up through the reports again, carried in on a stretcher with the French captain's dagger wound in his forearm and the gash in his leg, and Bride went himself to the dressing station and looked down at the man he had once expected to hang, and said, "You killed Marcenay."

"Yes, my lord."

"With a dagger, through the gorget, in a battery ditch."

"The sword was gone. It seemed the practical thing." Rostaing's face was grey with loss of blood, but the voice was steady. "I held the law against him, my lord. The duchess's law. She said the entry was marked in full. I would like to know if that means what I think."

"It means you're a captain again, if the surgeons keep you alive, and the commission will be backdated, because that's the kind of sentimental woman she is." Brive gruffed out a sound that might have been a laugh. "Don't make her regret the entry. I'm too old to watch another trial."

"Never, my lord."

"See that you don't." And the old marshal went back to his battle, and left the stretcher-bearers to carry the redeemed man down to the canal boats.

By noon the joined arms standard flew over the French camp, and the river gate stood open, and the first canal boats were carrying flour out and the wounded in, and the siege of Sainterre was over.

* * *

They met in the cloth hall, because it was the center of the city's story and because neither of them could have borne a private reunion with the day's work still undone. He came through the doors in his battered plate with the mud of the river woods on him and the stubble of the night march, and she came down the aisle of the great barn of a hall in the grey gown and the steel cap, with the lists in her hand, and the companies filing in behind them stopped to watch, because armies are incurably sentimental and know the value of what they see.

There was no speech. He crossed the floor and took both her hands, and looked at her face, the thinness and the smoke stains and the new lines, and then lower, at the barely visible change in her body that his hands recognized before his mind had finished the arithmetic, and he said the only thing he had planned on the march and rejected twice and decided anyway.

"You were in a church with a dagger. At four months."

"At three months, and only just. The city's women were with me, and the church was ours by the time I left it." She held his hands because the whole hall was watching and because she needed to. "Your letter said you would come when the ground gave. The ground gave."

"The ground was mined and flooded and paid for and voted on. I merely arrived." His thumb moved once across her knuckles, the only private thing he permitted himself. "Jeanne. The child. Is it well, truly?"

"Sister Margriet says so, and she has delivered four hundred babies and is not impressed by duchesses. It is well, and I am well, and you will be a father in the winter, and we will discuss your feelings at length after the count." She turned to the lists, because the lists were safe. "The city's dead first, then the army's, then the prisoners and the guns. Coppens has the city's book. Your clerks will have the rest."

The count took the rest of the day. The siege had cost Sainterre four hundred and eleven of its people, counting the flux; the relief had cost the field army two hundred and thirty; the French left near a thousand dead and wounded in the works and the roads, and the city took back its gates and its honor and four bombards with the royal arms on their bronze. The stories sorted themselves in the telling: Rostaing's debt paid in the battery ditch, the weavers and their gallows, the Grey Woman at the church, the drowned mine, the rockets and the reeds. Verhoeven's press, which had not stopped through the siege, set the first relief sheet that night, and by morning it was being read in the camp and the city and bundled for every road in the dominion.

* * *

Graçay's herald came at dusk under the parley flag, with the professional courtesies of a beaten man who intends to remain in his profession. The Sire de Graçay requested leave to bury his dead in the siege lines and to send his surgeons to the wounded he had been obliged to abandon, and he asked, in the careful language of chivalry, for the return of the body of Captain de Marcenay, whose family in Gascony would pay the customary sum.

"Say yes to the dead and the surgeons." Jeanne read the letter in the cloth hall with the aldermen and Thiébaut around her. "Say no to Marcenay's body. He hanged thirty-one militiamen in their charter uniforms and held a church full of women. Let the city decide his grave. Coppens?"

"The guilds would like him buried at the crossroads outside the Ghent gate, madame. Without a service. The families asked."

"Then write it as the city's answer, not mine, and tell the herald the French army's wounded will be treated by my surgeons at my cost, and the names sent under flag for exchange. One more paragraph. The Sire de Graçay's men fought, and that is an honorable trade. His free companies butchered, and that is not. I will treat with soldiers. I will ransom brigands only at the gallows. Make sure the letter is printed."

Thiébaut watched the herald ride out and said, when the doors were shut, "He'll show that letter in Paris. Every professional officer in France will read it."

"That's why it's printed. The Regent's army is held together by professionals who haven't been paid. I want them comparing their treatment with ours when the spring campaign comes up for debate. War is also a court of reputation, and I intend to win that one too."

The French dead were buried next day in the common ground beyond the siege works, with the priests of both sides and the city's bells, and the French surgeons worked in the hospital of Saint-Géry beside the convent women for a week, and more than one of them went home carrying a reputation of Vauréal that had nothing to do with its guns.

* * *

The private reunion came at last, late, in the chamber the aldermen had made ready in the cloth hall's upper floor, a room of stored bales with a bed made up in clean linen and a fire and a tub of hot water, and a guard at the stair foot with orders to kill any messenger short of a second invasion.

He shut the door, and the discipline of the day broke all at once, and he crossed the room and put his arms around her with a care that was almost comical, as though she were made of the new gunpowder, and she let him, and then she pulled his head down and kissed him with none of the morning's public restraint, and for a while there were no words at all, only the two of them holding on in the firelight like people come up from deep water.

"I read the letter eleven times." His voice was rough. "The march was the easiest part. I had something to do with the fear."

"I wrote it eleven times. The version I sent was the least alarming." She drew back enough to see him. "Thiébaut. The green case. I want to say this while I can say it plainly, before the rest of the war and the child and everything. I have been revising, as I promised. I am not finished, and I don't know that I ever will be. But I know what to do with the anger now. I keep it for the people who built the machine. I don't spend it on the boy who paid its price."

He closed his eyes. "That may be the nearest thing to absolution I will ever deserve."

"It isn't absolution. It's an audit. I've looked at the books and decided where the debts actually lie." The ghost of her own smile. "Also you are strictly forbidden from being noble about this. We have a government to run and a child to produce and a war to finish, and I require you in working order."

"Yes, madame."

"And one more thing. When this child is born, we are going to raise it differently. No wardships of convenience. No secrets kept for its own good. If there is a hard truth, it gets the truth, at an age when it can bear it, from us. I will not have another generation of this family built on locked rooms."

"On my oath." He kissed her forehead, the only place he seemed to trust himself to kiss. "Now, by your leave, I am going to sit in that tub and then fall asleep in that bed, and you are going to do the same, because the duchess who runs the world is currently swaying on her feet."

They slept twelve hours, fully at first across the coverlet and then, when the fire woke him in the small hours, properly under the linen, with his arm carefully arranged around the shape of the child, and outside the door the sentries paced their watch and the ruined city breathed and the first peacetime quiet in seven weeks settled over Sainterre like snow.

* * *

In the morning the work found them again, as work always does. Coppens came with the aldermen and the relief accounts. Brive came with the French prisoners and the question of exchanges. Dolfin's factor came with the terms of the emergency loan and a face of careful optimism, because Venetians can smell a victory before it is paid for. And the news sheet went out with the casualty lists and the charter text and the names, all the names, and the story rode every road: the city had held, the duchess had stayed, the alliance had broken the greatest siege the French crown could buy.

Jeanne sat at the cloth hall table with her hand on the growing ledger of the state and watched the morning sun come through the high glass windows onto the smoke-stained walls, and she thought: this is what survival costs, and it is worth every line of the invoice. Then she opened the next dispatch, because there was no other way to be a duchess, and went back to work.
