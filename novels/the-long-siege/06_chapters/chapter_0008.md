# Chapter Eight — Two Heads, One Pillow

The first problem of the marriage, Jeanne discovered, was not the bed. It was the breakfast.

For three weeks she had taken her first meal alone at the writing table, standing, with a clerk reading the night's dispatches between sips of hot milk. On the first morning of her married life she came from the bedchamber to find the antechamber occupied: Thiébaut already at the table with a map spread among the bread, the Abbé de Viray saying grace in a low voice, the Bastard Jean eating an entire cold capon with the concentration of a man laying siege, and two Lorraine pages she did not know taking up useful space near the window. A Vauréal page of hers stood against the opposite wall, holding her letters, wearing the face of a boy who has discovered foreigners in his kitchen.

"Good morning, madame." Thiébaut rose, and the room rose with him. "Viray thinks better before noon if fed, and Jean thinks better never, but eats regardless. There is bread with honey, and the milk is hot. Your clerk may read aloud; nothing in this room travels past that door."

She sat, because there was no useful alternative, and the clerk, after one look at her face, began the dispatches. It came to her halfway through the Sainterre letter that she was presiding over a council in her shift and mantle before she had broken her fast, and that the marriage had achieved in one night what the treaty's clauses had only described: two governments in one room, with one bread basket.

The household adapted faster than she did. Servants are the true political scientists of any court; within two days the antechamber table had acquired a permanent second inkstand, the kitchens had learned Lorraine preferences in cheese, and Lisette had established, by means Jeanne did not inquire into, an exact understanding of which Lorraine pages could be trusted with messages and which carried everything to the Bastard. The two retinues circled each other like strange dogs, sniffing for precedence, and found it where such things are always found: in the duchess's own arrangements. She kept her hours, her table, her seal. He kept his. They shared a roof, a bed, and the war, and the court, denied the spectacle of a quarrel, began the slow, suspicious work of accepting the new geography.

The map room gave it the quarrel it wanted on the fourth day.

* * *

She came down after the morning's petitions to find the muster lists unrolled on the long table and Thiébaut with Brive and Van Brock, reorganizing the feudal host. Not discussing. Reorganizing. New columns in a hand she did not recognize, the old Vauréal banners grouped into combined lances with Lorraine captains named beside them, the gun crews consolidated under a single master gunner, his own man, a one-eyed Lorrainer called Hautemer.

She read the top sheet twice. "What is this?"

"The host cannot take the field in its present shape." He did not look up. "Your vassals arrive with their little retinues and their little jealousies, and the French roll over them as they did at the Roches-Noires. I'm breaking the retinues into squadrons of mixed companies, Vauréal and Lorraine together, under captains I can answer for. The guns go under one hand. The pikes, when the Swiss arrive, form the center, not the wings."

"The pikes are a loan contract, not a certainty. And you have named the captains."

"Provisionally."

"Provisionally, in writing, on my table, without my seal." She heard the level in her own voice, the dangerous level. "My lord. In this country, captains are patents, and patents are mine. You signed that clause yourself."

That got his attention. Brive, to his credit, began an immediate and absorbing examination of the window. Van Brock found something wrong with his boot.

"The clause concerns offices and garrison commands." Thiébaut turned to face her. "Field appointments in an active campaign are the work of the commander, and the treaty gives me field command. I am not seizing your castles. I am making your army capable of not dying."

"Your treaty gives you joint field command under my war council. There has been no war council. There has been a man in my map room with my lists."

"Because if I wait for a council every time a company needs a new sergeant, the French will be baptizing their children in the cathedral." His voice had not risen; it never did, which made the anger in it more audible. "Jeanne, be angry about the manner if you like, and I will apologize for the manner. Do not be angry about the thing itself. The Roches-Noires army died of household politics. Yours is the same army with a new grief and fewer men."

It was true, and she hated that it was true, and the room knew it was true, which made it worse. She looked at Brive, and the old marshal gave her the smallest possible nod, a soldier's confirmation, and then looked back at the window with the dedication of a man at prayer.

"Very well." She folded the top sheet with precise hands. "The reorganizations stand provisionally, until the war council confirms them. Every captain's name goes onto a patent in my chancellery by Friday, and any man I strike, you replace without argument. In future, my lord, you will bring your reorganizations to me before they reach the table, and I will bring my objections to you before they reach the room. We may argue in private until the candles die. In front of the captains, we have one voice. Is that understood?"

The Lorrainers in the room went still, because they were waiting to see whether a duke of Lorraine could be spoken to this way and remain a duke. Thiébaut held her eyes for a long moment, and then something in his face gave way, not to anger but to the recognition of an equal.

"Understood, madame. And the patents by Friday."

"One more thing. Hautemer is a good man, and Brive tells me his gunnery is the best in either country. But the master gunner of Vauréal answers to me through the master of ordnance. He keeps the guns. Van Brock keeps the seal on the powder store. Two men, one battery. No single hand on all of it. That is not distrust. That is how my father kept his gunners from becoming kings."

"Your father," Thiébaut said, and the ghost of a smile crossed his face, "would have made a terrifying Venetian."

"He made a Vauréal. It amounts to the same thing."

The room exhaled and went back to work, and Brive, passing her at the door, murmured so that only she heard, "Well done, my lady. He'll respect it. The question was always whether you'd make him."

* * *

The charter framework was Foulques's draft and her architecture, and it took four nights to build.

The problem was arithmetic made political: she needed war taxes from the three northern cities, and the cities wanted self-government, and neither side could afford to look as though it was buying or being bought. The framework, as it emerged from her table, offered each city a sealed charter of limited liberties — elected aldermen from a franchise of guild masters, a city court for trade and labor cases, fixed tolls for a term of six years, and a licensed militia under city officers who held their commissions from the duchess. In exchange, the cities voted a war contribution in coin and kind, kept the peace within their walls, and admitted the duchess's judges for crown cases. It was less than de Key's faction demanded and more than any duke of Vauréal had ever granted, and it was designed, with cold care, to split the cities' moderates from their radicals.

"Coppens can sell this in Sainterre," Foulques said, turning the draft. "De Key will call it a cage with gilded bars."

"De Key will call anything short of his own republic a cage. The question is whether four thousand weavers prefer a cage to a siege." Jeanne dipped her pen. "Make the toll term six years rather than ten. A privilege that expires is a privilege that keeps the aldermen courteous. And add the clause on the press: no printed ordinance without the city court's mark. The printing shop is where de Key breeds his sermons."

Thiébaut, who sat at the other end of the same table going through supply contracts, looked up. "You license a printing press in '84 and your grandchildren will still be explaining it."

"My grandchildren can explain it from a throne if I keep the country. Unlicensed printing in a city in revolt is not a liberty. It is a weapon. The Sainterre press prints what the aldermen approve, and the aldermen are my charter. I am not censuring truth. I am licensing order, and the difference will matter to the people who pay for both."

"It will be called fear."

"It will be called government. Fear is the raw material; government is what you build with it." She sanded the page. "Your own Viray would say the same in Latin."

"Viray says everything in Latin. It saves him from being contradicted by people without education." Thiébaut set down his pen. "Jeanne. One substantive thing. The licensed militia. If the cities keep their own armed men, you are creating the very force that can shut your gates against you."

"Which is why the officers hold my commission and the powder is stored in the ducal arsenal under Van Brock's seal. A militia with officers and no powder is a parade. A parade that votes me taxes is a useful parade." She pushed the draft toward Foulques. "Seal the framework as a general proclamation, and send copies under safe conduct to all three cities before we march. Let de Key explain to his weavers why they should refuse the charter and keep the war."

The chancellor took it, and she saw again that small, considering look he gave her whenever the talk edged near the uses of power, as though he were comparing her to some older copy of the same document. He said only, "It will be in the mail by morning, madame."

* * *

The nights were the strange part, and the part the court could not see, which made them the most important.

They kept her chamber, the larger one, and the connecting door stood open now and was never locked, though the key remained in her keeping. The bedding ceremony's formalities gave way to an ordinary negotiated by exhaustion: who took which side of the bed, whose boots went where, the rule that dispatches did not enter the bed but might be read at its foot. He slept on the left because of the shoulder, and she kept the right because the window was hers, and within a week these were laws.

It was not love, whatever the court assumed. It was something more useful and more complicated: the nightly practice of a shared silence. They would lie in the dark after the candles were out, each still carrying the day, and talk in low voices about the things that could not be said in rooms full of clerks. He told her how Lorraine actually worked, which was nothing like the romances: a patchwork of bishoprics and lordships held together by roads and debts, where the duke's writ ran as far as he could ride and no farther. She told him the true accounts of Vauréal, the hidden borrowings, the vassals whose rents were fiction, the towns whose loyalty had a price she had not yet been quoted. In the dark, without faces, it was almost easy.

The physical life between them settled into its own cautious grammar. Some nights he simply held her hand the way he had on the first night, and they slept. Some nights, when the day had gone well or badly enough to require it, there was more, and she learned the geography of him the way she learned the roads of her own country, by use; the bad shoulder, the scar at the ribs from a French lance, the way he went still and watchful if a hand moved too fast, a thing she did not understand and did not press. He kept his rule, always: she had only to say the word. She never said it, though she tested once, early, turning her head away at a certain touch, and felt him stop at once without question, and the ease with which he stopped made the trust possible in a way no insistence could have.

On the ninth night she asked him the question she had been holding since the wedding. "Why did you really sign the light clauses? Don't give me the road again. Every man in Europe wants the regency of a rich duchy. You refuse it in writing, in advance, with your own hand."

The dark considered. "Because I saw what the wanting did to a man. I watched your father want everything in every room, and it made him great and it made him alone, and it got him killed at the Roches-Noires because there was no one he trusted enough to tell him the charge was folly." A pause. "And because a man who takes a woman's country from her has to live in it with her. I have lived in houses where I was not wanted. I will not make one of my own house."

She lay with that for a while. "You keep answering me."

"I keep being asked. Most people don't ask. They decide what I am and then listen for it."

"Most people have never been renounced in their own great hall." It came out harder than she meant.

The bed went still. "No," he said. "They have not." And then, carefully, as though crossing ice: "Jeanne. If you ever decide you can hear the answer, I will give you the whole of that morning. Not a version. The whole. But I won't force a door you've barred, and I won't tell it to make myself lighter at your expense. The truth of that day has other people's lives in it, and I don't own all of them."

"Other people." Her voice was steady. "Foulques, for instance."

He did not answer at once. "Foulques for one," he said, and left it there.

She filed it, and did not pursue it, and after a while his breathing slowed, and she lay awake listening to it, holding in her two hands the newest and most unwelcome fact of her reign: she was beginning, against every intention, to trust the man she had married.

* * *

The dispatch from Mont d'Or reached them on the eleventh day, and it was the commandant's own hand, written tight and small to fit the page. French engineers had pushed forward from Saint-Vaast; a second column was on the kingdom road; the fortress could be invested by the end of the month unless the field army showed itself. The commandant added, in a postscript, that he now had sixty-one men, the reinforcements having arrived, and that the duchess's name was still said at every watch.

The war council met that night and voted the march within the hour. The host would go north first, per the plan already proven: the Lorraine companies and the Vauréal squadrons to Sainterre, to settle the revolt while the French were still digesting the south, then turn south along the river roads before Graçay could complete his investment. Mont d'Or got another convoy, more gunners, and a promise in the duchess's own hand.

On the morning of departure Jeanne rode out of the Haut-Tertre in steel and velvet, with her husband fifty paces away at the head of his lances and the joined arms above them on a dozen banners, and the lower town cheered itself hoarse because armies marching away are always more popular than armies returning. She did not look back at the gatehouse window from which she had watched him arrive. She looked north, where the road ran between the spring fields toward the richest, angriest city in her inheritance.

They were still strangers in everything that mattered. They were already, in everything that counted for the next months, a government. The distinction would have to hold.

* * *

The proclamation went out under the great seal in three fair copies, one for each of the northern cities, and Foulques brought it to her for the final impression with his own hand, which was unusual; the chancellor of Vauréal did not play clerk.

"Foulques. Sit."

He sat, with the careful patience of a man who has heard that tone before.

"You have been strange since the Lorraine treaty came to the table. Not opposed. Strange. I have asked you once and you gave me a locked door. I am asking again, as the person who carries every consequence of whatever is behind it. Is there any agreement, debt, or written thing between my late father and the Duke of Lorraine that is not in the chancellery files? Any document?"

The silence lasted long enough for the taper to settle in its holder. He did not look away from her; whatever his faults, he had never flinched from a direct question. "There is a document, madame. One. It is in my own keeping, not the chancellery, because your father ordered it so, and because it concerns the honor of persons still living, including your own. I have carried it for seven years and I have not shown it to a soul. I will not show it now, against the word I gave to a dying man's office, unless and until the duke himself releases me, or you can tell me a necessity greater than the harm of its coming out."

"Is it a thing that damages the state?"

"It is a thing that damages persons. The state, I think, would survive it. I am less certain of the marriage."

"Then consider this your notice." Her voice held. "I will have the truth of it before the year is out, one way or another. I do not pay a chancellor to keep locked rooms in my own house. You may go."

He rose, bowed, and went with the document case under his arm, and she watched the door close behind him with the cold, strange feeling of a hunter who has found a track at last. Lisette, emerging from the antechamber where she had been officially mending linen, said without being asked, "I've had his clerk talking. The green case never leaves his room. He says his prayers twice as long as any canon, and he drinks a cup of wine alone every night before he sleeps. Men with easy documents don't drink alone."

"Noted. Keep watching, but nothing stolen. I want it given, not taken."

* * *

Oriabel took command of the baggage train as though it were a duchy of her own, which in the arithmetic of campaigns it nearly was: forty carts of munitions, tents, the portable chancellery with its seal chests, the hospital tents and the surgeon's instruments, grain and salt and the iron-bound chests of the soldiers' pay, each cart numbered and listed in triplicate. Jeanne found her on the quay arguing with a carter about axle grease, her widow's black replaced by a practical grey gown and boots.

"The carter believes grease is a luxury," Oriabel said. "The carter will believe otherwise at the first flooded ford. Cousin, tell your household that a duchess on campaign owns three changes of linen and no jewelry beyond what she wears, because anything else becomes a story told by whoever steals it."

"I own two changes. Lisette packed the third anyway."

"Then Lisette is wiser than her mistress, as maids have always been." Oriabel lowered her voice. "And the duke's people? How are they to be managed on the road? Two households in one camp is a quarrel with wheels."

"His household keeps its own fires and its own accounts, and shares the chancellery tent and the watchword. Any fight between the servants comes to me and to him together the same evening, and the first servant who makes a faction is sent home without references. Tell the women. He'll tell the men."

"You've discussed it."

"We discuss everything now. It is the principal advantage of the arrangement, and the principal danger." Jeanne watched the carts roll down to the quay one by one, the seals of the duchy on their canvas covers. "Oriabel. Did my mother ever say anything to you? About the early years with my father. About what a marriage is, when it begins as paper."

Her cousin was quiet a moment, watching the river. "She said that paper burns slow. She said you don't light it with speeches; you feed it with the ordinary days, until one morning you notice it's the only fire in the room that's never gone out. She also said your father was the most difficult man she ever loved, and that she would not have traded him for an easier one. Make of that what you will."

Before the march, the review. She had ordered the whole host assembled on the muster field, and Brive had grumbled that reviews wasted a day and frightened no one, and she had answered that reviews were not for the French, and ridden out anyway.

It was a different field from the muddy emptiness she had looked over six weeks before. Three thousand men now, in the rough shape of an army: the household men-at-arms gleaming in the old plate her father had collected, the Lorraine lances in their darker, better-kept harness, the foot in leather jacks with bills and the new pikes, the guns on their new low carriages behind the patient oxen. Small, beside the armies the French king could put in the field. Real, beside the eleven riders who had come home from the Roches-Noires.

She rode the lines slowly on the grey mare, with Thiébaut a horse's length behind her, and let the army see her. This, too, was government. A lord's face at the muster was a soldier's pay in a month when the pay was late; she had learned it from her father, who had understood spectacle even when he failed at everything else. She stopped at the Vauréal companies and spoke to the survivors of the Roches-Noires by name, because she had made Brive give her the lists and learned them the night before, and she saw what it did to a man to have his duchess remember his village and his wound. At the Lorraine companies she let Thiébaut do the speaking, and watched the men's faces soften as his horse passed, and understood the other lesson: an army that loves its captain is an army that must be loved through him.

The last stop was the guns. Van Brock and Hautemer stood together by the biggest bombard, the one called _Marguerite_ after a long-dead duchess, a bronze throat big enough for a man's fist, and the two gunners, Fleming and Lorrainer, were clearly deep in an argument that had survived three languages.

"My lady." Van Brock touched his brow. "She'll throw a stone two hundred paces and crack a wall that took ten years to build. The question is whether she can be moved fast enough to be useful."

"She can," Hautemer said. "On the new carriages. We proved it on the road from Nancy; my master will tell you."

Thiébaut nodded. "A gun that arrives is worth ten that sit in fortresses. Madame, when we reach Sainterre, let me put the battery where the guilds can see it. Not to fire. To be read. Cities negotiate better when they've seen the handwriting."

Jeanne looked at the great bronze tube, at the oxen, at the gunners with their powder-stained hands and their professional pride. "Do it. And keep _Marguerite_ closest to the road. A bombard with a woman's name at the head of a duchess's army is a thing the ballad-sellers will work for us for nothing."

The men laughed, even Brive, and the laugh ran down the nearby ranks, and she turned the grey mare and cantered back to the standard with the sound of it following her, and knew that for today at least, the review had bought what she came for.

That night, the last in the Haut-Tertre before the campaign, she could not sleep, and went down to the empty council chamber with a taper, and found the round table still covered with the maps and lists of the day's work. Her father's chair stood empty at its head. She stood behind it for a while, hands on its worn arms, looking at the painted arms on the wall and the dark windows beyond.

Footsteps. She turned, knife hand already at her belt, and it was Thiébaut in a shirt and hose, his own taper in his hand, the bad shoulder held stiffly.

"I saw the light," he said. "Couldn't sleep either?"

"The chair. I keep thinking he should be sitting in it."

"You're sitting in it. That's the answer he would have given, if he'd been capable of giving comfort." He came to stand beside her, not touching. "Jeanne. Whatever happens at Sainterre, one thing. The city is yours, not mine. I'll hold the line and the guns and the soldiers. The talking is yours, all of it. If the guilds see a Lorrainer dictating terms, the charter is worth nothing and we'll be back here in a year with a siege."

"I know. I had already written that into my own instructions."

"Good." A pause. "Then we are thinking the same thing, which is either excellent generalship or the beginning of a disaster."

She almost smiled. "With us, my lord, I expect it will prove to be both."
